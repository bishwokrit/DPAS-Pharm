## Slide 5: ACE Inhibitors
- [heading] FIRST-LINE IN CKD
- [heading] ACE Inhibitors
- **Mechanism: block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, reducing both vasoconstriction and aldosterone release (Figure 8).**
- Captopril, lisinopril, enalapril — "-pril" suffix across the class.
- **Adverse effects: dry cough (bradykinin accumulation — the single most common reason for switching to an ARB), angioedema (rare but potentially life-threatening), and hyperkalemia.**
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy — fetal renal toxicity; avoid in women who are or may become pregnant.**
- An initial rise in serum creatinine of up to ~30% after starting therapy is expected and acceptable — reflects reduced intraglomerular pressure, not drug-induced kidney injury; a larger rise warrants evaluation for renal artery stenosis.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: The "up to 30% creatinine rise is expected" point is one students frequently get wrong on exams and in practice — many will want to stop the ACE inhibitor at the first sign of a rising creatinine. Be explicit that this is a predictable hemodynamic effect, not toxicity.

Instructor script:

## Slide 7: ARBs and Direct Renin Inhibitors
- [heading] RELATED CLASSES
- [heading] ARBs and Direct Renin Inhibitors
- [heading] Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs)
- [heading] Direct Renin Inhibitors

## Slide 8: RAAS Blockers in Chronic Kidney Disease
- [heading] THE RENOPROTECTIVE MECHANISM
- [heading] RAAS Blockers in Chronic Kidney Disease
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs are first-line antihypertensive therapy in CKD, especially with proteinuria — not simply because they lower blood pressure.**
- Mechanism: by reducing angiotensin II, these drugs preferentially dilate the efferent (outflow) arteriole of the glomerulus, lowering intraglomerular pressure and reducing proteinuria — a direct, structural renoprotective effect beyond systemic BP control.
- This is the same efferent-arteriole effect that explains the expected creatinine rise on the previous slide — the mechanism and the "side effect" are two faces of the same action.
- **Monitor potassium and renal function within 1–2 weeks of starting or up-titrating therapy, and periodically thereafter.**
- **Hold or use with caution during acute illness causing volume depletion ("sick day rules") — combined with reduced renal perfusion, RAAS blockade can precipitate acute kidney injury.**

Instructor script:

## Slide 10: Interpreting a Creatinine Rise After Starting Lisinopril
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] Interpreting a Creatinine Rise After Starting Lisinopril
- A patient with hypertension and diabetic nephropathy is started on lisinopril. Two weeks later, a repeat basic metabolic panel shows serum creatinine has risen from 1.1 to 1.4 mg/dL.
- **Ask yourself:** **Is this an indication to stop the ACE inhibitor?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **A rise of roughly 27% is within the expected range for ACE inhibitor initiation, reflecting reduced intraglomerular pressure from efferent arteriolar dilation — not drug toxicity. Therapy should generally continue with ongoing monitoring; a rise beyond approximately 30%, or a much sharper increase, should prompt evaluation for renal artery stenosis or volume depletion.**

## Slide 15: Resistant Hypertension in CKD
- [heading] A COMMON COMPLICATION
- [heading] Resistant Hypertension in CKD
- **Resistant hypertension (BP above goal despite three or more antihypertensive classes, including a diuretic) is common in CKD, often driven by volume overload.**
- First step: confirm diuretic therapy is adequate — a thiazide may simply no longer be effective at the patient's current GFR (Session 1's loop-vs-thiazide cutoff).
- Switching from a thiazide to a loop diuretic, or increasing loop diuretic dosing, is often the most effective single change in advanced CKD.
- **Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone) can be added for resistant hypertension, but require close potassium and renal-function monitoring, especially on top of an ACE inhibitor or ARB.**

## Slide 19: Nitrates
- [heading] PRELOAD REDUCTION
- [heading] Nitrates
- **Mechanism: release nitric oxide, activating guanylate cyclase and increasing cGMP, producing smooth muscle relaxation — predominantly venodilation at usual doses, reducing preload and myocardial oxygen demand.**
- Nitroglycerin (sublingual for acute angina; IV/patch for sustained use), isosorbide mononitrate/dinitrate (oral, longer-acting).
- **Adverse effects: headache (vasodilation), reflex tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension.**
- Tolerance develops with continuous exposure — a nitrate-free interval (typically overnight) is required to maintain efficacy with long-acting formulations.
- **Absolute contraindication: concurrent use with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil and similar) — the combination can cause life-threatening hypotension.**
- **23**

> Speaker notes: The PDE5-inhibitor interaction is one of the highest-stakes medication-safety points in this whole unit — always worth a specific, memorable callout since it is a real-world, repeatedly tested safety question.

## Slide 27: Recognizing the Triple Whammy
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] Recognizing the Triple Whammy
- An older adult with hypertension (on lisinopril and hydrochlorothiazide) develops a knee injury and starts taking an over-the-counter NSAID regularly for pain. One week later, they present with lethargy and are found to have an acute rise in creatinine.
- **Ask yourself:** **What is the most likely explanation, and how could this have been prevented?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **This is a classic "triple whammy" presentation (Figure 16) — the NSAID, ACE inhibitor, and diuretic each independently reduced glomerular filtration pressure, and together precipitated acute kidney injury. Prevention requires counseling patients on RAAS blockers and diuretics to avoid regular NSAID use and to seek guidance before starting one, even an over-the-counter product.**

## Slide 35: Lecture Recall Quiz — Answer Key
- [heading] ANSWER KEY
- [heading] Lecture Recall Quiz — Answer Key
- **1.  B —** **Loop diuretics block NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, collapsing the lumen-positive potential that normally drives paracellular Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ reabsorption, so calcium excretion rises; thiazides act at the distal tubule and, through a separate mechanism, increase distal calcium reabsorption instead.**
- **2.  B —** **Thiazide diuretics lose most of their natriuretic efficacy once GFR falls below roughly 30 mL/min. Loop diuretics act upstream at the thick ascending limb and remain effective in advanced CKD.**
- **3.  B —** **A creatinine rise of up to approximately 30% after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB reflects reduced intraglomerular pressure from efferent arteriolar dilation — an expected hemodynamic effect, not toxicity — so therapy should generally continue with monitoring.**
- **4.  A —** **ACE inhibitors and ARBs preferentially dilate the efferent arteriole, lowering intraglomerular pressure and reducing proteinuria — a structural renoprotective effect beyond systemic blood pressure control, which is why they are preferred in proteinuric CKD specifically.**
- **5.  A —** **This is the "triple whammy": the NSAID constricts the afferent arteriole, the ACE inhibitor dilates the efferent arteriole, and the diuretic causes volume depletion — three independent reductions in glomerular filtration pressure that combine additively to synergistically, precipitating AKI.**
- **6.  B —** **Calcium channel blockers treat hypertension via arteriolar dilation (reducing afterload/systemic vascular resistance) and treat angina via the same arteriolar-dilation mechanism (reducing afterload and myocardial oxygen demand) — the clearest example of one drug class, one mechanism, two indications in this unit.**
