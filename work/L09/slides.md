## Slide 5: Renal Drug Clearance and Dosing
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 3
- [heading] Renal Drug Clearance and Dosing
- **The kidney is a major route of elimination for many drugs and active metabolites — renal function directly determines how long a drug stays in the body.**
- Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and creatinine clearance (CrCl) are the standard measures used to estimate renal drug-elimination capacity.
- As GFR falls, renally cleared drugs accumulate — dosing must be adjusted by reducing the dose, extending the interval, or both.
- Examples of drugs requiring renal dose adjustment: most antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin, many beta-lactams), digoxin, many direct oral anticoagulants, metformin, and the diuretics covered later in this session.
- **Renal impairment also raises drug-interaction risk — drugs that are individually safe can become dangerous in combination when renal clearance is already reduced (a theme that returns explicitly in Session 2's "triple whammy" discussion).**

## Slide 8: Dosing Adjustment in Renal Impairment
- [heading] APPLYING THE PRINCIPLE
- [heading] Dosing Adjustment in Renal Impairment
- [heading] What Changes
- [heading] Practical Considerations
- Reduce the dose, extend the dosing interval, or both — the specific approach depends on the drug
- Loading doses are often unchanged; maintenance doses are what get adjusted
- Active or toxic metabolites that are renally cleared matter just as much as the parent drug
- Estimate renal function (CrCl or eGFR) before dosing any renally cleared drug, especially in older adults
- Reassess as renal function changes — acute illness, dehydration, or new nephrotoxic drugs can drop GFR quickly
- When in doubt, consult dosing references or clinical pharmacy — this is not a "memorize every number" exercise

## Slide 10: Loop Diuretics
- [heading] THE MOST POTENT CLASS
- [heading] Loop Diuretics
- **Mechanism: block the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb — the site responsible for ~25% of filtered sodium reabsorption.**
- Furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide — used for edema (heart failure, cirrhosis, renal disease) and, at times, hypertension when a thiazide alone is insufficient (e.g., significant renal impairment).
- **Adverse effects: hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, ototoxicity (especially with rapid IV administration or high doses), and hyperuricemia.**
- Furosemide carries a sulfonamide-related structure — historical cross-reactivity concern with sulfa allergy, though the clinical risk is now considered low; still worth a careful allergy history.
- Loop diuretics increase calcium excretion — useful in hypercalcemia, and the opposite effect of thiazides (Figure 2 explains the mechanism).

## Slide 12: Thiazide Diuretics
- [heading] FIRST-LINE FOR HYPERTENSION
- [heading] Thiazide Diuretics
- **Mechanism: block the Na-Cl cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule — a less potent natriuretic site (~5–8% of filtered sodium) than the loop.**
- Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone — first-line agents for essential hypertension; chlorthalidone has a longer half-life and is often preferred for 24-hour BP control.
- **Adverse effects: hyponatremia (can be significant, especially in older adults), hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia (can precipitate gout), and hypercalcemia.**
- Thiazides decrease calcium excretion (opposite of loop diuretics) — can be therapeutically useful in patients with osteoporosis and recurrent calcium-containing kidney stones.
- Lose efficacy as GFR falls below approximately 30 mL/min — loop diuretics are generally preferred once renal function is significantly reduced.
- **14**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: The "thiazides lose efficacy in advanced CKD" point sets up a direct bridge to Session 2's hypertension-in-CKD content — flag that connection explicitly here.

Instructor script:

Block NCC in the distal tubule. First-line in hypertension. Adverse effects: hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia, hypercalcemia. Lose efficacy at GFR below about 30.

## Slide 15: Choosing a Diuretic for Advancing CKD
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] Choosing a Diuretic for Advancing CKD
- A patient with hypertension and progressively worsening chronic kidney disease (eGFR now 25 mL/min) has been well controlled on chlorthalidone for years, but blood pressure has crept up over the last several visits.
- **Ask yourself:** **What is the most likely explanation, and what change in therapy would you consider?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **Thiazide diuretics lose much of their natriuretic efficacy once GFR falls below roughly 30 mL/min, because there is too little filtered sodium reaching the distal tubule for the drug to act on meaningfully. A loop diuretic, which acts upstream at the thick ascending limb, remains effective even in advanced CKD and is the more appropriate choice here.**

## Slide 17: Potassium-Sparing Diuretics
- [heading] PROTECTING POTASSIUM
- [heading] Potassium-Sparing Diuretics
- **Two mechanistically distinct subgroups acting at the collecting duct, both reducing potassium and hydrogen ion secretion.**
- Aldosterone receptor antagonists: spironolactone, eplerenone — block aldosterone's effect directly; spironolactone also has antiandrogen effects (gynecomastia, menstrual irregularities) that eplerenone largely avoids.
- Epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) blockers: amiloride, triamterene — block sodium channels independent of aldosterone.
- **Primary adverse effect across the class: hyperkalemia — the risk rises sharply when combined with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or in renal impairment (a direct preview of Session 2's cardiorenal drug-interaction content).**
- Often used in combination with a loop or thiazide diuretic specifically to offset potassium loss from the more potent class.
- **21**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Flag the hyperkalemia-with-ACEi/ARB interaction explicitly as a preview — this exact combination becomes a major theme in Session 2 when RAAS blockers are introduced.

Instructor script:

Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone) and ENaC blockers (amiloride, triamterene). Hyperkalemia is the main risk, especially with ACE inhibitors, ARBs and CKD.

## Slide 19: Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors & Osmotic Diuretics
- [heading] NICHE BUT IMPORTANT
- [heading] Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors & Osmotic Diuretics
- **●  Acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor):** blocks Na+/HCO3- reabsorption in the proximal tubule; weak diuretic effect but valued for non-diuretic uses — glaucoma (reduces aqueous humor production), altitude sickness prophylaxis, and metabolic alkalosis correction. Causes a mild metabolic acidosis as a predictable adverse effect.
- **●  Mannitol (osmotic diuretic):** an inert, filtered but poorly reabsorbed solute that holds water in the tubule lumen osmotically. Used to reduce elevated intracranial or intraocular pressure and in certain cases of rhabdomyolysis-associated kidney injury prevention. Requires intact renal perfusion to be effective and can worsen volume overload if used inappropriately.
- **23**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Both of these classes are "know the niche use, not the everyday one" — students should walk away knowing acetazolamide = glaucoma/altitude sickness and mannitol = ICP/IOP reduction, more than thinking of either as a mainstream diuretic.

Instructor script:

Acetazolamide for glaucoma, altitude sickness and alkalosis. Mannitol for raised ICP and IOP.

## Slide 23: The Hyperkalemia Setup
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] The Hyperkalemia Setup
- A patient with stage 3 chronic kidney disease and heart failure is taking an ACE inhibitor and is started on spironolactone for additional heart-failure benefit. Two weeks later, a routine basic metabolic panel shows a potassium of 6.1 mEq/L.
- **Ask yourself:** **What is the mechanistic explanation for this finding, and what does it suggest about future drug combinations in this unit?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **Both the ACE inhibitor (by reducing aldosterone) and spironolactone (by directly blocking the aldosterone receptor) independently reduce potassium excretion — combined, and in the setting of CKD (which already impairs potassium excretion), the hyperkalemia risk compounds. This is the same additive-risk logic that will reappear as the "triple whammy" in Session 2.**

## Slide 27: Furosemide (Lasix)
- **DRUG PROFILE**
- [heading] Furosemide (Lasix)
- LOOP DIURETIC
- [heading] FORMULATION
- Tablets 20/40/80 mg; oral solution; IV injection 10 mg/mL
- [heading] DOSE (ADULT)
- [heading] Edema: 20-80 mg PO once or BID, titrate to response (up to 600 mg/day in severe CKD or HF); IV 20-40 mg slowly (no faster than 4 mg/min)
- [heading] INDICATIONS
- Edema (heart failure, cirrhosis, kidney disease), hypertension, acute pulmonary edema, hypercalcemia
- [heading] ADVERSE EFFECTS
- Hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, volume depletion, hyperuricemia, ototoxicity
