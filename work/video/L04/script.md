## CHAPTER 1 · GOUT (≈1:00–12:30)

**Title card:** *Chapter 1 — Gout: crystals and two very different treatment phases* (S3)

**1.1 · S3–S4 · How gout develops** — DRAW, one box at a time with purple arrows: *Purine metabolism → Uric acid → Monosodium urate crystals → Joint inflammation.* Spike forms from little dots at box 3.
SAY: "Gout is a crystal-driven inflammatory arthritis. The chain is short: purine metabolism makes uric acid; uric acid forms monosodium urate crystals; and the crystals set off joint inflammation. Remember this chain, because every gout drug targets one specific point on it."

**1.2 · S5 · Zoom in** — DRAW (camera zooms into the chain): *Purine breakdown → hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid* (small label "via xanthine oxidase" with a purple scissors doodle on the last arrow) → *hyperuricemia → crystals in joint → neutrophil/macrophage activation (NLRP3, IL-1β) → acute flare.* Neo the neutrophil wakes up, sees Spike, and panics (little "!!").
On the side, four orange sticky notes drop in one by one: *purine- and fructose-rich diet ↑ production · alcohol, esp. beer ↑ uric acid and ↓ renal excretion · thiazide and loop diuretics ↓ renal clearance · CKD ↓ excretion.*
SAY: "Let's zoom in. Purines break down to hypoxanthine, then xanthine, and xanthine oxidase turns that into uric acid. Too much uric acid in the blood is hyperuricemia; crystals settle in the joint; neutrophils and macrophages get activated — that's the NLRP3 and IL-1 beta step — and boom, an acute flare.
What pushes uric acid up? A diet rich in purines and fructose raises production. Alcohol — especially beer — raises uric acid and lowers how much the kidney gets rid of. Diuretics, especially thiazides and loop diuretics, reduce renal urate clearance. And chronic kidney disease lowers excretion and keeps the level high."

**1.3 · S6 (Figure 4) · Two buckets** — DRAW: two big buckets. Blue drug names fly into each: Bucket 1 *"Lowers uric acid"*: allopurinol, febuxostat (arrow to the xanthine oxidase step: "↓ production"), probenecid (arrow to a kidney doodle: "↑ excretion"). Bucket 2 *"Calms the flare"*: colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids (arrows to the inflamed joint).
SAY: "Here's the deck's big sorting trick: two buckets. Bucket one lowers uric acid — allopurinol and febuxostat reduce production; probenecid increases what the kidney excretes. Bucket two treats the inflamed joint — colchicine, NSAIDs and corticosteroids. Keep those buckets in your head; they're the whole chapter."

**1.4 · S9 · Acute vs chronic (the big one)** — DRAW: a vertical line splits the board. Left (orange): *ACUTE FLARE — goal: control pain and inflammation now — colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids.* Right: *CHRONIC — goal: lower serum uric acid, prevent future flares — allopurinol, febuxostat (↓ production), probenecid (↑ excretion).* Then a big red ⚠ banner across the middle: **"Do NOT start urate-lowering therapy during an acute flare."** A small clock doodle on the right side: *"start or adjust only once the flare has resolved."*
SAY: "This is one of the single most testable ideas in gout. Two different goals at two different times. During a flare, the goal is pain and inflammation control right now — colchicine, an NSAID, or a corticosteroid. Long term, the goal is lowering serum uric acid to prevent future flares — allopurinol, febuxostat, or probenecid. And here's the rule in red: do not start urate-lowering therapy during an acute flare. Starting or changing it during a flare can actually prolong the flare. You start or adjust it only once the flare has resolved."

**Mini recap 1A (music lifts briefly)** — DRAW: the split board shrinks to a sticky note: *Flare → calm it. Later → lower urate.*
SAY: "Quick recap: flare — calm it. Later — lower the urate. Never mix up the timing."

**1.5 · S10–S11 · Colchicine (mechanism template)**
- *Plain language:* DRAW: Neo the neutrophil trying to march toward Spike; ANALOGY bubble: "colchicine takes the wheels off the neutrophil's cart." SAY: "Colchicine doesn't touch uric acid at all. It stops the inflammatory cells from showing up and getting fired up."
- *Terminology + steps (purple, one line at a time):* *Colchicine binds tubulin → blocks microtubule polymerization → reduced neutrophil migration and NLRP3 activation → less inflammation at the crystal site* (green).
  SAY: "In real terms: colchicine binds tubulin and blocks microtubule polymerization — it disrupts microtubule formation in neutrophils. Result: less neutrophil migration and activation, less NLRP3 activation, and less inflammation at the crystal site."
- *Clinical (orange/red):* sticky notes: *start early — best within 24 hours of flare onset* · red: **narrow therapeutic window** · red: *GI effects (diarrhea) are common and dose-limiting* · *adjust dose for renal or hepatic impairment* · red ⚠ *CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors (clarithromycin, cyclosporine, strong CYP3A4 inhibitors) raise toxicity.*
  SAY: "Clinically: it works best started early — within 24 hours of the flare. It has a narrow therapeutic window, and GI effects, especially diarrhea, are common and dose-limiting. Adjust the dose for kidney or liver impairment. And watch interactions: CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors — like clarithromycin, cyclosporine, and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors — raise colchicine toxicity. That's your Lecture 1 CYP toolkit showing up again."
- *Recap:* "Colchicine: microtubules, neutrophils, start early, narrow window, CYP3A4."

**1.6 · S12–S13 · Allopurinol vs febuxostat**
- *Plain language:* DRAW: the xanthine → uric acid arrow from 1.2 returns; a blue "STOP" hand slaps onto the enzyme. SAY: "Now bucket one. These drugs shut down the factory that makes uric acid."
- *Terminology/steps:* *Hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid*; blue label: **allopurinol (via oxypurinol) and febuxostat block xanthine oxidase** → green *↓ uric acid production.*
  SAY: "Both allopurinol — working through its metabolite oxypurinol — and febuxostat are xanthine oxidase inhibitors. Same enzyme, less uric acid made."
- *Side-by-side cards:* Allopurinol card: *first-line for most patients* · red: **HLA-B*58:01 → risk of severe hypersensitivity / severe cutaneous reaction** (a small DNA doodle with "Lecture 1!") · *reduce dose in renal impairment and with azathioprine or mercaptopurine.* Febuxostat card: *also an XO inhibitor, different structure* · *alternative when allopurinol isn't tolerated or is contraindicated* · red ⚠ **boxed warning: cardiovascular death.**
  SAY: "Allopurinol is first-line for most patients. Big safety point: the HLA-B star 58:01 allele raises the risk of a severe hypersensitivity reaction — a direct callback to pharmacogenomics from Lecture 1. Reduce the dose in renal impairment, and when it's combined with azathioprine or mercaptopurine. Febuxostat is also a xanthine oxidase inhibitor with a different structure — the alternative when allopurinol isn't tolerated or is contraindicated — but it carries a boxed warning for cardiovascular death."
- *How to start them (S13):* DRAW: a staircase "start low → titrate up → toward a serum urate target"; red: *not during a flare*; blue: *give colchicine or an NSAID as prophylaxis for 3–6 months.*
  SAY: "How do you start one? Start low and titrate up toward a serum urate target. Never during a flare. And cover the first months with colchicine or an NSAID as prophylaxis — three to six months."
- *Recap:* "XO inhibitors: allopurinol first, check HLA-B*58:01; febuxostat second, boxed CV warning; start low, cover with prophylaxis."

**1.7 · S14–S15 · Probenecid and pegloticase**
- DRAW: a kidney tubule doodle. *Probenecid → blocks URAT1 in the renal tubule → reabsorption falls, excretion rises* (blue arrow, green "more uric acid out"). ANALOGY bubble: "a bouncer who stops uric acid from sneaking back in."
  SAY: "Probenecid is a uricosuric — not a xanthine oxidase inhibitor. It blocks the URAT1 transporter in the renal tubule, so less uric acid is reabsorbed and more is excreted."
- Red notes: *avoid with a history of uric acid kidney stones or significant renal impairment* · *needs adequate hydration* · *second-line.*
  SAY: "Because it sends more uric acid through the kidney, avoid it in patients with uric acid kidney stones or significant renal impairment, and make sure they hydrate. It's an older, second-line oral option."
- DRAW: Pac-man-like enzyme labeled *uricase (pegloticase, rasburicase)* eats uric acid and spits out a happy water droplet labeled *allantoin — soluble, excreted.*
  SAY: "Pegloticase is a recombinant uricase enzyme: it converts uric acid to allantoin, which is soluble and easily excreted. It's given by IV infusion and reserved for severe, refractory gout — because of cost, infusion-reaction risk, and the need for specialty administration."
- Red ⚠: **Pegloticase and rasburicase: contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (hemolysis)** · *infusion reactions are common; anti-drug antibodies can reduce response.*
  SAY: "Two safety points: pegloticase and rasburicase are contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because of hemolysis risk — another Lecture 1 gene. And infusion reactions are common; anti-drug antibodies can reduce the response."
- *Recap:* "Probenecid: kidney, URAT1, no stones. Pegloticase: enzyme, refractory only, test G6PD."

**1.8 · S16–S18 · Back to our cold-open patient** — DRAW: the swollen toe returns. Three blue choices appear; the "treat flare, defer ULT" one gets a green check.
SAY: "So — our patient with the hot, swollen big toe. Acute treatment, chronic therapy, or both? Treat the acute flare first — colchicine, an NSAID or a corticosteroid — and defer urate-lowering therapy until the flare resolves. Starting it now could worsen or prolong the flare. Two seconds, like I promised."

**1.9 · S19 · Names reveal the class** — DRAW: a name-tag table writes itself: *-ostat → XO inhibitor (febuxostat; allopurinol is the exception) · -ase → enzyme/uricase (pegloticase, rasburicase) · colchicine → plant alkaloid, no stem · probenecid → uricosuric · -profen → NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) · -sone/-olone → corticosteroids (prednisone, methylprednisolone).*
SAY: "Name clues help: 'ostat' marks xanthine oxidase inhibitors — febuxostat — with allopurinol as the exception. 'Ase' means enzyme, so pegloticase and rasburicase are uricases. 'Profen' NSAIDs and 'sone' or 'olone' corticosteroids treat flares. Use the suffix as a hook — then confirm the class before you prescribe."

**Chapter 1 recap (recap music)** — DRAW: the two buckets return with every drug in place; red ⚠ notes pinned: *HLA-B*58:01 · febuxostat CV death · colchicine + CYP3A4/P-gp · probenecid stones · uricase G6PD.*
SAY: "Gout in one breath: flare — colchicine, NSAID or steroid; later — lower urate with allopurinol first, febuxostat second, probenecid for under-excretion; pegloticase for refractory gout. And the red flags: HLA-B star 58:01, febuxostat's CV boxed warning, colchicine with CYP3A4 inhibitors, stones with probenecid, and G6PD with uricases."

## CHAPTER 2 · SKELETAL MUSCLE RELAXANTS (≈12:30–24:00)

**Title card:** *Chapter 2 — Muscle relaxants: spasm, spasticity, rigidity* (S20)

**2.1 · S21 · Not the same thing** — DRAW: three panels. *Muscle spasm*: a back with a lightning bolt — "sudden, involuntary contraction, often from acute musculoskeletal injury; usually centrally acting relaxants." *Spasticity*: a leg that resists faster when moved faster (speedometer doodle) — "velocity-dependent increased tone, from upper motor neuron injury." *Rigidity*: a stiff board — "constant increased tone, independent of velocity."
SAY: "First, vocabulary — because these are not the same thing. A spasm is a sudden, involuntary contraction, often from an acute musculoskeletal injury, usually treated with centrally acting relaxants. Spasticity is increased tone that depends on velocity — the faster you move the limb, the more it resists — and it comes from upper motor neuron injury. Rigidity is constant increased tone, regardless of velocity. Spasticity and rigidity often need distinct drug approaches."

**2.2 · S22 · Where muscle tone is controlled (built top to bottom)** — DRAW a vertical cable, one level at a time (purple): *Upper motor neurons (descending inhibitory pathways) → spinal interneurons (GABA, glycine, alpha-2) → alpha motor neuron → neuromuscular junction (ACh release) → muscle fiber contraction (SR Ca²⁺ release).* Then blue drug flags snap to their levels: *cyclobenzaprine → brainstem; baclofen, tizanidine → spinal interneurons and afferents; botulinum toxin → junction; dantrolene → inside the fiber on RyR1.*
SAY: "Let's walk the path from the top down. Upper motor neurons send descending inhibitory signals. In the spinal cord, interneurons use GABA, glycine, and alpha-2 signaling. Then the alpha motor neuron, then the neuromuscular junction where acetylcholine is released, and finally the muscle fiber, which contracts when the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases calcium. Now drop the drugs on the map: cyclobenzaprine works in the brainstem; baclofen and tizanidine on spinal interneurons and afferents; botulinum toxin at the junction; and dantrolene inside the fiber, at RyR1."

**2.3 · S23–S24 (Figure 5) · Two buckets again** — DRAW: big "CNS" cloud vs a muscle fiber. Into the CNS cloud: *cyclobenzaprine, tizanidine, baclofen (first-line); carisoprodol, methocarbamol, metaxalone, chlorzoxazone (other options).* Onto the muscle: *dantrolene.* At the junction: *botulinum toxin.* Red-pen circle around dantrolene: "the odd one out."
SAY: "This is the highest-yield distinction in the section: where does the drug act? Centrally acting agents — cyclobenzaprine, tizanidine and baclofen first-line, plus carisoprodol, methocarbamol, metaxalone and chlorzoxazone — reduce muscle tone from inside the CNS. Peripherally: dantrolene acts directly on skeletal muscle, and botulinum toxin acts at the neuromuscular junction — useful when a central mechanism isn't wanted or isn't safe. Dantrolene is the peripheral exception."

**Mini recap 2A** — sticky note: *CNS: baclofen · tizanidine · cyclobenzaprine (+ others). Muscle: dantrolene. Junction: botulinum toxin.*

**2.4 · S27–S28 · Baclofen** — The Pill walks in wearing "BACLOFEN".
- *Plain:* ANALOGY bubble: "turns down the volume on the spinal cord's 'contract!' messages." SAY: "Baclofen quiets the spinal cord's signals to the muscle."
- *Steps (purple):* *Binds GABA-B receptors (spinal cord) → opens K⁺ channels, closes Ca²⁺ channels: presynaptic inhibition → less excitatory neurotransmitter release → lower motor neuron firing → reduced spasticity* (green).
  SAY: "Technically: baclofen is a GABA-B receptor agonist in the spinal cord. It opens potassium channels and closes calcium channels — presynaptic inhibition — so less excitatory transmitter is released, motor neurons fire less, and spasticity drops."
- *Clinical:* *use: spasticity, especially spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis* · *intrathecal option for severe spasticity* · red: **CNS depression — sedation, additive with other CNS depressants** · *weakness* · *renal dose adjustment* · red ⚠ **never stop abruptly (especially intrathecal): seizures, hyperthermia, rebound spasticity.**
  SAY: "It's used for spasticity — especially from spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis — with an intrathecal option for severe cases. The major concern is CNS depression: sedation, and added risk with other CNS depressants. It causes weakness, and it needs renal dose adjustment. And the big one: never stop it abruptly — especially the intrathecal pump. Withdrawal can cause seizures, hyperthermia and rebound spasticity."
- *Recap:* "Baclofen: GABA-B, spasticity, sedation, kidneys, never stop abruptly."

**2.5 · S29–S30 · Tizanidine**
- *Steps:* *Activates central alpha-2 receptors → less presynaptic release of excitatory amino acids → lower motor neuron activity → reduced muscle tone.*
  SAY: "Tizanidine is a central alpha-2 adrenergic agonist. It reduces presynaptic release of excitatory amino acids, so motor neurons fire less and tone falls."
- *Clinical:* red: *sedation, hypotension and bradycardia, dry mouth* · red: **hepatotoxicity → monitor liver enzymes** · red ⚠ **CYP1A2 inhibitors — ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine — strongly raise levels (contraindicated)** · *additive effect with antihypertensives.* DRAW: a small blood-pressure cuff with a down arrow next to "alpha-2".
  SAY: "Because it's alpha-2, think blood pressure: hypotension and bradycardia, plus sedation and dry mouth. It's hepatotoxic, so monitor liver enzymes with regular use. And a must-know interaction: CYP1A2 inhibitors — ciprofloxacin and fluvoxamine — strongly raise tizanidine levels, so they're contraindicated together. It also adds to antihypertensives."
- *Recap:* "Tizanidine: alpha-2, low BP, liver tests, no cipro or fluvoxamine."

**2.6 · S31–S32 · Cyclobenzaprine**
- *Steps:* *acts in the brainstem → antagonizes 5-HT2 and descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways → reduced tonic somatic motor activity → muscle relaxation.*
  SAY: "Cyclobenzaprine is structurally related to the tricyclic antidepressants. It acts in the brainstem, antagonizing 5-HT2 and descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways, which reduces tonic somatic motor activity."
- *Clinical:* *primary use: short-term (2–3 weeks) for acute, localized muscle spasm* · *prominent sedation — often dosed at night* · red: **anticholinergic — dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion; Beers list — caution in older adults** · red ⚠ **serotonin syndrome with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol; MAOIs contraindicated.** DRAW: a grandpa doodle with a red "Beers" tag.
  SAY: "It's for short-term treatment of acute, localized muscle spasm — two to three weeks. Sedation is prominent, so it's often dosed at night. It's anticholinergic — dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion — which puts it on the Beers list: use caution in older adults. And there's serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs, SNRIs and tramadol; MAOIs are contraindicated."
- *Recap:* "Cyclobenzaprine: tricyclic cousin, brainstem, sleepy, anticholinergic, serotonin syndrome."

**2.7 · S33 · The rest of the central crew + botulinum toxin** — DRAW four quick character cards: *Carisoprodol* (red padlock): **metabolized to meprobamate; Schedule IV; dependence/misuse potential.** *Methocarbamol*: generally less sedating than cyclobenzaprine. *Metaxalone*: relatively favorable sedation profile. *Chlorzoxazone* (red liver): rare but notable hepatotoxicity. Then *Botulinum toxin* at the junction: **blocks presynaptic ACh release; focal spasticity or dystonia; local, temporary — repeat injections.**
SAY: "Rounding out the central group: carisoprodol is metabolized to meprobamate and is a Schedule IV controlled substance with dependence and misuse potential. Methocarbamol is generally less sedating than cyclobenzaprine. Metaxalone has a relatively favorable sedation profile in this group. Chlorzoxazone carries a rare but notable risk of hepatotoxicity. And botulinum toxin is a third mechanism entirely: it blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction — used for focal spasticity or dystonia, with a local, temporary effect that needs repeat injections."

**2.8 · S34–S35 · Dantrolene (mechanism template)**
- *Plain:* DRAW: a muscle fiber with a calcium "water tower" (the sarcoplasmic reticulum). ANALOGY bubble: "dantrolene turns the tap on the calcium tower." SAY: "Dantrolene is the one that skips the nervous system and goes straight to the muscle."
- *Steps (purple, one at a time):* *Nerve impulse: action potential, DHPR activated → RyR1 opens: Ca²⁺ released from the SR → contraction → (blue) dantrolene blocks RyR1 → less Ca²⁺ → relaxation* (green).
  SAY: "Step by step: the action potential activates the DHPR; RyR1 opens and calcium pours out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum; the fiber contracts. Dantrolene blocks RyR1 — it decreases calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum — so the muscle relaxes. That acts directly on the fiber, not the CNS, unlike every central agent in this section."
- *Clinical:* orange: **use #1: malignant hyperthermia — a true emergency (triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine); IV formulation for the acute crisis** · *use #2: chronic spasticity when central agents aren't appropriate* · *also used for neuroleptic malignant syndrome* · red: *hepatotoxicity · generalized weakness.* DRAW: an operating-room doodle with a thermometer shooting up; the Pill rushes in with an IV bag.
  SAY: "Its must-know use: malignant hyperthermia, a perioperative emergency triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine, where IV dantrolene is literally life-saving. Second use: chronic spasticity when central agents aren't appropriate. It's also used for neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Watch for hepatotoxicity and generalized weakness."
- *Recap:* "Dantrolene: RyR1, muscle not brain, malignant hyperthermia, liver."

**2.9 · S38 · Name clues** — DRAW the table: *-curonium/-curium → nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers (vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium) · succinylcholine → depolarizing blocker · -azepam → benzodiazepine, GABA-A (diazepam) · cyclo- (tricyclic ring) → cyclobenzaprine · -idine (imidazoline) → central alpha-2 (tizanidine) · baclofen → no shared stem, GABA-B · -trolene → RyR1 (dantrolene) · -toxin → blocks ACh release (botulinum toxin).*
SAY: "Name clues: 'curonium' and 'curium' are nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers; succinylcholine is the depolarizing one. 'Azepam' — benzodiazepines like diazepam, acting at GABA-A. 'Cyclo' for the tricyclic-like cyclobenzaprine, 'idine' for the alpha-2 agonist tizanidine, 'trolene' for dantrolene at RyR1, and 'toxin' for botulinum toxin. Baclofen has no shared stem — you just have to know it's GABA-B."

**Chapter 2 recap** — DRAW: the S22 cable map returns with every drug flag and one red word each: *baclofen — don't stop · tizanidine — cipro/liver · cyclobenzaprine — elderly/serotonin · carisoprodol — Schedule IV · dantrolene — MH/liver.*
SAY: "Muscle relaxants in one breath: spasm, spasticity and rigidity differ. Baclofen — GABA-B, never stop abruptly. Tizanidine — alpha-2, low blood pressure, liver tests, no ciprofloxacin or fluvoxamine. Cyclobenzaprine — short-term, sedating, anticholinergic, serotonin syndrome. Carisoprodol — Schedule IV. ## CHAPTER 3 · BONE PHARMACOLOGY (≈24:00–36:30)

**Title card:** *Chapter 3 — Bone: balancing formation and resorption* (S39)

**3.1 · S40 · Bone remodeling** — DRAW: the seesaw. Bricky the osteoblast (hard hat) on one side: *build new bone matrix — promoted by several therapies.* Chomp the osteoclast on the other: *resorb existing bone — suppressed by most therapies.*
SAY: "Bone is always being rebuilt. Osteoblasts build new bone matrix; osteoclasts resorb — break down — existing bone. Nearly every osteoporosis drug works by tipping this balance: slowing osteoclasts, or stimulating osteoblasts."

**3.2 · S41 · RANKL / OPG** — DRAW step by step: *osteoblast or stromal cell expresses RANKL (a little key) → RANKL binds RANK on osteoclast precursors (the lock) → osteoclast differentiation and activation → bone resorption releases calcium.* Then: *OPG = decoy receptor* (a fake lock that grabs the key). Blue: *denosumab mimics OPG.* Notes: *estrogen increases OPG · PTH: intermittent dosing favors formation, continuous exposure raises RANKL and resorption · sclerostin inhibits Wnt signaling in osteoblasts.* ANALOGY bubble: "RANKL is the key that turns on the chomper; OPG is a decoy lock that catches the key."
SAY: "How do osteoclasts get switched on? Osteoblasts or stromal cells express RANKL. RANKL binds RANK on osteoclast precursors, which drives osteoclast differentiation and activation — and resorption releases calcium. OPG is a decoy receptor that grabs RANKL and blocks it. Estrogen increases OPG, and the drug denosumab mimics OPG. Two more facts you'll need later: PTH given intermittently favors bone formation, while continuous PTH raises RANKL and resorption. And sclerostin inhibits Wnt signaling in osteoblasts."

**3.3 · S42–S43 (Figure 6) · Calcium homeostasis loop** — DRAW: a blood vessel with calcium pebbles. Pebbles drop → parathyroid doodle releases PTH → three purple arrows drawn one at a time: *bone (↑ osteoclast resorption) · kidney (↑ calcium reabsorption, activates vitamin D) · intestine (indirectly, via activated vitamin D → ↑ absorption)* → pebbles rise. Calcitonin enters from the side: *lowers serum calcium, opposes PTH.*
SAY: "Calcium is the substrate being regulated. When serum calcium drops, PTH is released and raises it three ways: from bone, through osteoclast resorption; from the kidney, through more calcium reabsorption and activating vitamin D; and from the intestine — but indirectly, through that activated vitamin D, which increases absorption. Calcitonin does the opposite: it lowers serum calcium and opposes PTH. That's a negative feedback loop, and it's the backdrop for every bone drug."

**3.4 · S46 · Risk factors** — DRAW five orange icons: *aging · estrogen deficiency (postmenopausal) · **chronic glucocorticoid use** (circled) · low calcium or vitamin D · low physical activity.*
SAY: "Osteoporosis risk factors: aging, estrogen deficiency like after menopause, low calcium or vitamin D, low physical activity — and the high-yield one: chronic glucocorticoid use, which connects straight back to the steroids from Session 1."

**3.5 · S47–S48 · Bisphosphonates (mechanism template)**
- *Plain:* DRAW: Chomp the osteoclast bites into bone and gets a stomach-ache. ANALOGY bubble: "the bone is booby-trapped; the chomper eats the trap." SAY: "Bisphosphonates hide in the bone, and the osteoclast that eats them shuts down."
- *Steps:* *drug binds hydroxyapatite at resorption sites → osteoclast takes it up during resorption → nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates inhibit FPP synthase (mevalonate pathway) → loss of prenylation of small GTPases → osteoclast apoptosis → less resorption* (green).
  SAY: "Real terms: the drug binds hydroxyapatite at resorption sites. The osteoclast takes it up while resorbing. Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates inhibit FPP synthase in the mevalonate pathway; small GTPases lose prenylation; and the osteoclast undergoes apoptosis — so there's less resorption."
- *The family:* *alendronate, risedronate — oral, weekly · ibandronate — oral, monthly · zoledronic acid — IV, as infrequently as once yearly* (a calendar doodle filling in fewer squares each row — Figure 7). Green: *first-line for most patients.*
  SAY: "They're first-line for most patients with osteoporosis. Alendronate and risedronate are oral, typically weekly; ibandronate is oral, monthly; and zoledronic acid is IV, as infrequently as once a year — which matters a lot for adherence."
- *Clinical (S48):* *long skeletal half-life — effect persists after stopping* · *replete calcium and vitamin D before starting* · red: *contraindicated with severe renal impairment (CrCl below about 30–35; the profiles use 35)* · red: *osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femur fracture with long-term use* · *consider a drug holiday after 3–5 years of oral therapy.*
  SAY: "Key facts: a long skeletal half-life, so the effect persists after stopping. Make sure calcium and vitamin D are repleted first. They're contraindicated in severe renal impairment — creatinine clearance below about 30 to 35; the drug profiles use 35. Long-term use brings rare but serious osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femur fracture, so consider a drug holiday after three to five years of oral therapy."

**3.6 · S52–S53 · Why the strict pill rules?** — DRAW a chain: *poor oral absorption (under 1%) → food, calcium, coffee and antacids reduce it further → take fasting with plain water, stay upright 30 minutes → lowers esophageal irritation risk.* Then an orange checklist: *full glass of water · first thing in the morning · empty stomach, well before food · stay upright (sitting or standing).* Red: *avoid with esophageal stricture, achalasia, or inability to sit upright.* Adverse effects card: *esophageal irritation · hypocalcemia (correct Ca and vitamin D first) · ONJ (rare) · atypical femoral fractures (rare, long-term).* IV card: *zoledronic acid → acute-phase (flu-like) reaction, mitigated with acetaminophen.*
SAY: "Why such strict rules for the pills? Absorption is tiny — under one percent — and food, calcium, coffee and antacids cut it further. So: take it first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach, with a full glass of plain water, and stay upright for thirty minutes. That keeps the tablet from irritating the esophagus — which is also why we avoid oral bisphosphonates with an esophageal stricture, achalasia, or if the patient can't sit upright. Adverse effects: esophageal irritation, hypocalcemia, and rarely osteonecrosis of the jaw or atypical femoral fractures. IV zoledronic acid can cause a flu-like acute-phase reaction, which acetaminophen helps."

**Mini recap 3A** — sticky note: *Bisphosphonates: osteoclast apoptosis · first-line · water, empty stomach, upright 30 min · kidneys · ONJ/AFF long-term.*

**3.7 · S54–S55 (Figure 8) · Beyond bisphosphonates — the seesaw** — DRAW: drugs land one at a time on the seesaw. Resorption side (blue onto Chomp): *denosumab (RANKL antibody, blocks osteoclast formation) · raloxifene (SERM) · calcitonin · (bisphosphonates).* Formation side (onto Bricky): *teriparatide (PTH 1-34, daily injection) and abaloparatide — anabolic · romosozumab (sclerostin antibody) — builds bone and limits resorption.* Green at the bottom: *BMD up, fracture risk down.*
SAY: "Beyond bisphosphonates, sort everything onto the seesaw. On the 'less resorption' side: denosumab, a monoclonal antibody against RANKL that blocks osteoclast formation; raloxifene, a selective estrogen receptor modulator with an estrogen-like effect on bone; and calcitonin. On the 'more formation' side — the anabolic exceptions: teriparatide, a PTH 1-34 analog given as a daily injection, and abaloparatide — and romosozumab, a sclerostin antibody that both builds bone and limits resorption. Either way: bone density up, fracture risk down."
- Red notes appear by each drug (S55): *teriparatide: 2-year lifetime limit, then follow with an antiresorptive* · *denosumab: stopping causes rebound vertebral fractures — transition to a bisphosphonate* · *raloxifene: ↑ VTE risk, hot flashes; may reduce breast cancer risk* · *romosozumab: boxed warning — MI, stroke, CV death.*
SAY: "And each one has a rule. Teriparatide: two years in a lifetime, then follow with an antiresorptive. Denosumab: if it's stopped, rebound vertebral fractures — so transition to a bisphosphonate. Raloxifene raises VTE risk and causes hot flashes, but may reduce breast cancer risk. Romosozumab carries a boxed warning for heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death."

**3.8 · S59 · Choosing therapy** — DRAW an orange checklist: *fracture risk (high risk → maybe anabolic first) · renal function · previous fractures · glucocorticoid exposure · adherence (weekly oral vs yearly IV vs injectable) · route preference.*
SAY: "Choosing a therapy is the Lecture 1 decision framework again: fracture risk — higher-risk patients may need an anabolic agent first; renal function; previous fractures; glucocorticoid exposure; adherence — weekly pill, yearly IV, or an injectable; and route preference."

**3.9 · S62 · Name clues** — DRAW: *-dronate → bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid = zoledronate) · -sumab → monoclonal antibodies (denosumab → RANKL, romosozumab → sclerostin) · -fene → SERM (raloxifene) · -tide → PTH analogs (teriparatide, abaloparatide) · calcitonin → hormone, no stem.*
SAY: "Name clues: 'dronate' is a bisphosphonate; 'sumab' marks the bone antibodies — denosumab for RANKL, romosozumab for sclerostin; 'fene' is the SERM raloxifene; 'tide' marks the PTH analogs; and calcitonin is just a hormone, no stem."

**Chapter 3 recap** — DRAW: the full seesaw with every drug and its red rule.
SAY: "Bone in one breath: osteoblasts build, osteoclasts chomp. Bisphosphonates are first-line — strict pill rules, kidney limits, rare jaw and femur problems. Denosumab blocks RANKL — never just stop it. Raloxifene — VTE. Teriparatide and abaloparatide build bone — two-year limit. Romosozumab builds and blocks — CV boxed warning."

## CHAPTER 5 · DRUG-PROFILE LIGHTNING ROUND (≈40:00–46:00) — *optional, see question 4*

**Title card:** *Chapter 5 — Lightning round: the details that change decisions* (S72: "know the dose logic, the serious adverse effect, the contraindication that changes decisions and the toxicity")
Format: fast flip-cards, one per drug, each with three lines — **dose rule · contraindication · toxicity** — and a tiny drawing. Music a little faster; narration tight.

- **Colchicine (S73):** flare 1.2 mg, then 0.6 mg an hour later, within 24 h; prophylaxis 0.6 mg once or twice daily · contraindicated: severe renal/hepatic impairment *with* CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors · toxicity: narrow window — severe GI toxicity, marrow suppression, multi-organ failure; avoid clarithromycin.
- **Allopurinol (S74):** start 50–100 mg, titrate every 2–5 weeks to serum urate under 6 mg/dL · HLA-B*58:01, renal dose reduction · allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (fever, rash, eosinophilia, organ injury); interacts with azathioprine and mercaptopurine.
- **Febuxostat (S75):** 40 mg, up to 80 mg if urate above 6 after 2 weeks · not with azathioprine or mercaptopurine; caution in CV disease · boxed warning: CV death; monitor liver tests.
- **Probenecid (S76):** contraindicated: uric acid stones, eGFR under 30, blood dyscrasias, during an acute flare · kidney stones — hydrate, alkalinize urine · raises penicillin, methotrexate and NSAID levels.
- **Pegloticase (S77):** IV every 2 weeks with methotrexate co-therapy · G6PD deficiency; stop other urate-lowering drugs · boxed warning: anaphylaxis, infusion reactions, hemolysis and methemoglobinemia in G6PD deficiency — premedicate, monitor urate.
- **Baclofen (S78):** start low, titrate · caution in renal impairment; never stop abruptly · overdose: coma, respiratory depression; withdrawal: seizures, hyperthermia, rebound spasticity.
- **Tizanidine (S79):** titrate (max 36 mg/day) · strong CYP1A2 inhibitors; caution with antihypertensives and liver disease · hypotension, bradycardia, CNS depression; check liver tests.
- **Cyclobenzaprine (S80):** 2–3 weeks · MAOIs within 14 days, arrhythmias, recent MI, hyperthyroidism; Beers list · serotonin syndrome with SSRIs and tramadol; tricyclic-type toxicity (arrhythmia, seizures).
- **Carisoprodol (S81):** up to 2–3 weeks · Schedule IV; acute intermittent porphyria · CNS depression with opioids and alcohol; dependence and withdrawal.
- **Methocarbamol (S82):** · IV form avoided in renal impairment (polyethylene glycol) · urine discoloration; CNS depression.
- **Dantrolene (S83):** malignant hyperthermia 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeat up to 10 mg/kg · active liver disease (oral); caution with calcium channel blockers (IV) · boxed warning: hepatotoxicity (oral); weakness.
- **Alendronate (S84):** 70 mg once weekly, fasting, 8 oz plain water, upright 30 min · stricture/achalasia, can't sit upright, hypocalcemia, CrCl below 35 · esophageal injury; ONJ and atypical femur fracture long-term.
- **Zoledronic acid (S85):** 5 mg IV once yearly (osteoporosis) · CrCl below 35, uncorrected hypocalcemia, pregnancy · AKI and hypocalcemia — hydrate, replete calcium and vitamin D first.
- **Denosumab (S86):** 60 mg SC every 6 months · hypocalcemia, pregnancy; severe hypocalcemia boxed warning in advanced CKD · rebound multiple vertebral fractures if stopped or doses missed — transition to a bisphosphonate.
- **Teriparatide (S87):** 20 mcg SC daily; 2-year lifetime limit · Paget disease, prior skeletal radiation, bone metastases, hypercalcemia, open epiphyses · hypercalcemia, orthostatic hypotension; follow with an antiresorptive.
- **Raloxifene (S88):** 60 mg daily · history of VTE, pregnancy, breastfeeding; stroke risk · boxed warning: VTE and death from stroke — stop before prolonged immobilization.
