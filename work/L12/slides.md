## Slide 5: Cholinergic (Muscarinic) Control of the Detrusor
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 6
- [heading] Cholinergic (Muscarinic) Control of the Detrusor
- **The parasympathetic pelvic nerve releases acetylcholine, which acts on M3 muscarinic receptors on the detrusor muscle to produce contraction — the primary driver of bladder emptying (voiding).**
- M3 is the dominant muscarinic subtype in the bladder, though M2 receptors are also present in high density (their functional contribution is smaller under normal conditions but may increase in certain disease states).
- This is the same muscarinic receptor family (and the same general autonomic pharmacology framework from Chapter 6) that governs other smooth muscle and glandular targets throughout the body — the bladder is simply one more target organ.
- **Because M3 blockade reduces detrusor contraction, antimuscarinic drugs (Section 5) are a logical treatment for conditions of bladder overactivity.**

## Slide 7: Adrenergic Control of the Bladder Neck and Detrusor
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 6
- [heading] Adrenergic Control of the Bladder Neck and Detrusor
- **The sympathetic hypogastric nerve releases norepinephrine, which acts on two different adrenergic receptor subtypes in two different locations, producing two different (and complementary) effects.**
- Alpha1 receptors at the bladder neck, trigone, and proximal urethra: norepinephrine causes contraction, helping maintain continence during the storage phase.
- Beta3 receptors on the detrusor body: norepinephrine causes relaxation, allowing the bladder to fill without a premature urge to void.
- Net effect during storage: sympathetic tone keeps the outlet closed (alpha1) while keeping the reservoir relaxed (beta3) — parasympathetic tone is low. During voiding, this pattern reverses.
- **Both of these receptor subtypes become specific drug targets later in this session: alpha1 blockers for BPH (Section 6), and beta3 agonists for overactive bladder (Section 5).**

## Slide 13: Antimuscarinic Agents
- [heading] BLOCKING THE CONTRACTION SIGNAL
- [heading] Antimuscarinic Agents
- **Mechanism: block M3 (and, to a lesser extent, M2) muscarinic receptors on the detrusor, reducing involuntary contractions and increasing functional bladder capacity.**
- Agents: oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, darifenacin — differ mainly in receptor selectivity and anticholinergic side-effect burden rather than core mechanism.
- **Adverse effects follow directly from systemic antimuscarinic blockade: dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and — importantly — cognitive impairment and confusion, especially in older adults.**
- Anticholinergic burden is cumulative across all of a patient's medications — oxybutynin is generally considered to have the highest CNS anticholinergic burden of this class and is often avoided in older adults or those with dementia risk.
- Caution/contraindication: narrow-angle glaucoma and significant urinary retention can be worsened by antimuscarinic therapy.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

## Slide 15: Beta-3 Agonists
- [heading] ENHANCING THE RELAXATION SIGNAL
- [heading] Beta-3 Agonists
- **Mechanism: stimulate beta3 adrenergic receptors on the detrusor, enhancing smooth muscle relaxation during the storage/filling phase.**
- Agent: mirabegron — the primary beta3 agonist in clinical use for overactive bladder.
- **Key advantage: lacks the systemic anticholinergic burden of antimuscarinics, making it a preferred option in older adults or patients already on multiple anticholinergic medications.**
- Adverse effects: can raise blood pressure (monitor in patients with uncontrolled hypertension) and may cause headache or nasopharyngitis; less commonly, urinary retention.
- Mirabegron can be used alone or combined with an antimuscarinic for refractory symptoms, since the two act by complementary (not redundant) mechanisms.

## Slide 21: Alpha1 Blockers for BPH
- [heading] RELAXING THE OUTFLOW TRACT
- [heading] Alpha1 Blockers for BPH
- **Mechanism: block alpha1 receptors at the bladder neck, prostatic smooth muscle, and proximal urethra, relaxing outflow resistance and improving urinary flow in benign prostatic hyperplasia.**
- Agents: tamsulosin, silodosin (more uroselective, less vascular effect) vs. doxazosin, terazosin (less selective, more likely to lower blood pressure systemically).
- **Adverse effects: orthostatic hypotension and a "first-dose effect" (pronounced hypotension with the first dose) — because alpha1 receptors also mediate vascular smooth muscle tone, not just bladder neck tone.**
- **This is the exact same receptor family targeted by alpha1-blocking antihypertensives and central agents discussed in Unit 5 — the difference here is clinical intent (urinary flow vs. blood pressure), not a different drug class.**
- Floppy iris syndrome is a recognized intraoperative complication during cataract surgery in patients on alpha1 blockers — relevant to preoperative medication reconciliation.

## Slide 23: 5-Alpha-Reductase Inhibitors
- [heading] CALLBACK TO CHAPTER 40
- [heading] 5-Alpha-Reductase Inhibitors
- **Mechanism: block 5-alpha-reductase, the enzyme that converts testosterone to the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT) — the same mechanism introduced in Session 1 for hirsutism and androgenetic alopecia.**
- Agents: finasteride, dutasteride — in BPH, reducing DHT shrinks prostatic tissue over time, improving urinary symptoms.
- Onset is slow: measurable symptomatic benefit typically takes 3–6 months, unlike the rapid onset of alpha1 blockers — the two classes are frequently combined for BPH, targeting both rapid symptom relief (alpha1 blocker) and long-term disease modification (5-ARI).
- **Adverse effects: decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, and — a key counseling point — these agents lower PSA levels by roughly half, which must be accounted for when interpreting PSA values in patients on long-term therapy.**
- Pregnancy caution: finasteride/dutasteride can cause ambiguous genitalia in a male fetus; pregnant partners should avoid handling crushed or broken tablets.

## Slide 26: PDE5 Inhibitors
- [heading] AN ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION
- [heading] PDE5 Inhibitors
- **Mechanism: block phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5), the enzyme that normally breaks down cGMP, prolonging smooth muscle relaxation in the corpus cavernosum and sustaining erectile function (Figure 16).**
- Agents: sildenafil, tadalafil (longer half-life, allows more flexible dosing), vardenafil.
- Common adverse effects: headache, flushing, dyspepsia, and visual disturbances (blue-tinged vision, more notable with sildenafil).
- **Absolute contraindication: concurrent use with any nitrate — both raise NO/cGMP signaling, and together they can cause profound, life-threatening hypotension. This mirrors the identical nitrate/PDE5-inhibitor warning from Unit 5's angina content, now from the opposite side of the interaction.**
- Use caution with alpha1 blockers as well — the additive hypotensive risk, while less severe than with nitrates, still warrants spacing doses or careful monitoring when both are used.

## Slide 42: Lecture Recall Quiz — Answer Key
- [heading] ANSWER KEY
- [heading] Lecture Recall Quiz — Answer Key
- **1.  A —** **Unopposed estrogen drives unchecked endometrial proliferation, significantly raising the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer in a patient with a uterus — this is why a progestin is always paired with estrogen therapy unless the patient has had a hysterectomy.**
- **2.  A —** **Tamoxifen is a tissue-selective estrogen receptor modulator that acts as an agonist in the endometrium (raising cancer risk) while acting as an antagonist in breast tissue; raloxifene shares the breast antagonist and bone agonist effects but is essentially neutral at the endometrium.**
- **3.  A —** **Hyperinsulinemia stimulates ovarian theca-cell androgen production directly and lowers hepatic SHBG production, raising free testosterone — the mechanistic link at the center of the PCOS-insulin resistance cycle (Figure 7).**
- **4.  A —** **Mirabegron enhances beta3-mediated detrusor relaxation without blocking muscarinic receptors, avoiding the cumulative anticholinergic burden that can precipitate or worsen confusion in susceptible patients, particularly older adults.**
- **5.  A —** **Alpha1 receptors are not bladder-specific — the same receptor family mediates vascular smooth muscle tone throughout the body, so alpha1 blockade at the bladder neck also lowers systemic vascular resistance, producing orthostatic hypotension and a first-dose effect.**
- **6.  A —** **Nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors both raise NO/cGMP signaling through independent mechanisms; combined, the effect on vascular smooth muscle relaxation is additive to synergistic, producing profound and potentially fatal hypotension.**
