## Slide 6: Growth Hormone Disorders
- [heading] GROWTH HORMONE AXIS
- [heading] Growth Hormone Disorders
- **●  GH deficiency:** treated with recombinant human growth hormone (somatropin); monitor growth velocity in children and IGF-1 levels in adults.
- **●  GH excess (acromegaly):** treated with somatostatin analogs (octreotide, lanreotide — suppress GH release), the GH-receptor antagonist pegvisomant, or dopamine agonists (cabergoline) in select cases.
- **●  Clinical pearl:** octreotide also has major uses outside the pituitary axis — variceal bleeding and carcinoid syndrome — worth flagging as a cross-system agent.

## Slide 8: Hyperprolactinemia and ADH Disorders
- [heading] PROLACTIN & POSTERIOR PITUITARY
- [heading] Hyperprolactinemia and ADH Disorders
- [heading] Hyperprolactinemia
- [heading] ADH Disorders
- Dopamine agonists are first-line: cabergoline, bromocriptine
- Dopamine normally inhibits prolactin release — agonists restore that inhibitory tone
- Used for prolactinomas and drug-induced hyperprolactinemia

- SIADH (ADH excess): fluid restriction first-line; vasopressin-receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) or demeclocycline for refractory cases
- Opposite problems — know which direction each drug moves free water

## Slide 12: Levothyroxine for Hypothyroidism
- [heading] REPLACEMENT THERAPY
- [heading] Levothyroxine for Hypothyroidism
- **Mechanism: synthetic T4, converted peripherally to active T3.**
- First-line for hypothyroidism; narrow therapeutic index — dose titrated using TSH (primary hypothyroidism) with repeat labs every 6–8 weeks after a dose change.
- Take on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before breakfast, consistently relative to other medications and food.
- **Significant absorption interactions: calcium, iron, and antacids can all reduce absorption — separate dosing by at least 4 hours.**
- **Older adults and patients with cardiac disease: start at lower doses and titrate slowly to avoid precipitating angina/arrhythmia.**
- **14**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: The absorption-interaction list is extremely high-yield — many patients are also on calcium or iron supplements, so timing counseling matters as much as the drug itself.

Instructor script:

Know: About 1.6 mcg/kg/day is full replacement. Start low in older adults and cardiac disease. Check TSH at 6-8 weeks. Estrogen and pregnancy raise needs. Over-replacement causes atrial fibrillation and bone loss.

## Slide 15: Methimazole vs. Propylthiouracil (PTU)
- [heading] HYPERTHYROIDISM
- [heading] Methimazole vs. Propylthiouracil (PTU)
- [heading] Methimazole
- [heading] Propylthiouracil (PTU)
- First-line for most non-pregnant patients — once-daily dosing, more convenient

- Preferred in first trimester of pregnancy and in thyroid storm

- Boxed warning: severe hepatotoxicity (more than methimazole) — reserve for specific indications

## Slide 16: Thyroid Storm: A Multi-Drug Approach
- [heading] ENDOCRINE EMERGENCY
- [heading] Thyroid Storm: A Multi-Drug Approach
- **A life-threatening hyperthyroid crisis requiring rapid, multi-pronged therapy.**
- Beta-blockers (propranolol) — control adrenergic symptoms (tachycardia, tremor) and also blunt peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion.
- PTU (preferred over methimazole) — blocks new hormone synthesis and peripheral conversion.
- Iodine solution (given after PTU/methimazole) — acutely blocks hormone release; must follow antithyroid drug to avoid providing substrate for new synthesis.
- Corticosteroids — also blunt peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion and treat relative adrenal insufficiency.
- **18**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: The "iodine after PTU, not before" sequencing is a classic high-yield detail — giving iodine first can actually worsen the crisis by providing more substrate for hormone synthesis before synthesis is blocked.

Instructor script:

Thyroid storm is treated with four drugs that hit four targets; the next two slides show the mechanism and the sequence.

- [heading] Sequencing Therapy in Thyroid Storm

## Slide 20: Glucocorticoids vs. Mineralocorticoids
- [heading] TWO HORMONE FAMILIES
- [heading] Glucocorticoids vs. Mineralocorticoids
- [heading] Glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone, dexamethasone)
- [heading] Mineralocorticoids (fludrocortisone)
- Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects
- Spectrum of relative potency and mineralocorticoid activity across agents
- Used across nearly every organ system for inflammatory/autoimmune disease
- Promotes renal sodium retention and potassium excretion
- Used as replacement therapy in primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease), alongside a glucocorticoid
- Monitor blood pressure and potassium
- **24**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Fludrocortisone is often under-emphasized relative to glucocorticoids — make the point explicitly that primary adrenal insufficiency needs BOTH hormone classes replaced, not just cortisol.

Instructor script:

Fludrocortisone replaces mineralocorticoid effect in Addison disease. Adverse effects: hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, infection, mood change, cataracts, adrenal suppression.

## Slide 23: Adrenocortical Antagonists
- [heading] BLOCKING ADRENAL HORMONES
- [heading] Adrenocortical Antagonists
- **●  Ketoconazole:** inhibits multiple steps in steroidogenesis (CYP enzymes); used for Cushing syndrome; significant drug-interaction and hepatotoxicity concerns.
- **●  Metyrapone:** blocks the final step of cortisol synthesis (11-β-hydroxylase); used diagnostically and for Cushing syndrome.
- **●  Mifepristone:** a glucocorticoid-receptor antagonist; used for hyperglycemia associated with Cushing syndrome when surgery is not an option (distinct from its progesterone-receptor antagonist use in pregnancy termination).
- **●  Spironolactone:** a mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist; used for hyperaldosteronism and as a potassium-sparing diuretic — ties back to the diuretic pharmacology concepts from earlier coursework.
- **29**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Mifepristone’s dual identity (glucocorticoid-receptor antagonist here vs. progesterone-receptor antagonist elsewhere) is worth flagging explicitly so students don’t conflate the two uses or assume a single mechanism.

Instructor script:

Enzyme blockers (ketoconazole, metyrapone) lower cortisol.

Know: Ketoconazole: hepatotoxicity, QT, strong CYP3A4 inhibition. Metyrapone: androgen and deoxycorticosterone excess.

## Slide 27: Recognizing Adrenal Crisis
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] Recognizing Adrenal Crisis
- A patient who has been on chronic high-dose prednisone for a rheumatologic condition abruptly stops the medication after running out of refills, then presents with hypotension, weakness, nausea, and confusion.
- **Ask yourself:** **What is the most likely cause of this presentation, and how should it be managed?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **This presentation is consistent with acute adrenal insufficiency (adrenal crisis) from abrupt glucocorticoid withdrawal after HPA-axis suppression. Management requires immediate IV hydrocortisone (which has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity) and IV fluids — this is a medical emergency, not a routine medication-refill issue.**
