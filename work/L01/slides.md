## Slide 5: What Is Pharmacology?
- [heading] DEFINITIONS
- [heading] What Is Pharmacology?
- **●  Pharmacology:** the scientific study of how drugs interact with living systems — from the molecular level to the whole patient.
- **●  Drug (medication):** any chemical substance used to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease, or to alter a physiologic process.
- **●  Pharmacologic effect:** a measurable biological change a drug produces (e.g., lower blood pressure).
- **●  Therapeutic effect:** the intended, beneficial pharmacologic effect for a given condition.
- **●  Adverse drug reaction (ADR):** an unintended and harmful response occurring at normal therapeutic doses.
- **●  Toxicity:** harmful effects that occur as the dose or exposure exceeds the therapeutic range.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Emphasize that "therapeutic effect" and "pharmacologic effect" are not synonyms — a drug can have pharmacologic effects that are not therapeutic (that is often exactly what an adverse effect is).

## Slide 7: Chemical, Generic, and Brand Names
- [heading] NAMING A DRUG
- [heading] Chemical, Generic, and Brand Names
- **●  Chemical name:** describes the exact molecular structure (rarely used in clinical practice).
- **●  Generic name:** the official, non-proprietary name assigned to the active drug — one generic name per drug, regardless of manufacturer.
- **●  Brand (trade) name:** the manufacturer’s marketed name; the same generic drug may have several brand names, and brand names change between countries.
- Example: acetaminophen (generic) is sold as Tylenol and under many store brands.
- **PA students should learn and prescribe by generic name — it is unambiguous, international, and avoids confusing a patient’s multiple brand-name products for different drugs.**

## Slide 14: Evidence-Based Drug Approval
- [heading] JUDGMENT
- [heading] Evidence-Based Drug Approval

- **●  Benefit vs. risk:** the central question regulators and prescribers must weigh for every approval and every prescription.

## Slide 22: Agonists vs. Antagonists
- [heading] BINDING & ACTIVATION
- [heading] Agonists vs. Antagonists
- [heading] Agonists (activate)
- [heading] Antagonists (block)
- Full agonist — maximal receptor activation
- Partial agonist — submaximal activation, even at full receptor occupancy
- Inverse agonist — reduces baseline (constitutive) receptor activity
- Competitive — reversibly competes with agonist at the same site; overcome by more agonist
- Noncompetitive — binds elsewhere or irreversibly reduces the maximal effect
- Irreversible — permanently inactivates the receptor until new receptor is synthesized

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: A classic exam trap: a competitive antagonist shifts the dose-response curve right (same Emax, more drug needed); a noncompetitive/irreversible antagonist lowers the Emax instead.

## Slide 30: The Dose-Response Curve
- [heading] READING THE CURVE
- [heading] The Dose-Response Curve
- **●  Potency:** how much drug is required to produce a given effect — a more potent drug needs a lower dose.
- **●  Efficacy (Emax):** the maximum effect a drug can produce, no matter how high the dose goes.
- **●  EC₅₀:** the drug concentration producing 50% of the maximal effect — the standard measure of potency.

## Slide 38: The Therapeutic Index
- [heading] SAFETY MARGIN
- [heading] The Therapeutic Index
- **Therapeutic Index (TI) = TD₅₀ ÷ ED₅₀ (toxic dose in 50% ÷ effective dose in 50%).**
- A wide TI means a large margin between an effective dose and a toxic dose — generally safer to dose.
- A narrow TI means effective and toxic doses are close together — requires careful dosing and often blood-level monitoring.
- **Classic narrow-TI drugs: warfarin, lithium, digoxin, phenytoin.**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: These four narrow-TI drugs are extremely high-yield — students should be able to name all four without hesitation and know that each requires therapeutic drug monitoring.

## Slide 50: Bioavailability

- [heading] Bioavailability
- **Bioavailability (F) = the fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged.**

- [heading] WHERE THE DRUG GOES
- [heading] Distribution
- **●  Plasma protein binding:** only unbound ("free") drug is pharmacologically active and able to cross membranes.
- **●  Lipid solubility:** lipophilic drugs cross membranes more readily and distribute into fatty tissue.
- **●  Tissue perfusion:** highly perfused organs (brain, heart, kidney) receive drug faster than poorly perfused tissue (fat, bone).
- **●  Volume of distribution (Vd):** a calculated value relating total drug in the body to plasma concentration — a high Vd suggests extensive tissue distribution.

> Speaker notes: Vd is a theoretical volume, not a real anatomic space — a drug with a very high Vd (like digoxin) is telling you it is mostly sequestered in tissue, not floating around in plasma.

## Slide 53: The Blood-Brain Barrier
- [heading] DISCUSSION
- [heading] The Blood-Brain Barrier
- **Why can some drugs readily enter the CNS while others cannot?**
- Tight junctions between endothelial cells restrict paracellular movement.
- Small, lipophilic, non-ionized drugs cross more easily than large, polar, or highly protein-bound drugs.
- Efflux transporters (like P-glycoprotein) can actively pump some drugs back out of the CNS.
