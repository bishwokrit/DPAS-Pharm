## Slide 5: Estrogens
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 40
- [heading] Estrogens
- **Mechanism: bind intracellular estrogen receptors (ER-alpha, ER-beta), regulating gene transcription in reproductive, bone, cardiovascular, and CNS tissue.**
- Physiologic effects: endometrial proliferation, breast development, bone density maintenance, favorable lipid profile effects (↑ HDL, ↓ LDL).
- Clinical uses: hormonal contraception (combined with a progestin), menopausal hormone therapy for vasomotor symptoms, and hypogonadism.
- **Adverse effects: venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk, increased risk with smoking and age over 35, nausea, breast tenderness, and a possible increase in certain hormone-sensitive cancer risks with long-term use.**
- Contraindications: history of VTE, estrogen-sensitive breast cancer, uncontrolled hypertension, and smokers over age 35 — all raise cardiovascular/thrombotic risk substantially.

## Slide 6: Progestins
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 40
- [heading] Progestins
- **Mechanism: bind progesterone receptors, counteracting estrogen-driven endometrial proliferation and thickening cervical mucus.**
- Clinical uses: a required component of combined hormonal contraception (protects the endometrium from unopposed estrogen), progestin-only contraception, and management of abnormal uterine bleeding.
- Progestin-only methods — the "mini-pill," depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injection, the subdermal implant, and the levonorgestrel intrauterine system — avoid estrogen-related VTE risk entirely.
- **Adverse effects: irregular bleeding/spotting (especially early in use), mood changes, and — with DMPA specifically — reversible bone mineral density loss with prolonged use.**
- **Unopposed estrogen (estrogen without a progestin) in a patient with a uterus significantly increases endometrial cancer risk — this is why progestin-only or combined regimens, never estrogen alone, are used in patients with an intact uterus.**

## Slide 11: Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs)
- [heading] TISSUE-SELECTIVE ESTROGEN ACTION
- [heading] Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs)
- **Mechanism: bind the estrogen receptor, but act as an agonist in some tissues and an antagonist in others — the defining feature of this class.**
- Tamoxifen: ER antagonist in breast tissue (used for ER-positive breast cancer treatment and prevention) but an ER agonist in bone (preserves density) and, notably, in the endometrium.
- **Tamoxifen's endometrial agonist activity increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer — patients require counseling on abnormal bleeding and routine gynecologic follow-up.**
- Raloxifene: ER antagonist in breast tissue and ER agonist in bone, but — unlike tamoxifen — essentially neutral at the endometrium, making it a preferred option for osteoporosis prevention in postmenopausal women without a breast cancer indication.
- Both carry an increased VTE risk, similar in magnitude to estrogen therapy itself.

## Slide 15: Androgens & Antiandrogens
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 40
- [heading] Androgens & Antiandrogens
- **●  Testosterone (androgen):** used for male hypogonadism; adverse effects include erythrocytosis, hepatotoxicity (oral formulations), and suppression of endogenous testicular function/fertility. Monitor hematocrit and PSA with long-term use.
- **●  Spironolactone (antiandrogen):** blocks the androgen receptor (in addition to its aldosterone-antagonist diuretic effect from Unit 5) — used off-label for hirsutism and acne in women, especially in PCOS.

- **●  Finasteride / dutasteride (5-alpha-reductase inhibitors):** block conversion of testosterone to the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT); used for hirsutism and androgenetic alopecia here, and — as Session 2 will cover — for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

## Slide 20: PCOS: A Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap Condition
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 40 & 41
- [heading] PCOS: A Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap Condition
- **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is defined by a combination of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology — but its pharmacologic management is fundamentally a metabolic problem as much as a reproductive one.**
- Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia are central to PCOS pathophysiology in most patients, independent of body weight.
- Hyperinsulinemia stimulates ovarian theca-cell androgen production directly and lowers hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) production, raising free (biologically active) testosterone.
- Clinical features follow directly from this mechanism: hirsutism, acne, and anovulation/irregular cycles from hyperandrogenism; and an elevated long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome from the underlying insulin resistance.
- **This is why PCOS management routinely pulls drugs from Chapter 41 (antidiabetic agents) as well as Chapter 40 (hormonal agents) — the two chapters converge on the same patient.**

## Slide 22: Antidiabetic Agents in Reproductive-Metabolic Management
- [heading] FROM CHAPTER 41
- [heading] Antidiabetic Agents in Reproductive-Metabolic Management
- **Metformin: first-line insulin-sensitizing agent in PCOS management; improves insulin sensitivity, can restore ovulatory cycles, and is commonly used alongside or instead of hormonal therapy depending on the patient's goals (cycle regulation vs. fertility).**
- Metformin is not a direct hormonal agent — it improves hyperandrogenism indirectly, by reducing hyperinsulinemia's stimulatory effect on ovarian androgen production (Figure 7).
- **Adverse effects: GI upset (common, often transient), and rare but serious lactic acidosis risk in renal impairment — recall the renal dose-adjustment principles from Unit 5.**
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly used off-label in PCOS patients with obesity, given their weight-loss and insulin-sensitizing effects — an emerging extension of the same reproductive-metabolic overlap principle.
- Combined hormonal contraception (Section 1) remains first-line specifically for cycle regulation and hirsutism/acne when pregnancy is not desired — metformin and hormonal therapy are frequently used together, not as alternatives.
