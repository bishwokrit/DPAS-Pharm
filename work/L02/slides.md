## Slide 4: Why Do We Metabolize Drugs?
- [heading] PURPOSE
- [heading] Why Do We Metabolize Drugs?
- Inactivate an active drug, making it easier to excrete.
- Activate a prodrug into its active form.
- Produce an active metabolite that contributes to (or extends) the drug effect.
- **Occasionally produce a toxic metabolite — the basis of some drug-induced organ injury.**

## Slide 5: Phase I vs. Phase II Reactions
- [heading] TWO PHASES
- [heading] Phase I vs. Phase II Reactions
- [heading] Phase I — Functionalization
- [heading] Phase II — Conjugation
- Oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis
- Primarily carried out by cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes
- Often creates or unmasks a reactive functional group
- Glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation, methylation, glutathione conjugation
- Attaches a polar group to the molecule
- Produces a more water-soluble product for renal/biliary excretion

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Phase I often comes before Phase II, but not always — some drugs go straight to conjugation.

## Slide 6: CYP450 Drug Interactions
- [heading] CLINICALLY CRITICAL
- [heading] CYP450 Drug Interactions
- **●  Enzyme inhibition:** a drug slows a CYP enzyme, raising the blood level of any drug that CYP normally clears.
- **●  Enzyme induction:** a drug increases CYP enzyme production, lowering the blood level (and effect) of drugs that CYP clears.
- **Example: Drug A inhibits CYP3A4 → Drug B (a CYP3A4 substrate) accumulates → toxicity risk rises.**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: This is one of the highest-yield concepts in all of pharmacology — nearly every major drug class has at least one clinically important CYP interaction.

## Slide 14: Renal Elimination
- [heading] THE KIDNEY IN DETAIL
- [heading] Renal Elimination
- **●  Glomerular filtration:** free (unbound) drug passes from blood into the renal tubule based on molecular size.
- **●  Tubular secretion:** active transporters move additional drug from blood into the tubule.
- **●  Tubular reabsorption:** lipophilic, un-ionized drug can passively diffuse back into the blood, reducing net elimination.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Urine pH manipulation (e.g., alkalinizing urine in aspirin overdose) works by trapping ionized drug in the tubule, blocking reabsorption.

## Slide 15: Renal Disease and Drug Dosing
- [heading] CLINICAL CONNECTION
- [heading] Renal Disease and Drug Dosing
- **Decreased renal function → decreased drug clearance → increased drug exposure and accumulation risk.**
- eGFR and creatinine clearance (CrCl) are the standard bedside estimates of renal function used to adjust dosing.
- Any renally-cleared, narrow-therapeutic-index drug (think: many antibiotics, digoxin, lithium) needs a dose or interval adjustment in renal impairment.

## Slide 19: Half-Life (t½)
- [heading] CORE PARAMETER
- [heading] Half-Life (t½)
- **Half-life (t½) = the time required for the plasma drug concentration to fall by 50%.**
- Half-life is determined by clearance and volume of distribution together — it is not a fixed drug "property" independent of physiology.
- A drug is roughly 97% eliminated after about 5 half-lives.
- Example 2**:** Percentage-based half-life: Basic decay
- **Formula: % remaining = 100% x (1/2)^(number of half-life)**
- A drug has a half-life of 6 hours. A Patient receives an IV dose giving a starting plasma concentration of 100mg/L
- 1 half-life: 50% remains
- 2 half-life: 25% remains
- 3 half-life: 12.5% remains
- 4 half-life:  6.25% remains
- 5 half-life:  3.125% remains (estimated 97% “eliminated”
- Example 1: Basic decay:
- **Formula: C(t) = C** **o** **x (1/2)^(t/t** **1/2** **)**
- A drug has a half-life of 6 hours. A Patient receives an IV dose giving a starting plasma concentration of 100mg/L
- t = 6 h (1 half-life): 50mg/L (50% remains)
- t = 12 h (2 half-life): 25 mg/L (75% eliminated)
- t = 18 h (3 half-life): 12.5 mg/L (87.5% eliminated)
- t = 24 h (4 half-life): 6.25 mg/L  (93.75% eliminated)
- t = 30 h (5 half-life): 3.125 mg/L (97% eliminated)

## Slide 23: Steady State, Loading, and Maintenance Dosing
- [heading] DOSING IN PRACTICE
- [heading] Steady State, Loading, and Maintenance Dosing
- **●  Steady state:** the point at which drug intake equals drug elimination; reached in about 4–5 half-lives with regular dosing.
- **●  Loading dose:** a larger initial dose used to rapidly reach a therapeutic concentration, without waiting for steady state.
- **●  Maintenance dose:** the regular, ongoing dose that keeps the drug within the therapeutic range once steady state is reached.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Loading doses are based on Vd; maintenance doses are based on clearance — worth stating explicitly if students have seen the formulas.

## Slide 27: Clearance
- [heading] EFFICIENCY OF ELIMINATION
- [heading] Clearance
- **Clearance = the volume of plasma from which drug is completely removed per unit time.**
- Clearance determines how efficiently a drug is removed from the body — and, along with Vd, determines half-life.
- Total clearance is usually the sum of hepatic and renal clearance.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

- [heading] Renal Impairment and Drug Accumulation

## Slide 32: What Is Pharmacogenomics?
- [heading] DEFINITION
- [heading] What Is Pharmacogenomics?
- **Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation changes an individual’s response to a drug.**
- Genetic variation can alter:
  - Drug metabolism (e.g., CYP enzyme activity)
  - Drug transport across membranes
  - Drug target structure or expression
  - Overall drug response and risk of adverse reactions

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

- [heading] HIGH-YIELD EXAMPLES
- [heading] Pharmacogenomics in Practice
- [heading] CYP2D6 Variation
- [heading] CYP2C19 Variation

- Affects clopidogrel activation and some antidepressant/PPI dosing
- [heading] TPMT Variation
- [heading] HLA-Associated Reactions
- Deficiency raises toxicity risk with thiopurine drugs (e.g., azathioprine)

## Slide 41: Agonist or Antagonist?
- [heading] INTEGRATED VIGNETTE
- [heading] Agonist or Antagonist?
- A patient is given a drug that binds the same receptor as their home medication, but their home medication’s effect becomes weaker after the new drug is started.
- **Ask yourself:** **Is the new drug most likely a competitive antagonist, and how could you confirm it pharmacologically?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **A competitive antagonist can be overcome by increasing the agonist dose — if raising the home medication’s dose restores the effect, that supports competitive (rather than irreversible) antagonism.**
