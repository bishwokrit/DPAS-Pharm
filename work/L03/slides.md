## Slide 5: What Causes Musculoskeletal Pain?
- [heading] CLASSIFICATION
- [heading] What Causes Musculoskeletal Pain?
- [heading] By Mechanism
- [heading] By Time Course
- Nociceptive — direct activation of pain receptors by tissue injury
- Inflammatory — pain driven by inflammatory mediators
- Neuropathic — pain from nerve injury or dysfunction
- Acute — recent onset, often tied to identifiable injury or flare
- Chronic — persists beyond expected healing time, often > 3 months
- Treatment approach differs sharply between the two

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Most musculoskeletal pain in this lecture is a mix of nociceptive and inflammatory — pure neuropathic pain is more the territory of a separate pharmacology unit.

## Slide 6: Inflammation Drives Pain
- [heading] THE CASCADE
- [heading] Inflammation Drives Pain
- **Tissue injury → release of inflammatory mediators → sensitization of nociceptors → pain.**
- Key mediators include:
  - Prostaglandins
  - Bradykinin
  - Histamine
  - Cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6)

> Speaker notes: This mediator list is the roadmap for the whole lecture — NSAIDs target prostaglandins, biologic DMARDs target specific cytokines.

## Slide 10: Acetaminophen
- [heading] MECHANISM & EFFECTS
- [heading] Acetaminophen
- **●  Analgesic effect:** relieves mild-to-moderate pain, with a central mechanism that is still incompletely understood.
- **●  Antipyretic effect:** reduces fever via central effects on the hypothalamic temperature-regulating center.
- **●  Anti-inflammatory effect:** minimal at the periphery — unlike NSAIDs, acetaminophen is not a reliable anti-inflammatory agent.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: This is the single most important distinction students need: acetaminophen treats pain and fever well, but it is not an anti-inflammatory drug the way NSAIDs are.

## Slide 12: The Hidden Acetaminophen Overdose
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] The Hidden Acetaminophen Overdose
- A patient takes acetaminophen regularly for chronic back pain. For a cold, they also start an over-the-counter combination cold-and-flu medication.
- **Ask yourself:** **What is the hidden danger in this combination?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **Many combination cold, flu, and sleep products also contain acetaminophen — unintentional double-dosing is a leading cause of acetaminophen-related liver injury. Always ask about ALL sources of acetaminophen.**

## Slide 15: COX-1 vs. COX-2
- [heading] TWO ISOFORMS
- [heading] COX-1 vs. COX-2
- [heading] COX-1
- [heading] COX-2
- "Housekeeping" enzyme
- Maintains gastric mucosal protection and normal renal blood flow
- Inhibition contributes to GI and renal adverse effects
- Induced mainly at sites of inflammation
- Primary source of inflammatory prostaglandins
- Selective inhibition targets inflammation with less GI toxicity

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Stress this is a simplified teaching model, not an absolute separation — COX-1 and COX-2 functions overlap, and COX-2 is also constitutively expressed in the kidney and elsewhere.

## Slide 16: Nonselective NSAIDs
- [heading] AGENTS
- [heading] Nonselective NSAIDs
- **Nonselective NSAIDs inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2.**
- Ibuprofen — common first-line choice for mild-moderate pain
- Naproxen — longer half-life, twice-daily dosing
- Diclofenac — available in oral and topical formulations
- Indomethacin — more potent, more GI/CNS adverse effects
- **Ketorolac — short-term use only, significant GI/renal risk**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

## Slide 17: COX-2 Selective Inhibition: Celecoxib
- [heading] SELECTIVE AGENT
- [heading] COX-2 Selective Inhibition: Celecoxib
- **Celecoxib selectively inhibits COX-2 over COX-1.**
- GI effects: lower risk of ulcer/bleeding than nonselective NSAIDs.
- **Cardiovascular risk: COX-2 selectivity is associated with increased cardiovascular event risk — a key trade-off.**
- Renal effects: similar renal risk to nonselective NSAIDs — COX-2 selectivity does not protect the kidney.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: This is a classic "trade one risk for another" teaching point — better GI profile, but not a free lunch cardiovascularly or renally.

## Slide 20: NSAID Contraindications & Interactions
- [heading] PRESCRIBING SAFELY
- [heading] NSAID Contraindications & Interactions
- [heading] Use Caution / Avoid In
- [heading] High-Yield Drug Interactions
- Active GI bleeding or ulcer history
- Chronic kidney disease
- Heart failure or cardiovascular disease
- Anticoagulant therapy
- Late pregnancy
- NSAIDs + anticoagulants → ↑ bleeding risk
- NSAIDs + ACE inhibitors/ARBs → ↑ AKI risk ("triple whammy" with diuretics)
- NSAIDs + diuretics → reduced diuretic effect, ↑ renal risk
- NSAIDs + corticosteroids → ↑ GI ulcer risk

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: The "triple whammy" (NSAID + ACEi/ARB + diuretic) is a very high-yield acute kidney injury pattern to flag explicitly.

## Slide 21: Is an NSAID the Right First Choice?
- [heading] CLINICAL CASE
- [heading] Is an NSAID the Right First Choice?
- A 72-year-old patient with osteoarthritis also has chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and is taking an anticoagulant.
- **Ask yourself:** **Is an NSAID a good first-line choice for this patient’s pain? Why or why not?**
- **Clinical pearl:** **This patient has three separate NSAID red flags at once (renal disease, anticoagulation, and age-related bleeding risk) — acetaminophen or a non-NSAID strategy is generally safer first-line.**

## Slide 23: Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid Arthritis
- [heading] TWO DIFFERENT DISEASES
- [heading] Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid Arthritis
- [heading] Osteoarthritis (OA)
- [heading] Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)
- Primarily degenerative joint disease
- Cartilage breakdown with mechanical wear
- Treatment goal: pain control and function
- Autoimmune, systemic inflammatory disease
- Immune-mediated joint destruction
- Treatment goal: suppress disease activity, not just pain

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: This distinction is the hinge the whole rest of the section turns on: OA is managed primarily with analgesics/NSAIDs, while RA requires disease-modifying therapy.

## Slide 24: Pharmacologic Treatment of Osteoarthritis
- [heading] OA MANAGEMENT
- [heading] Pharmacologic Treatment of Osteoarthritis
- Acetaminophen — reasonable first-line for mild OA pain
- Oral NSAIDs — effective, but carry the systemic risks already discussed
- Topical NSAIDs — effective local relief with much lower systemic exposure
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injections — short-term relief for flares in a single joint
- Other approaches — activity modification, physical therapy, weight management

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

## Slide 25: What Makes a Drug "Disease-Modifying"?
- [heading] CONCEPT
- [heading] What Makes a Drug "Disease-Modifying"?
- **●  DMARD:** Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drug — a drug intended to alter the course of RA, not just mask its symptoms.
- **●  Goal of therapy:** slow or halt joint destruction and systemic inflammation, rather than providing analgesia alone.

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Contrast explicitly with NSAIDs: an NSAID can make a patient feel better without changing the underlying disease trajectory — a DMARD is meant to change the trajectory itself.

## Slide 26: Conventional (Non-Biologic) DMARDs
- [heading] CONVENTIONAL DMARDS
- [heading] Conventional (Non-Biologic) DMARDs
- **Methotrexate — the anchor drug for most RA regimens (next slide)**
- Hydroxychloroquine — milder disease; relatively favorable safety profile
- Sulfasalazine — often used in combination regimens
- Leflunomide — alternative when methotrexate is not tolerated

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

## Slide 27: Methotrexate
- [heading] ANCHOR DRUG
- [heading] Methotrexate
- **Mechanism: inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, reducing immune cell proliferation at low RA doses.**
- **Dosed weekly (not daily) — a critical patient-safety point, since daily dosing can be fatal.**
- Folate supplementation is routinely co-prescribed to reduce toxicity.
- Key adverse effects: hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, pulmonary toxicity.
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic).**

_[1 small image(s)/icons]_

> Speaker notes: Methotrexate’s weekly dosing is one of the highest-yield patient-safety teaching points in all of rheumatology pharmacology — emphasize it strongly.

## Slide 28: Biologic DMARDs
- [heading] TARGETED THERAPY
- [heading] Biologic DMARDs
- [heading] TNF Inhibitors
- [heading] IL-6 Inhibitors
- Block tumor necrosis factor-alpha signaling
- Examples: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab
- Block interleukin-6 receptor signaling
- Example: tocilizumab
- [heading] B-Cell Targeted Therapy
- [heading] T-Cell Costimulation Modulators
- Depletes or modulates B lymphocytes
- Example: rituximab
- Block the second signal needed for T-cell activation
- Example: abatacept

## Slide 30: DMARD Safety: Immunosuppression
- [heading] SHARED RISK
- [heading] DMARD Safety: Immunosuppression
- **The major safety theme across all DMARDs: immunosuppression → increased infection risk.**
- Baseline screening — e.g., tuberculosis and hepatitis B/C screening before starting biologics
- Vaccination — update vaccines, especially live vaccines, before starting therapy
- Ongoing monitoring — regular labs to catch cytopenias, hepatotoxicity, or infection early
- Infection precautions — counsel patients to report fevers or infection symptoms promptly
