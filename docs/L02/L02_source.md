## Topic: Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions
Learning objectives covered: SO1, IO1.2, IO1.4

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — Key definitions
- Drug metabolism = how the body chemically transforms drugs. (S3)
- Phase I — Functionalization: oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis. (S5)
- Phase II — Conjugation: glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation, methylation, glutathione conjugation. (S5)
- **Enzyme inhibition:** a drug slows a CYP enzyme, raising the blood level of any drug that CYP normally clears. (S6; SG U1-R10)
- **Enzyme induction:** a drug increases CYP enzyme production, lowering the blood level (and effect) of drugs that CYP clears. (S6; SG U1-R10)

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — How it works
- Why metabolize? To inactivate an active drug, making it easier to excrete. (S4)
- To activate a prodrug into its active form. (S4)
- To produce an active metabolite that contributes to (or extends) the drug effect. (S4)
- **Occasionally to produce a toxic metabolite — the basis of some drug-induced organ injury.** (S4)
- Phase I is primarily carried out by cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and often creates or unmasks a reactive functional group. (S5)
- Phase II attaches a polar group to the molecule, producing a more water-soluble product for renal/biliary excretion. (S5)
- Phase I often comes before Phase II, but not always — some drugs go straight to conjugation. (S5 (notes))
- Inhibition slows clearance (substrate level rises); induction speeds clearance (substrate level falls). (S6; SG U1-R10)
- [TB] CYP3A4 alone metabolizes over 50% of the prescription drugs metabolized by the liver. (p. 60)
- [TB] Acetaminophen is mostly conjugated (glucuronide/sulfate); in overdose glutathione is depleted and the toxic metabolite (NAPQI) injures the liver — **N-acetylcysteine** given within 8–16 hours protects against fulminant hepatotoxicity. (p. 66)

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — Clinical relevance
- CYP450 interactions are one of the highest-yield concepts in all of pharmacology — nearly every major drug class has at least one clinically important CYP interaction. (S6 (notes))
- **Example: Drug A inhibits CYP3A4 → Drug B (a CYP3A4 substrate) accumulates → toxicity risk rises.** (S6; SG U1-R10)
- Inhibition pushes a substrate's level ABOVE 100% of baseline (toxicity risk); induction pushes it BELOW 100% (therapeutic-failure risk). (S7 (notes))
- Inhibiting the CYP that clears a substrate is especially dangerous when the substrate has a narrow therapeutic index. (S11)
- Enzyme induction speeds substrate clearance, lowering its level and effect — the opposite of inhibition. (S48)
- [TB] Liver disease (e.g., cirrhosis, hepatitis) slows metabolism of some drugs enough that ordinary doses can cause coma. (p. 73)
- [TB] Severe ADRs contribute to about 100,000 US deaths a year and about 7% of hospital admissions. (p. 71)

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — Drugs named as examples
- NAPQI (from acetaminophen) is the textbook example of a toxic metabolite produced by normal Phase I metabolism — previewing the acetaminophen toxicity lecture. (S4 (notes))
- CYP3A4 — the enzyme in the lecture's inhibitor/substrate example. (S6)
- [TB] **Inducers:** rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, barbiturates, St. John's wort; **inhibitors:** ketoconazole, clarithromycin/erythromycin, ritonavir, grapefruit juice. (p. 63; p. 64)
- [TB] St. John's wort induces CYP3A4 and speeds metabolism of oral contraceptives, warfarin, cyclosporine, and others. (p. 72)

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — Exam traps
- Inhibitor → substrate level UP (toxicity); inducer → substrate level DOWN (therapeutic failure). Don't swap them. (S7 (notes); S9)
- CYP3A4 is a Phase I (CYP450) enzyme — it does not "only affect Phase II metabolism." (S5; S10-11)
- Not every drug goes through Phase I first — some go straight to Phase II conjugation. (S5 (notes))

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why does an inhibitor cause toxicity? The CYP enzyme that normally clears the substrate is slowed, so the substrate can no longer be cleared normally and accumulates. (S6; S9)
- Why it happens: Why does Phase II help excretion? Attaching a polar group makes the drug more water-soluble, so it can leave in urine or bile. (S5)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): **IN**hibitor = level goes **IN**to the danger zone (↑, toxicity). In**DU**cer = level goes **D**own (↓, failure). (S6; S7 (notes))
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Phase **I** = **I**nitial change (oxidize, reduce, hydrolyze — CYP450). Phase **II** = **T**ag it (conjugate a polar group → water-soluble). (S5)

### Drug Metabolism & CYP450 Interactions — High-yield summary
- Phase I (CYP450): oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis. Phase II: conjugation → water-soluble. (S5)
- **Inhibitor → substrate ↑ → toxicity**; **inducer → substrate ↓ → therapeutic failure**. (S6; S7 (notes))
- Narrow-TI substrate + CYP inhibitor = especially dangerous. (S11)
- Metabolism can produce toxic metabolites (e.g., NAPQI). (S4; S4 (notes))

## Topic: Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing
Learning objectives covered: SO2, IO1.2, IO1.3, IO1.5

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — Key definitions
- Drug excretion = clearing drug from the body. (S12)
- **Glomerular filtration:** free (unbound) drug passes from blood into the renal tubule based on molecular size. (S14)
- **Tubular secretion:** active transporters move additional drug from blood into the tubule. (S14)
- **Tubular reabsorption:** lipophilic, un-ionized drug can passively diffuse back into the blood, reducing net elimination. (S14)

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — How it works
- Kidney: the dominant route for most drugs and metabolites. (S13)
- Bile / feces: important for some large or protein-bound drugs. (S13)
- Lungs: volatile agents (e.g., anesthetic gases). (S13)
- Sweat / breast milk: minor routes, but breast milk matters for lactating patients. (S13)
- **Decreased renal function → decreased drug clearance → increased drug exposure and accumulation risk.** (S15)

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — Clinical relevance
- eGFR and creatinine clearance (CrCl) are the standard bedside estimates of renal function used to adjust dosing. (S15)
- Any renally-cleared, narrow-therapeutic-index drug (think: many antibiotics, digoxin, lithium) needs a dose or interval adjustment in renal impairment. (S15)
- For a primarily renally eliminated drug when eGFR drops: decrease the dose and/or extend the dosing interval, guided by eGFR/CrCl. (S16-17)
- Urine pH manipulation (e.g., alkalinizing urine in aspirin overdose) works by trapping ionized drug in the tubule, blocking reabsorption. (S14 (notes))
- This is the PK foundation for the renal-impairment case and the NSAID-in-CKD case later. (S15 (notes))

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — Drugs named as examples
- Renally-cleared, narrow-TI drugs: many antibiotics, digoxin, lithium. (S15)
- Aspirin overdose — urine alkalinization traps ionized drug. (S14 (notes))
- Anesthetic gases — eliminated by the lungs. (S13)

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — Exam traps
- Only FREE (unbound) drug is filtered at the glomerulus. (S14)
- Reabsorption REDUCES net elimination — and only lipophilic, un-ionized drug is reabsorbed. (S14)
- Renal impairment → lower dose and/or longer interval — NOT "no adjustment," a higher dose, or "IV only." (S16-17)

### Comparison: Three renal processes (S14)
| Process | Direction | How | Which drug |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glomerular filtration | Blood → tubule | Based on molecular size | Free (unbound) drug |
| Tubular secretion | Blood → tubule | Active transporters | Additional drug |
| Tubular reabsorption | Tubule → blood | Passive diffusion — **reduces net elimination** | Lipophilic, un-ionized drug |

### Comparison: Routes of drug elimination (S13)
| Route | Importance | Example / who it matters for |
|---|---|---|
| Kidney | **Dominant** for most drugs and metabolites | Not covered in materials |
| Bile / feces | Important for some | Large or protein-bound drugs |
| Lungs | Specific agents | Volatile agents (anesthetic gases) |
| Sweat / breast milk | Minor | Breast milk matters for lactating patients |

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why does alkalinizing urine help in aspirin overdose? It keeps the drug ionized in the tubule, and only un-ionized drug can diffuse back — so reabsorption is blocked and more drug leaves. (S14; S14 (notes))
- Why it happens: Why adjust doses in CKD? Less renal function → less clearance → more exposure and accumulation of renally-cleared drugs. (S15)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Kidney handling = "**F**ilter, **S**ecrete, **R**eabsorb" — **F**ree drug filtered, **S**ecreted by transporters, **R**eabsorbed if lipophilic & un-ionized. (S14)

### Drug Excretion & Renal Dosing — High-yield summary
- Kidney = dominant elimination route; filtration (free drug), secretion (transporters), reabsorption (lipophilic, un-ionized). (S13; S14)
- **↓ renal function → ↓ clearance → ↑ exposure** → lower dose and/or longer interval, guided by **eGFR/CrCl**. (S15; S17)
- Renally cleared narrow-TI drugs: many antibiotics, digoxin, lithium. (S15)

## Topic: PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance
Learning objectives covered: SO3, IO1.1, IO1.3

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — Key definitions
- **Half-life (t½) = the time required for the plasma drug concentration to fall by 50%.** (S19)
- **Steady state:** the point at which drug intake equals drug elimination; reached in about 4–5 half-lives with regular dosing. (S23; SG U1-R11)
- **Loading dose:** a larger initial dose used to rapidly reach a therapeutic concentration, without waiting for steady state. (S23)
- **Maintenance dose:** the regular, ongoing dose that keeps the drug within the therapeutic range once steady state is reached. (S23)
- **Clearance = the volume of plasma from which drug is completely removed per unit time.** (S27)
- [TB] **t½ = 0.7 × Vd / CL** — half-life rises when Vd rises or clearance falls. (p. 56)
- [TB] **Loading dose = Vd × target concentration** (e.g., theophylline: 35 L × 10 mg/L = 350 mg for a 70-kg adult). (p. 52)

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — How it works
- PK calculations = the math PAs actually use at the bedside. (S18)
- Half-life is determined by clearance and volume of distribution together — it is not a fixed drug "property" independent of physiology. (S19)
- A drug is roughly 97% eliminated after about 5 half-lives. (S19; SG U1-R11)
- **Formula: % remaining = 100% x (1/2)^(number of half-life)** — i.e., per number of half-lives elapsed. (S19)
- **Formula: C(t) = C₀ x (1/2)^(t/t½)** — C₀ = starting concentration, t = time elapsed. (S19)
- Worked example (both formulas): t½ = 6 h; an IV dose gives a starting plasma concentration of 100 mg/L — see the table for each half-life. (S19)
- Clearance determines how efficiently a drug is removed — and, along with Vd, determines half-life. (S27)
- Total clearance is usually the sum of hepatic and renal clearance. (S27)
- Loading doses are based on Vd; maintenance doses are based on clearance. (S23 (notes))
- Steady state takes ~4–5 half-lives regardless of dose size — the same reason a drug is ~97% eliminated ~5 half-lives after stopping it. (S30)
- Time to steady state (about 4–5 half-lives) is governed by half-life. (S48)
- [TB] During regular dosing: 50% of steady state after 1 half-life, 75% after 2, and over 90% after 4 — the textbook's "four half-lives" rule of thumb. (p. 47)
- [TB] If the dosing interval is shorter than four half-lives, accumulation will be detectable. (p. 48)
- [TB] **Capacity-limited (saturable) elimination:** clearance falls as concentration rises — important for phenytoin, ethanol, and aspirin. (p. 47)

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — Clinical relevance
- Case: significant CKD + a medication primarily eliminated unchanged by the kidney. **What happens to the drug's concentration and half-life, and would you adjust the dose, the dosing interval, or both?** (S28)
- **Clinical pearl:** reduced clearance raises drug concentration and prolongs half-life; many renally-cleared drugs are managed by extending the dosing interval, reducing the dose, or both, guided by eGFR/CrCl. (S28)
- The logic chain: clearance ↓ → half-life ↑ → time to steady state ↑ → accumulation risk ↑ if the regimen is not adjusted. (S28 (notes))
- Reduced renal clearance raises both drug concentration and half-life. (S48)
- A loading dose reaches the therapeutic target immediately instead of waiting ~4–5 half-lives; it does not change the maintenance dose or remove the need for monitoring. (S26)

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — Drugs named as examples
- Not covered in materials — this section uses illustrative drugs ("a drug with a half-life of 6 hours"). (S19)

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — Exam traps
- After 3 half-lives, 12.5% REMAINS (87.5% eliminated) — read whether the question asks remaining or eliminated. (S19; S22)
- Time to steady state depends on half-life — not on dose size, bioavailability, or route. (S30; S48)
- Half-life is not a fixed property — it changes with clearance and Vd (e.g., in renal impairment). (S19; S28)
- A loading dose does NOT permanently lower the maintenance dose or eliminate monitoring. (S26)

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why does CKD prolong half-life? Half-life depends on clearance (with Vd) — less renal clearance means the concentration takes longer to fall by 50%, so the drug accumulates. (S19; S27; S28 (notes))
- Why it happens: Why does a loading dose skip the wait? It is a larger first dose that reaches the therapeutic concentration right away instead of building up over ~4–5 half-lives. (S23; S26)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): "**Five to fill, five to empty**": ~4–5 half-lives to reach steady state; ~5 half-lives to be ~97% eliminated. (S19; S23; S30)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): **L**oading → **L**iters (Vd). **M**aintenance → **M**oving out (clearance). (S23 (notes))

### PK Calculations: Half-Life, Steady State & Clearance — High-yield summary
- **t½** = time for plasma concentration to fall 50%; set by **clearance + Vd**. (S19)
- Each t½ halves what remains: 50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25 → ~3% (~97% eliminated at 5 t½). (S19; S22)
- **Steady state ≈ 4–5 t½**, regardless of dose; loading dose gets there immediately. (S23; S26; S30)
- **Clearance** = plasma volume cleared per unit time; total = hepatic + renal. (S27)
- CKD: clearance ↓ → t½ ↑ → concentration ↑ → adjust dose and/or interval. (S28; S28 (notes))

## Topic: Pharmacogenomics
Learning objectives covered: SO4, IO1.3, IO1.4

### Pharmacogenomics — Key definitions
- **Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation changes an individual's response to a drug.** (S32)
- Why the same dose does not work the same way in everyone. (S31)

### Pharmacogenomics — How it works
- Genetic variation can alter drug metabolism (e.g., CYP enzyme activity). (S32)
- It can alter drug transport across membranes. (S32)
- It can alter drug target structure or expression. (S32)
- It can alter overall drug response and risk of adverse reactions. (S32)
- Personalized pharmacotherapy combines genotype, phenotype, disease state, and other medications into individualized therapy. (S34)
- **Pharmacogenomics is one input among several — genotype alone never dictates therapy in isolation.** (S34)
- [TB] Codeine is converted to morphine (200× more potent) by CYP2D6; CPIC recommends an alternative analgesic for CYP2D6 poor **and** ultrarapid metabolizers. (p. 81; p. 83)
- [TB] Clopidogrel: about 85% of a dose is inactivated by esterases; CYP2C19 activates the rest. CYP2C19 poor/intermediate metabolizers → prasugrel or ticagrelor. (p. 81; p. 82)
- [TB] TPMT intermediate activity: start thiopurines at 30–70% of the target dose; deficient: use a drastically reduced dose or an alternative. (p. 83)
- [TB] Warfarin dose varies up to 20-fold between patients; CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genotypes explain much of it. (p. 90)

### Pharmacogenomics — Clinical relevance
- CYP2D6 variation alters metabolism of many antidepressants and opioids (e.g., codeine activation). (S33; SG U1-R12)
- CYP2C19 variation affects clopidogrel activation and some antidepressant/PPI dosing. (S33)
- TPMT deficiency raises toxicity risk with thiopurine drugs (e.g., azathioprine). (S33; SG U1-R12)
- Certain HLA alleles sharply raise the risk of severe hypersensitivity to specific drugs. (S33)
- HLA-B*5801 is associated with severe hypersensitivity reactions to allopurinol — a textbook pharmacogenomic screening consideration (seen again in the gout lecture). (S36; S33 (notes); SG U1-R12)

### Pharmacogenomics — Drugs named as examples
- Codeine (CYP2D6), clopidogrel (CYP2C19), azathioprine (TPMT), allopurinol (HLA-B*5801); also antidepressants, opioids, PPIs. (S33; S36)
- [TB] CYP2D6 metabolizes up to one-quarter of clinically used drugs (β blockers, antidepressants, antipsychotics, opioids). (p. 78)
- [TB] CYP2D6 ultrarapid metabolizers: up to one-third of Ethiopians and Saudi Arabians; CYP2C19 poor metabolizers: 3–5% of Caucasians, 18–23% of Japanese. (p. 67)
- [TB] **HLA-B*57:01** → abacavir hypersensitivity; **G6PD deficiency** → rasburicase contraindicated; slow NAT2 acetylators (~50% in the USA) → isoniazid toxicity. (p. 70; p. 83; p. 88)

### Pharmacogenomics — Exam traps
- HLA-B*5801 before allopurinol = severe hypersensitivity risk — not absorption, not taste, and not irrelevant to drug selection. (S35-36)
- Genotype is one input — never the sole basis for therapy. (S34)

### Comparison: Pharmacogenomics in practice (S33; S36)
| Variant | What it changes | Drugs named |
|---|---|---|
| CYP2D6 variation | Metabolism (incl. codeine **activation**) | Many antidepressants, opioids (codeine) |
| CYP2C19 variation | Activation / dosing | Clopidogrel; some antidepressants, PPIs |
| TPMT deficiency | ↑ Toxicity risk | Thiopurines (azathioprine) |
| HLA alleles (HLA-B*5801) | ↑↑ Severe hypersensitivity risk | Allopurinol |

### Pharmacogenomics — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why can a CYP2D6 variant change codeine's effect? Codeine needs CYP2D6 activation — change the enzyme's activity and you change how much active drug is made. (S32; S33)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Gene → drug pairs: **2D6** → co**D**eine; **2C19** → **C**lopidogrel; **T**PMT → **T**hiopurines; **HLA-B*58** → **A**llopurinol (**A**llergy-like hypersensitivity). (S33; S36)

### Pharmacogenomics — High-yield summary
- Pharmacogenomics = genetic variation changing drug response (metabolism, transport, target, ADR risk). (S32)
- **HLA-B*5801 + allopurinol** → severe hypersensitivity risk. (S36)
- CYP2D6–codeine; CYP2C19–clopidogrel; TPMT–azathioprine. (S33)
- Genotype is **one input among several**. (S34)

## Topic: Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making
Learning objectives covered: SO5, IO1.3, IO1.4, IO1.5

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — Key definitions
- From concepts to clinical decisions: one integrated pharmacology pathway — Dose → Absorption → Distribution → Receptor / Target → Effect, with Metabolism and Excretion clearing the drug. (S37; S38)
- **Every topic in this lecture is a stop somewhere along this pathway — use it as your mental checklist when you evaluate any drug.** (S38)

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — How it works
- Before you prescribe — the clinical decision-making checklist: Indication — is there a clear reason to treat? (S39)
- Contraindications and allergies. (S39)
- Renal and hepatic function. (S39)
- Drug interactions (including CYP450). (S39)
- Age, pregnancy, and other patient-specific factors. (S39)
- Correct dose, route, and monitoring plan. (S39)
- Memorize this list — it is exactly the checklist applied in the MSK cases (NSAID in CKD, methotrexate in pregnancy, etc.). (S39 (notes))

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — Clinical relevance
- CYP interaction in action: a patient stable on a CYP3A4-substrate medication starts a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor. **What happens to the first drug's level, and what would you watch for clinically?** (S40)
- **Clinical pearl: always check for CYP450 interactions before adding a new drug to an existing regimen — one of the most preventable causes of toxicity.** (S40; S43)
- Agonist or antagonist? A new drug binds the same receptor as a home medication, and the home medication's effect weakens. **Is the new drug most likely a competitive antagonist, and how could you confirm it pharmacologically?** (S41)
- **Clinical pearl: a competitive antagonist can be overcome by increasing the agonist dose — if raising the home medication's dose restores the effect, that supports competitive (rather than irreversible) antagonism.** (S41)
- [SG] Study-guide case: 68-year-old woman, stage 4 CKD (eGFR 24 mL/min), starting a narrow-TI oral antiarrhythmic eliminated mainly unchanged by the kidney; she started a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor 3 weeks ago. (SG U1-S)
- [SG] Two concerns: reduced renal clearance from CKD will raise the antiarrhythmic's concentration and prolong its half-life regardless of the new drug; separately, if it has any hepatic/CYP3A4 component, the CYP3A4 inhibitor would compound accumulation further. (SG U1-S)
- [SG] Before dispensing: check eGFR/CrCl, consider a lower dose or extended interval, and closely monitor renal function and signs of toxicity (arrhythmia worsening, other drug-specific toxicity) after starting. (SG U1-S)
- [SG] If the drug were instead cleared by the liver via CYP3A4: the direct renal-clearance concern becomes less central (though CKD can still indirectly affect hepatic drug handling), and the CYP3A4 interaction becomes the dominant concern. (SG U1-S)

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — Drugs named as examples
- Not covered in materials — the integrated vignettes use unnamed drugs ("a CYP3A4-substrate medication," "a home medication"). (S40; S41)

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — Exam traps
- The single check most aimed at preventing a toxic, predictable drug-drug interaction is reviewing CYP450 interactions — not brand name, chemical name, or half-life alone. (S42-43)
- Weaker home-medication effect + restored by a higher dose = competitive; not restored = irreversible. (S41)
- Reviewing indication, contraindications, renal/hepatic function, interactions, and monitoring = the clinical decision-making framework — not pharmacogenomic testing or Phase IV surveillance. (S47-48)

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why check CYP450 before adding any drug? An inhibitor added to a stable regimen raises the other drug's level — predictable, so it is one of the most preventable causes of toxicity. (S6; S40)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Checklist "**I C R I A D**": **I**ndication · **C**ontraindications/allergies · **R**enal/hepatic · **I**nteractions (CYP450) · **A**ge/pregnancy · **D**ose, route, monitoring. (S39)

### Putting It All Together: Clinical Decision-Making — High-yield summary
- Memorize the checklist: indication, contraindications/allergies, renal/hepatic, interactions (CYP450), age/pregnancy, dose/route/monitoring. (S39; S39 (notes))
- **Always check CYP450 interactions before adding a new drug.** (S40)
- These five ideas are the toolkit for every drug class for the rest of the course, starting with musculoskeletal pharmacology. (S44 (notes))
