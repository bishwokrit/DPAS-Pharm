1Part 1 · Drug class

Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy

SO1SO4IO2.1IO2.2IO2.3IO2.4IO2.5

Class

  • Gout is a crystal-driven inflammatory arthritis with two very different treatment phases.S3
  • The chain: purine metabolism → uric acid → monosodium urate crystals → joint inflammation. Everything downstream — colchicine, allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid — targets a specific point along this chain.S4; S4 (notes)
  • Purine breakdown: hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid (via xanthine oxidase) → hyperuricemia → urate crystals in the joint.S5
  • Crystals activate neutrophils and macrophages (NLRP3, IL-1β) → the acute flare, the end of the cascade.S5
  • Raise urate: diets rich in purines and fructose; alcohol, especially beer (more production, less renal excretion).S5
  • Lower urate excretion: diuretics, especially thiazides and loop agents; chronic kidney disease sustains hyperuricemia.S5
  • Walk the full chain and sort the drugs into two buckets: “lowers uric acid” (allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid) vs. “treats the acute flare” (colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids) — the same acute-vs-chronic distinction made explicit next.S6; S6 (notes)
  • Acute flare — goal: control pain and inflammation now. Agents: colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids.S9
  • Chronic management — goal: lower serum uric acid to prevent future flares. Agents: allopurinol, febuxostat (↓ production); probenecid (↑ excretion).S9
  • TBHumans lack uricase, the enzyme other mammals use to turn uric acid into soluble allantoin; most people with hyperuricemia never have a gout attack.p. 695
  • TBTreat gout, not a number: long-term drug treatment of asymptomatic hyperuricemia is unproven. Adherence is poor (18–26% in younger patients).p. 695

Prototype + other drugs named

  • Colchicine — acute treatment (plant-derived alkaloid, no stem).S10; S19
  • Allopurinol — first-line urate-lowering therapy for most patients.S12
  • Febuxostat — alternative xanthine oxidase inhibitor.S12
  • Probenecid — uricosuric (second-line oral). Pegloticase, rasburicase — uricases (refractory).S14; S15

Mechanism of action

  • Mechanism: disrupts microtubule formation in neutrophils. Colchicine binds tubulin → less neutrophil migration and NLRP3 activation → less inflammation at the crystal site.S10; S11; SG U2-M
  • Colchicine works on the cell’s cytoskeleton, so its effect builds in the early hours of a flare.S11 (notes)
  • Allopurinol (via its metabolite oxypurinol) and febuxostat block xanthine oxidase → ↓ uric acid production.S12; S13; SG U2-R9
  • Febuxostat is also a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, with a different chemical structure.S12
  • Probenecid — mechanism: inhibits renal tubular reabsorption of uric acid, increasing its excretion (blocks URAT1). A uricosuric, not a xanthine oxidase inhibitor — a different strategy.S14; S15; SG U2-R9
  • Pegloticase, rasburicase: recombinant uricase converts uric acid to allantoin, which is soluble and excreted.S14; S15
  • Uricosurics block reabsorption; uricases remove uric acid enzymatically, which is why they suit refractory disease.S15 (notes)
  • NSAIDs and corticosteroids reduce joint inflammation in a flare.S6
  • TBAllopurinol is a purine analog; its metabolite oxypurinol (half-life 15 hours) also inhibits xanthine oxidase, so it is dosed once daily.p. 695
  • TBColchicine relieves gout pain and inflammation in 12–24 hours without changing urate metabolism or excretion.p. 696
  • TBGlucocorticoids in gout: reduce neutrophil migration, prostaglandins, and IL-1β.p. 698

Indications

  • Treat the acute flare first (colchicine, NSAID, or corticosteroid); urate-lowering therapy is deferred until the flare resolves, then used for long-term prevention.S16; SG U2-R10
  • Colchicine is most effective when started early in a flare — best within 24 hours of onset.S10; S11
  • Allopurinol is first-line for most patients; febuxostat is the alternative when allopurinol is not tolerated or contraindicated.S12
  • Probenecid is an older, second-line oral option. Pegloticase — a recombinant uricase enzyme for severe, refractory gout not controlled by other agents.S14; S14 (notes); S15
  • TBColchicine flare regimen: 1.2 mg, then 0.6 mg an hour later; prophylaxis 0.6 mg once or twice daily during urate-lowering therapy initiation.p. 696
  • TBCorticosteroids are a good alternative when NSAIDs or colchicine are contraindicated, including CKD: prednisone 30–50 mg/d for 1–2 days, tapered over 7–10 days, or intra-articular.p. 698
  • TBAll NSAIDs except aspirin, salicylates, and tolmetin treat gout flares.p. 697
  • TBFebuxostat appears safe in moderate CKD; it can be tried after allopurinol hypersensitivity, though a reaction may still occur.p. 697

Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)

  • Febuxostat: boxed warning for cardiovascular death — it carries its own cardiovascular safety considerations.S12; S13
  • Colchicine: narrow therapeutic window — GI adverse effects (diarrhea) are common and dose-limiting.S10; S11
  • Allopurinol: the HLA-B*58:01 allele raises the risk of severe hypersensitivity / severe cutaneous reactions.S12; S13
  • Pegloticase: infusion reactions are common; anti-drug antibodies can reduce response.S15
  • TBAllopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (SJS/TEN, fever, eosinophilia, hepatitis, renal failure) usually appears 2–4 weeks after starting — stop at the first rash.p. 696
  • TBColchicine can also cause marrow suppression, myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and neuritis; it is not removed by dialysis.p. 696
  • TBFebuxostat: liver function abnormalities, diarrhea, headache, nausea.p. 697

Contraindications

  • Do NOT start urate-lowering therapy during an acute flare — starting or changing it then can actually prolong the flare. Start or adjust only once the flare has resolved.S9; S9 (notes); SG U2-R10
  • Probenecid: avoid with a history of uric acid kidney stones or significant renal impairment; needs adequate hydration.S14; S15
  • Pegloticase and rasburicase: contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (hemolysis risk).S15
  • TBAspirin is avoided in gout: at low doses (≤2.6 g/d) it causes renal retention of uric acid.p. 697
  • TBUricosurics should not be used in patients who already excrete large amounts of uric acid.p. 698

Drug interactions

  • Colchicine + CYP3A4 / P-gp inhibitors (clarithromycin, cyclosporine, strong CYP3A4 inhibitors) → raised levels and toxicity.S11
  • Allopurinol + azathioprine or mercaptopurine: reduce the dose (xanthine oxidase also clears these drugs).S13
  • TBAllopurinol with azathioprine: cut the azathioprine dose by 75%.p. 696
  • TBProbenecid also reduces renal secretion of other weak acids, such as penicillin.p. 698

Pharmacokinetics

  • Colchicine: adjust the dose for renal or hepatic impairment.S11
  • Allopurinol: reduce the dose in renal impairment.S13
  • Pegloticase is given by IV infusion.S14
  • TBAllopurinol: start 100 mg/d (≤50 mg/d in CKD), titrate every 2–5 weeks to uric acid <6 mg/dL (max 800 mg/d).p. 695

Monitoring

  • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors: start low and titrate upward toward a serum urate target.S13
  • When starting urate-lowering therapy, give colchicine or an NSAID as prophylaxis for 3–6 months.S13
  • TBProbenecid: keep urine volume high and urine pH above 6.0 early on to prevent stones; start 2–3 weeks after an attack.p. 698
  • TBPegloticase: premedicate with a corticosteroid and antihistamine; flares are common in the first 3–6 months.p. 698
Clinical pearl / why
  • This acute-vs-chronic distinction is one of the single most testable concepts in gout pharmacology.S9 (notes)
  • Allopurinol vs. febuxostat: both sit on the same enzyme; the choice turns on safety profile and tolerability.S13 (notes)
  • HLA-B*5801 is a direct callback to Lecture 1’s pharmacogenomics section.S12 (notes)
  • Pegloticase is reserved for refractory cases given its cost, infusion-reaction risk, and need for specialty administration.S14
  • Case: sudden, severe pain, redness, and swelling of the first metatarsophalangeal joint — an acute gout flare. Is this the moment to start acute treatment, chronic urate-lowering therapy, or both? Acute treatment now; ULT later.S16
  • Name clues: suffixes often reveal the mechanism before you recall the drug — use them as memory hooks, then confirm each class before prescribing.S19; S19 (notes)
  • -ostat marks xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol is the exception); -ase names enzymes, so pegloticase and rasburicase are uricases; -sone, -olone mark corticosteroids used to treat flares.S19
  • TBAllopurinol already running when a flare hits? Don’t stop it — stopping can worsen and lengthen the attack.p. 696

Skeletal Muscle Relaxants

SO2SO4IO2.1IO2.2IO2.3IO2.5IO2.6

Class

  • Different mechanisms for spasm, spasticity, and rigidity — not the same thing, and they often require distinct pharmacologic approaches.S20; S21
  • Muscle spasm: sudden, involuntary contraction, often from acute musculoskeletal injury; typically treated with centrally acting relaxants.S21
  • Spasticity: velocity-dependent increased tone from upper motor neuron injury. Rigidity: constant increased tone, independent of velocity.S21
  • Where tone is controlled: upper motor neurons (descending inhibitory pathways) ▸ spinal interneurons (GABA, glycine, alpha-2) ▸ alpha motor neuron ▸ neuromuscular junction (ACh release) ▸ muscle fiber contraction (SR Ca²⁺ release).S22
  • Centrally acting (act within the CNS to reduce muscle tone): cyclobenzaprine, tizanidine, baclofen — first-line; carisoprodol, methocarbamol, metaxalone, chlorzoxazone — other options.S23
  • Peripherally / locally acting: dantrolene acts directly on skeletal muscle itself; botulinum toxin acts at the neuromuscular junction — useful when a central mechanism is not desired (or not safe).S23
  • Highest-yield distinction: “where does the drug act?” sorts every agent into one of two buckets — dantrolene is the only one in the peripheral (muscle) bucket.S24; S24 (notes); SG U2-R11
  • TBSpasticity is common after spinal injury, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, and stroke; these drugs relieve painful spasm better than they restore function.p. 510; p. 511

Prototype + other drugs named

  • Baclofen — GABA-B agonist (spinal cord).S27
  • Tizanidine — central α2-adrenergic agonist.S29
  • Cyclobenzaprine — tricyclic-like central relaxant (brainstem).S31
  • Dantrolene — RyR1 blocker in the muscle fiber.S34

Mechanism of action

  • Central agents reduce CNS excitatory output to motor neurons → ↓ muscle tone. Baclofen and tizanidine act on spinal interneurons and afferents; cyclobenzaprine acts in the brainstem.S22; S24; SG U2-R11
  • Baclofen — mechanism: GABA-B receptor agonist in the spinal cord. Binding GABA-B → opens K⁺ channels, closes Ca²⁺ channels (presynaptic inhibition) → less excitatory transmitter release → lower motor neuron firing → reduced spasticity.S27; S28; SG U2-M
  • Tizanidine — mechanism: central α2-adrenergic agonist. Activating alpha-2 receptors → reduced presynaptic release of excitatory amino acids → lower motor neuron activity → reduced muscle tone.S29; S30
  • Cyclobenzaprine: acts in the brainstem; antagonizes 5-HT2 and descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways → reduced tonic somatic motor activity → muscle relaxation. Structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants.S31; S32
  • Dantrolene — mechanism: decreases calcium release from the skeletal muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum. Nerve impulse → DHPR activated → RyR1 opens → Ca²⁺ released → contraction; dantrolene blocks RyR1 → less Ca²⁺ → relaxation.S34; S35; S22; SG U2-R11; SG U2-M
  • Dantrolene acts inside the fiber on RyR1 — directly on the muscle fiber, not the CNS — distinct from every centrally acting agent.S34; S22
  • Botulinum toxin — blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction (ACh release at the junction) — a third mechanism, distinct from both CNS-acting drugs and dantrolene’s sarcoplasmic-reticulum action.S33; S33 (notes); S22
  • TBDiazepam (GABA-A) reduces spasticity partly in the spinal cord, but sedates at the doses needed.p. 511
  • TBDantrolene binds RyR1; cardiac and smooth muscle use RyR2, so they are minimally depressed.p. 513

Indications

  • Baclofen: spasticity, particularly from spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis; intrathecal option for severe spasticity.S27; S28
  • Cyclobenzaprine — primary use: short-term treatment of acute, localized muscle spasm; short-term use only, 2–3 weeks.S31; S32
  • Dantrolene — clinical use #1: malignant hyperthermia (a true emergency indication) (triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine) — a true emergency, perioperative indication where the drug is literally life-saving.S34; S34 (notes); S35; SG U2-R11
  • Dantrolene #2: chronic spasticity, when central agents are not appropriate; also used for neuroleptic malignant syndrome. IV formulation for acute crisis.S34; S35
  • Botulinum toxin: focal spasticity or dystonia; effect is local and temporary, requiring repeat injections.S33
  • TBMalignant hyperthermia: give IV dantrolene 1 mg/kg, repeated as needed, while controlling acidosis and temperature.p. 513
  • TBCyclobenzaprine is ineffective for spasm from cerebral palsy or spinal cord injury.p. 513
  • TBGabapentin has shown promise as a spasmolytic in multiple sclerosis.p. 512

Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)

  • TBBotulinum toxin: black box warning for distant spread of toxin, greatest in children treated for spasticity.p. 513
  • Baclofen: CNS depression — sedation and weakness, additive risk with other CNS depressants.S27; S28
  • Baclofen withdrawal: abrupt discontinuation (especially intrathecal) can cause a dangerous syndrome — seizures, hyperthermia, rebound spasticity.S27; S28
  • Tizanidine: sedation, hypotension and bradycardia, dry mouth, hepatotoxicity.S29; S30
  • Cyclobenzaprine: prominent sedation (commonly dosed at night); anticholinergic effects — dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion.S31; S32
  • Dantrolene: hepatotoxicity; generalized weakness.S35
  • Carisoprodol — metabolized to meprobamate; Schedule IV controlled substance with dependence/misuse potential.S33
  • Chlorzoxazone: rare but notable hepatotoxicity risk. Methocarbamol is generally less sedating than cyclobenzaprine; metaxalone has a relatively favorable sedation profile.S33
  • TBTizanidine causes hypotension in 16–33%; abrupt withdrawal causes rebound hypertension and tachycardia.p. 512
  • TBHigh-dose baclofen: somnolence, respiratory depression, coma; more seizures in epilepsy.p. 512
  • TBCarisoprodol withdrawal after long use: anxiety, tremor, twitching, insomnia, hallucinations.p. 511

Contraindications

  • Cyclobenzaprine: use caution in older adults (Beers list); MAOIs contraindicated.S31; S32
  • Tizanidine with ciprofloxacin or fluvoxamine is contraindicated (CYP1A2).S30

Drug interactions

  • Tizanidine + CYP1A2 inhibitors (ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine) → strongly raised levels; additive effect with antihypertensives.S30
  • Cyclobenzaprine: serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol; MAOIs contraindicated.S32
  • Baclofen: additive sedation with other CNS depressants.S27

Pharmacokinetics

  • Baclofen: renal dose adjustment.S28
  • TBTizanidine: start 2 mg every 6–8 hours, max 36 mg/d; adjust for hepatic or renal impairment.p. 512
  • TBDantrolene: only about one-third of an oral dose is absorbed; half-life about 8 hours.p. 513

Monitoring

  • Tizanidine requires liver function monitoring (LFTs) with regular use.S29; S30

Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis

SO3SO4IO2.1IO2.2IO2.3IO2.4IO2.5IO2.6

Class

  • Bone pharmacology is about balancing bone formation and bone resorption.S39; S67
  • Osteoblasts build new bone matrix (promoted by several osteoporosis therapies); osteoclasts resorb existing bone (suppressed by most osteoporosis therapies).S40
  • Nearly every osteoporosis drug shifts this balance one way or the other — slowing osteoclasts or stimulating osteoblasts.S40 (notes)
  • RANKL/OPG: osteoblasts or stromal cells express RANKL → RANKL binds RANK on osteoclast precursors → osteoclast differentiation and activation → bone resorption releases calcium.S41
  • OPG is a decoy receptor that blocks RANKL. Estrogen increases OPG; denosumab mimics OPG. Sclerostin inhibits Wnt signaling in osteoblasts.S41
  • PTH: intermittent dosing favors formation; continuous exposure raises RANKL and resorption.S41
  • Calcium homeostasis: PTH raises serum calcium (mobilizes bone calcium when levels drop); vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption; calcitonin lowers serum calcium and opposes PTH; calcium is the substrate being regulated (intake, bone storage, excretion).S42
  • PTH feedback loop: falling serum calcium → PTH → raises calcium via bone resorption, renal reabsorption, and (via vitamin D activation) intestinal absorption; restored calcium inhibits further PTH.S43; SG U2-R12
  • Risk factors: aging; estrogen deficiency (e.g., postmenopausal status); chronic glucocorticoid use; low calcium or vitamin D intake; low physical activity.S46
  • TBAbout 98% of the body’s calcium is in bone; PTH, FGF23, and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D are the principal regulators.p. 811; p. 812
  • TBExcess endogenous PTH nets bone resorption, but low intermittent doses build bone first — the basis for teriparatide.p. 815
  • TBGlucocorticoids block vitamin D–driven gut calcium transport, raise renal calcium loss, block formation, and initially stimulate resorption.p. 818

Prototype + other drugs named

  • Alendronate — oral bisphosphonate (first-line).S47
  • Denosumab — RANKL monoclonal antibody.S54
  • Teriparatide — PTH analog (anabolic).S54
  • Raloxifene — SERM; romosozumab — sclerostin antibody.S54

Mechanism of action

  • Bisphosphonates — mechanism: inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. They bind hydroxyapatite at resorption sites → osteoclasts take them up → nitrogen-containing agents inhibit FPP synthase (mevalonate pathway) → loss of prenylation of small GTPases → osteoclast apoptosis.S47; S48
  • Denosumab: monoclonal antibody, RANKL inhibitor — blocks osteoclast formation.S54; S55; SG U2-M
  • Teriparatide (PTH 1-34, daily injection) / abaloparatide: PTH-analog “anabolic” agents that stimulate osteoblasts and new bone formation.S54; S55; SG U2-M
  • Romosozumab: sclerostin antibody that both builds bone and limits resorption — increases formation, decreases resorption → BMD up, fracture risk down.S54; S55
  • SERMs — selective estrogen receptor modulators (raloxifene): estrogen-like effect on bone — antiresorptive, like calcitonin.S54; S55
  • Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis: glucocorticoids increase osteoclast-mediated resorption and suppress osteoblast-driven formation.S71
  • TBAmino-bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate) inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway.p. 820
  • TBDenosumab does not kill osteoclasts and does not deposit in bone, so its effect reverses when stopped.p. 820

Indications

  • Bisphosphonates are first-line for most patients with osteoporosis.S47
  • Higher-risk patients may need an anabolic agent first.S59
  • Raloxifene also reduces breast cancer risk.S54; S55
  • TBRaloxifene protects against spine fractures but not hip fractures; bisphosphonates, denosumab, and teriparatide protect both.p. 827
  • TBVitamin D (800 IU/d) with calcium improves bone density, reduces falls, and prevents fractures — the base under every osteoporosis drug.p. 827
  • TBCalcitonin is less effective (spine fractures only); bisphosphonates and calcitonin are first-line for Paget disease.p. 828; p. 829

Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)

  • Romosozumab: boxed warning for MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death.S55
  • TBRomosozumab: black box — contraindicated after MI or stroke within the past year.p. 820
  • Bisphosphonates: esophageal irritation; hypocalcemia; osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare); atypical femoral fractures (rare, with long-term use).S48; S52
  • IV zoledronic acid: acute-phase reaction (flu-like), mitigated with acetaminophen.S53
  • Raloxifene: raises VTE risk; hot flashes.S55
  • Denosumab discontinuation causes rebound vertebral fractures — transition to a bisphosphonate.S55
  • TBOsteonecrosis of the jaw is rare at osteoporosis doses (~1/100,000 patient-years), more common with high-dose IV zoledronate for cancer.p. 820
  • TBDenosumab: possible infection risk, transient hypocalcemia (CKD, vitamin D deficiency), and rebound resorption if interrupted.p. 820

Contraindications

  • Bisphosphonates: contraindicated with severe renal impairment (CrCl below 30–35 mL/min) — some are avoided in significant renal impairment.S48; S59
  • Oral bisphosphonates: avoid in esophageal stricture, achalasia, or inability to sit upright.S53
  • TBUnlike bisphosphonates, denosumab can be used in advanced renal disease (not renally cleared) — watch calcium.p. 820

Drug interactions

  • Food, calcium, coffee, and antacids reduce oral bisphosphonate absorption further.S53

Pharmacokinetics

  • Bisphosphonates have a long skeletal half-life — the effect persists after stopping.S48
  • Poor oral absorption (under 1%) → take fasting with plain water.S53
  • Dosing: alendronate, risedronate — oral, typically weekly; ibandronate — oral, monthly; zoledronic acid — IV, as infrequently as once yearly.S47; S49
  • Teriparatide is limited to 2 years lifetime, then follow with an antiresorptive.S55
  • TBBisphosphonates: less than 10% of an oral dose is absorbed; about half goes to bone, the rest is excreted unchanged in urine.p. 819
  • TBDenosumab 60 mg SC every 6 months; teriparatide 20 mcg SC daily; romosozumab 210 mg SC monthly for one year, then an antiresorptive.p. 819; p. 827

Monitoring

  • Oral counseling: take with a full glass of water, first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach well before food, and remain upright (sitting or standing) for the specified period after dosing (30 minutes).S52; S53; SG U2-R13
  • Ensure calcium and vitamin D repletion before starting; correct hypocalcemia first.S48; S53
  • Consider a drug holiday after 3–5 years of oral therapy (osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femur fracture with long-term use).S48
  • TBReassess after 5 years of bisphosphonate (3 for zoledronate); a drug holiday if fracture risk allows.p. 820

  • Teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab are the anabolic exception — they actively build new bone; most other osteoporosis drugs just slow resorption.S54 (notes); S56
  • Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis is especially high-yield — it connects to the corticosteroid use for OA/RA in Session 1.S46 (notes)
  • Choosing therapy: fracture risk, renal function, previous fracture history, glucocorticoid exposure, adherence (weekly oral vs. yearly IV vs. injectable), and route preference. This checklist is a direct echo of Lecture 1’s clinical decision-making framework.S59; S59 (notes)

Lecture check 1 · S7-8
In Figure 4, probenecid and allopurinol both lower serum uric acid, but through different mechanisms. What is the key difference?
Answer: A
Figure 4 shows allopurinol (and febuxostat) acting as xanthine oxidase inhibitors that reduce uric acid production, while probenecid acts at the kidney to increase renal excretion of uric acid that has already been produced — two different strategies for the same goal of lowering serum uric acid.S7-8
Lecture check 2 · S17-18
A patient presents with an acute gout flare. Which action is most appropriate at this visit?
Answer: A
Starting or changing urate-lowering therapy during an acute flare can worsen or prolong it. Treat the flare first; urate-lowering therapy is started or adjusted only once it resolves.S17-18
Lecture check 3 · S25-26
In Figure 5, which agent is the odd one out, acting directly on the skeletal muscle fiber rather than within the central nervous system?
Answer: D
Dantrolene decreases calcium release from the skeletal muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum, acting directly on the muscle fiber — unlike cyclobenzaprine, tizanidine, and baclofen, which all act within the CNS to reduce excitatory output to motor neurons.S25-26
Lecture check 4 · S36-37
Which skeletal muscle relaxant acts directly on skeletal muscle itself (not the CNS), making it the agent of choice in malignant hyperthermia?
Answer: B
Dantrolene’s peripheral mechanism — decreasing calcium release from the skeletal muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum — is unique among these agents and is exactly why it is the emergency treatment for malignant hyperthermia.S36-37
Lecture check 5 · S44-45
In Figure 6, PTH release raises serum calcium through three parallel actions. Which organ system is NOT one of the three direct targets of PTH shown in the diagram?

PTH acts directly on bone (↑ osteoclast resorption) and kidney (↑ calcium reabsorption, activates vitamin D); its effect on the intestine is indirect, mediated through activated vitamin D. The lung is not part of this feedback loop at all.S44-45

Lecture check 7 · S57-58
In Figure 8, teriparatide and romosozumab sit on the "bone formation" side of the balance, while alendronate and raloxifene sit on the "bone resorption" side. What does this grouping represent?

The "anabolic" agents (teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab) sit on the bone-formation side because they stimulate osteoblasts, while bisphosphonates (alendronate), denosumab, SERMs (raloxifene), and calcitonin sit on the bone-resorption side because they inhibit osteoclasts — two different strategies for shifting the same remodeling balance.S57-58
