## Topic: Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion
Learning objectives covered: SO1, IO3.1

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — Key definitions
- Acid-peptic disease is **a balance between mucosal defense and acid-driven aggression**. (S4)
- **Aggressive factors:** gastric acid (HCl), pepsin, H. pylori infection, NSAID use (suppresses protective prostaglandins). (S5; SG U3-R1)
- **Protective (mucosal defense) factors:** mucus–bicarbonate layer; prostaglandins (stimulate mucus/bicarbonate, maintain mucosal blood flow); epithelial cell turnover. (S5; SG U3-R1)
- **GERD:** reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus, causing heartburn/regurgitation and, over time, esophageal mucosal injury. (S7)
- **Peptic ulcer disease (PUD):** a break in the mucosa of the stomach or duodenum; most commonly caused by H. pylori infection or NSAID use. (S7)
- **Gastritis:** inflammation of the gastric mucosa — acute (e.g., NSAID- or alcohol-related) or chronic (e.g., H. pylori-driven). (S7)
- [TB] Over 90% of peptic ulcers are caused by H. pylori infection or NSAIDs; bile also counts as an aggressive factor. (p. 1153)

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — How it works
- Disease appears when aggressive factors win or defenses fall: aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, H. pylori, NSAIDs) overwhelm mucus, bicarbonate and prostaglandin-driven blood flow → mucosal injury → GERD, ulcer or gastritis. (S5 (notes); S6)
- **NSAIDs block COX-1 prostaglandins**, removing mucosal protection — the clearest example of tipping the balance. (S5 (notes); S6)
- **H. pylori urease makes ammonia**, which injures the mucosa. (S6)
- **Stress and critical illness raise the risk of stress ulceration.** (S6)
- **Gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison) causes severe acid hypersecretion.** (S6)
- **Gastrin, histamine, and acetylcholine all converge on the parietal cell proton pump.** H2 blockers cut one input (histamine); PPIs block the last step itself. (S8; S8 (notes))
- [TB] **Physiology of acid secretion:** gastrin acts mainly by releasing histamine from ECL cells; acetylcholine (M3) stimulates the parietal cell directly; somatostatin from antral D cells turns gastrin off. (p. 1154)

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — Clinical relevance
- GERD, PUD and gastritis share the same drug toolkit: reduce acid, protect the mucosa, and/or eradicate H. pylori. (S7 (notes))
- H. pylori and NSAIDs cause most PUD. (S7 (notes))

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — Drugs named as examples
- Figure 1 is the conceptual spine of the antisecretory section: be able to explain why PPIs are more complete acid suppressors than H2RAs from the diagram alone. (S8 (notes))

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — Exam traps
- H2RAs block only the histamine input; gastrin and acetylcholine still drive the pump — PPIs block the final common pathway regardless of which input is active. (S8; SG U3-R2)

### Acid-Peptic Disease & Gastric Acid Secretion — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why do NSAIDs cause ulcers? They block the COX-1 prostaglandins that make mucus and bicarbonate and keep mucosal blood flow up — they take the defense away. (S5 (notes); S6)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Three keys, one lock: **gastrin, histamine, ACh** all turn the pump; PPIs change the lock. (S8)

## Drug class: Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO2, SO3, SO5, IO3.1, IO3.2, IO3.3, IO3.5

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Class
- Turning down acid production, or shielding the mucosa from it. (S11)
- **Antacids** neutralize acid already made; **H2 blockers** block the histamine input; **PPIs** block the final common pathway — the most potent acid suppression. (S12; S13; S15; S22)
- Antacids are the oldest and most “patient-directed” class — a contrast to the receptor- and enzyme-targeted agents. (S12 (notes))

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Prototype + other drugs named
- Omeprazole — PPI prototype (esomeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, dexlansoprazole). (S15)
- Famotidine — most commonly used H2 blocker; cimetidine the CYP-inhibiting one. (S13)
- Calcium carbonate, magnesium hydroxide, aluminum hydroxide — antacids. (S12)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Mechanism of action
- **Antacids — mechanism: directly neutralize existing gastric acid (do not reduce acid production).** **Acid in stomach** → **antacid base (Al/Mg hydroxide, calcium carbonate) neutralizes H+** → **higher gastric pH** → rapid but short relief. (S12)
- **H2RAs — mechanism: competitively block histamine at the H2 receptor on parietal cells.** Histamine from ECL cells → H2 receptor → cAMP rises → stimulates the proton pump; famotidine occupies the receptor competitively, so less acid. (S13; S14)
- **PPIs — mechanism: irreversibly inhibit the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase proton pump — the final common pathway of acid secretion.** (S15; SG U3-R2)
- **PPI prodrug activated by acid** → **binds H⁺/K⁺ ATPase irreversibly** → **final step of acid secretion blocked**. (S15)
- Pathway: enteric-coated prodrug absorbed in the small intestine → reaches the parietal cell canaliculus → activated by acid → binds a cysteine on H⁺/K⁺-ATPase covalently → **pump irreversibly blocked until new pumps are made**. (S16; S16 (notes))
- PPIs achieve substantially greater acid-suppression potency and longer duration of effect than H2RAs (Figure 4: potency 95 vs. 45; duration 90 vs. 50, illustrative). (S17; S17 (notes))
- [TB] H2 blockers inhibit 60–70% of 24-hour acid secretion; PPIs inhibit 90–98% because they block the final common pathway. (p. 1156; p. 1159)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Indications
- **Antacids:** onset is rapid, but duration is short — best for intermittent, mild symptom relief. (S12)
- **H2RAs:** mild-to-moderate GERD, maintenance therapy, adjunct for peptic ulcer healing; best at controlling **nocturnal** acid. (S13; S14)
- **PPIs:** first-line for GERD, PUD healing, and H. pylori regimens. (S15)
- [TB] Infrequent heartburn (fewer than three times per week): antacids or as-needed H2 blockers; antacids act faster but last 1–2 hours vs. 6–10 hours. (p. 1156)
- [TB] PPIs heal over 90% of duodenal ulcers within 4 weeks; GERD recurs in over 80% within 6 months of stopping. (p. 1159)
- [TB] Stress-related bleeding prophylaxis in high-risk ICU patients: an oral PPI if enteral meds are possible, otherwise IV H2 blocker or PPI. (p. 1157)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **PPI long-term adverse effects: vitamin B12 deficiency, hypomagnesemia, increased fracture risk, and increased risk of Clostridioides difficile infection** — also pneumonia and acute interstitial nephritis. (S15; S16; SG U3-R3)
- **Abrupt PPI discontinuation after prolonged use can cause rebound acid hypersecretion.** (S15)
- **Aluminum hydroxide — constipating. Magnesium hydroxide — can cause diarrhea; use caution in renal impairment (magnesium accumulation). Calcium carbonate — can cause constipation; risk of milk-alkali syndrome with excessive use.** (S12)
- **Cimetidine:** antiandrogenic effects (gynecomastia) with prolonged use. (S13; S14)
- **Famotidine:** confusion risk in older adults with renal impairment. (S13 (notes); S14)
- **H2RAs: tolerance (tachyphylaxis) can develop with continuous use**, reducing effectiveness over time. (S13; S14)
- [TB] PPIs carry an FDA warning for hip, spine and wrist fractures and for life-threatening hypomagnesemia; 2–3-fold more C. difficile infection. (p. 1160)
- [TB] Sodium bicarbonate causes belching and metabolic alkalosis; excess calcium carbonate with dairy causes hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency and alkalosis (milk-alkali). (p. 1155)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Contraindications
- PPIs: contraindicated with rilpivirine (an HIV drug that needs stomach acid). (S46; S71)
- [TB] Magnesium and aluminum antacids are renally excreted: avoid long-term use in renal insufficiency. (p. 1155)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Drug interactions
- **Antacids can impair absorption of many other oral drugs — separate dosing by at least 2 hours.** They bind levothyroxine, quinolones, tetracyclines, and iron. (S12; S53; S71)
- **Omeprazole (a CYP2C19 inhibitor) can reduce clopidogrel activation, blunting its antiplatelet effect.** Omeprazole and esomeprazole both inhibit CYP2C19. (S15; S16; SG U3-R4)
- **Cimetidine is a potent CYP450 inhibitor (1A2, 2C19, 3A4)** — a callback to Lecture 1: it raises warfarin, phenytoin, and theophylline levels. (S13; S13 (notes); S14; S52)
- Famotidine has minimal CYP450 interaction. (S13)
- [TB] Antacids should not be given within 2 hours of tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, itraconazole, and iron. (p. 1155)
- [TB] With clopidogrel, prefer pantoprazole or rabeprazole (minimal CYP2C19 inhibition). (p. 1161)
- [TB] Less acid lowers absorption of ketoconazole, itraconazole, digoxin, and atazanavir. (p. 1161)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Pharmacokinetics
- **PPIs are best absorbed/effective when taken 30–60 minutes before a meal** (timed to peak pump activity) — they work best on actively secreting pumps. (S15; S16)
- CYP2C19 genotype alters PPI exposure; ultrarapid metabolizers may need higher doses. (S15 (notes); S16)
- Famotidine needs renal dose adjustment. (S13 (notes); S14)
- [TB] PPIs: food cuts bioavailability by about 50%; half-life about 1.5 hours yet acid inhibition lasts up to 24 hours; full effect takes 3–4 days. (p. 1158)
- [TB] PPIs need no renal dose reduction; consider reduction in severe liver impairment. (p. 1159)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Monitoring
- Many patients stay on PPIs far longer than necessary — the long-term adverse-effect list drives deprescribing conversations. (S15 (notes))

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Ask: what happens to warfarin or phenytoin levels if cimetidine is added? They rise (CYP inhibition). (S13 (notes))
- Name stems: **-prazole** PPIs (irreversibly block H+/K+ ATPase); **-tidine** H2 receptor antagonists (famotidine, ranitidine, cimetidine; competitively block the H2 receptor). (S28)

- Schematic (illustrative): ECL cell releases histamine → binds the H2 receptor on the parietal cell → cAMP rises → H+/K+ ATPase pumps move H+ into the gastric lumen. Famotidine blocks the H2 receptor, so less acid is secreted. (S14; S14 (notes))

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why before breakfast? PPIs only hit pumps that are actively secreting; a meal switches pumps on, so the drug should be peaking when they turn on. (S15; S16)
- Why it happens: Why does PPI use cause B12 deficiency? Acid frees B12 from food protein; less acid, less B12 released. (SG U3-R3)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Antacid bowel effects: **Al**uminum **al**ways constipates; **Mg** = **m**ore **g**oing (diarrhea). (S12)

### Antacids, H2 Blockers & Proton Pump Inhibitors — High-yield summary
- PPIs block the parietal cell’s final common pathway and are the most potent antisecretory agents, but carry real long-term risks (B12 deficiency, hypomagnesemia, fracture, C. difficile). (S44; S15)
- Antacids: fast, short, don’t reduce production; separate other drugs by 2 hours. (S12)
- Cimetidine: CYP inhibitor + gynecomastia; famotidine: renal dosing, minimal CYP. (S13; S14)
- Omeprazole + clopidogrel: CYP2C19 inhibition blunts activation. (S15; SG U3-R4)

## Drug class: Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO2, SO4, SO5, IO3.1, IO3.2, IO3.4, IO3.6

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Class
- Shielding the mucosa (sucralfate, misoprostol, bismuth) and treating the cause (H. pylori eradication). (S11; S20; S23)
- **H. pylori is the leading identifiable cause of peptic ulcer disease — eradication, not just acid suppression, is the goal.** (S23; S24 (notes))

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Prototype + other drugs named
- Sucralfate — barrier over the ulcer base. (S20)
- Misoprostol — PGE1 analog for NSAID-ulcer prevention. (S20)
- Bismuth quadruple and PPI-based triple therapy for H. pylori. (S23)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Mechanism of action
- **Sucralfate:** in acid (pH below 4) it polymerizes, binds the ulcer base and **forms a protective barrier**; requires an acidic environment to activate; minimal systemic absorption. (S20; S21; SG U3-R5)
- **Misoprostol:** **a prostaglandin E1 analog** — acts on EP receptors to reduce acid and increase mucus and bicarbonate, **restoring the protective prostaglandin tone that NSAIDs suppress**. (S20; S21; SG U3-R5)
- **Why H. pylori needs multiple drugs:** it lives in gastric mucus and buffers acid with urease-made ammonia. (S24)
- A PPI raises gastric pH so bacteria enter a replicating state and antibiotics are more stable; amoxicillin blocks cell wall synthesis; clarithromycin (50S ribosome) or metronidazole adds a second antibiotic. (S24; S24 (notes))
- [TB] Sucralfate is sucrose complexed to sulfated aluminum hydroxide; less than 3% is absorbed. (p. 1161)
- [TB] Bismuth coats ulcers, has direct antimicrobial effects and binds enterotoxins (traveler’s diarrhea). (p. 1162)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Indications
- **Triple therapy:** PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin for 10–14 days (usual duration 14 days). (S23; S23 (notes))
- **Bismuth quadruple therapy:** PPI (or H2RA) + bismuth subsalicylate + metronidazole + tetracycline — preferred where clarithromycin resistance is a concern, with recent macrolide exposure, or with penicillin allergy. (S23; S23 (notes); S24; SG U3-R6)
- **Penicillin allergy:** substitute metronidazole for amoxicillin in triple therapy. (S23)
- Misoprostol is used specifically to prevent NSAID-induced ulcers. (S20)
- [TB] Misoprostol cuts NSAID-induced ulcers to less than 3% and ulcer complications by 50%, but dosing 3–4 times daily and diarrhea limit use. (p. 1162)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Misoprostol: contraindicated in pregnancy — potent uterotonic/abortifacient effect** (boxed warning). Common adverse effects: diarrhea and cramping. (S20; S21; S57; SG U3-R5)
- Sucralfate: constipation is common; aluminum accumulates in CKD. (S20 (notes); S21)
- [TB] Bismuth blackens stool (can mimic GI bleeding) and darkens the tongue; avoid in renal insufficiency. (p. 1162)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Contraindications
- Avoid tetracycline in pregnancy and in children. (S23 (notes); S24)
- Penicillin allergy rules out amoxicillin in H. pylori regimens. (S23; S71)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Drug interactions
- **Separate sucralfate from other drugs by 2 hours** (give on an empty stomach, separated from antacids/PPIs). (S20; S21)
- **Clarithromycin inhibits CYP3A4** — must not be combined with simvastatin, lovastatin, or ergotamine. (S23 (notes); S24; S71)
- **Metronidazole plus alcohol causes a disulfiram-like reaction.** (S23 (notes); S24; S71)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Pharmacokinetics
- Sucralfate has minimal systemic absorption. (S20)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Monitoring
- **Confirm eradication with testing (urea breath test or stool antigen) after completing therapy** — at least 4 weeks after, with the PPI held for 2 weeks (PPIs cause false negatives). (S23; S23 (notes); S24)
- Counsel on completing a 10–14 day, 3–4 drug regimen — multi-drug regimens are a classic adherence problem. (S23 (notes))
- [TB] After antibiotics, continue the PPI once daily for a total of 4–6 weeks to complete ulcer healing. (p. 1159)

### Mucosal Protectants & H. pylori Eradication — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: confirmed H. pylori, penicillin allergy, and a macrolide two months ago. **Which eradication regimen is more appropriate for this patient, and why?** (S25)
- Answer: bismuth quadruple therapy — recent macrolide exposure raises clarithromycin-resistance concern and the allergy rules out standard triple therapy. (S25; S25 (notes); SG U3-S)
- Misoprostol’s pregnancy contraindication links to its other use (labor induction/medical abortion) — mechanism explains the contraindication. (S20 (notes))
- Name stems: **-prostol** prostaglandin analogs (misoprostol); **-cillin** penicillins (amoxicillin; blocks cell wall synthesis); **-thromycin** macrolides (clarithromycin; -mycin alone is not reliable, e.g., gentamicin); sucralfate and bismuth subsalicylate share no stem. (S28; S28 (notes))

## Drug class: Antiemetics
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO3, SO4, SO5, IO3.1, IO3.2, IO3.3, IO3.4, IO3.6

### Antiemetics — Class
- **Four receptor systems feed one final common reflex.** (S29)
- Four inputs converge on the **vomiting center in the medulla**: **gut and vagal (5-HT3)**, the **chemoreceptor trigger zone (D2, 5-HT3, NK1)**, the **vestibular (H1, M1)** system, and **higher cortical (anxiety, anticipatory)** input — benzodiazepines and cannabinoids. (S32; S32 (notes))
- **Motion sickness is vestibular** — H1/M1 blockers (meclizine, scopolamine) fit it. (S33 (notes); SG U3-R7)
- Matching each drug to its input pathway (Figure 9) is the single most testable concept in this section. (S33 (notes))

### Antiemetics — Prototype + other drugs named
- Ondansetron — 5-HT3 antagonist. (S30)
- Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine — D2 antagonists. (S30)
- Aprepitant — NK1 antagonist. (S30)
- Meclizine, scopolamine — H1/M1 antagonists. (S30)
- Dronabinol, nabilone — cannabinoids (CB1). (S38)

### Antiemetics — Mechanism of action
- **5-HT3 antagonists** block serotonin at gut vagal afferents/CTZ. (S30)
- Chemotherapy or gut irritation damages enterochromaffin cells → serotonin binds 5-HT3 on vagal afferents and the CTZ → signal to the vomiting center; **ondansetron blocks 5-HT3, so no signal reaches the vomiting center**. (S30; S31; S31 (notes))
- **D2 antagonists** block dopamine at the CTZ (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine). (S30)
- **NK1 antagonists** block substance P/neurokinin at the CTZ (aprepitant). (S30)
- **H1/M1 antagonists** block histamine/acetylcholine from the vestibular system (meclizine, scopolamine). (S30)
- **Metoclopramide — mechanism: D2 antagonism (antiemetic effect) plus enhancement of upper-GI motility (prokinetic effect).** D2 blockade in the CTZ → less nausea; D2 blockade plus **5-HT4 agonism** in the gut → more acetylcholine → faster gastric emptying. (S36; S37; S37 (notes))
- Dronabinol (CB1 agonist) crosses the blood-brain barrier and activates CB1 receptors in the vomiting center, CTZ and hypothalamus, reducing neurotransmitter release → less nausea, more appetite. (S38; S39; S39 (notes))
- Mechanism is distinct from the four pathways (5-HT3, D2, NK1, H1/M1). (S38; S39; S39 (notes); SG U3-M)
- [TB] **Pathophysiology of vomiting:** the vomiting center sits in the lateral medullary reticular formation; the CTZ (area postrema) lies outside the blood-brain barrier. (p. 1169)

### Antiemetics — Indications
- Ondansetron: give before chemotherapy; oral ODT and IV forms are available. (S31)
- **Metoclopramide clinical uses: nausea/vomiting and diabetic gastroparesis.** (S36)
- **Dronabinol — a synthetic cannabinoid (CB1 receptor agonist) used for refractory chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting and appetite stimulation.** Typically reserved for patients who do not respond adequately to standard antiemetics. (S38; S38 (notes))
- **Chemotherapy:** combine classes with different targets — a 5-HT3 antagonist, an NK1 antagonist, and dexamethasone; avoid duplicating the same mechanism. (S32; S32 (notes))
- **Match drug to cause:** gut/vagal triggers respond to 5-HT3 antagonists; the CTZ to D2 or NK1 antagonists. (S32)
- [TB] 5-HT3 antagonists control vagal and chemotherapy emesis but poorly control motion sickness; palonosetron also prevents delayed emesis. (p. 1170)
- [TB] **Pharmacokinetics & pharmacodynamics (5-HT3):** first-generation agents have half-lives of 4–9 hours; palonosetron 40 hours; no dose cut in renal insufficiency. (p. 1170)
- [TB] NK1 antagonist + 5-HT3 antagonist + dexamethasone prevents acute emesis in 80–90% of highly emetogenic chemotherapy patients. (p. 1171)
- [TB] Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, diazepam) prevent anticipatory vomiting before chemotherapy. (p. 1172)

### Antiemetics — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- Metoclopramide — **boxed warning: risk of tardive dyskinesia, particularly with prolonged use or high doses — limit duration of therapy.** Avoid beyond 12 weeks. (S36; S37; SG U3-R8)
- **Other extrapyramidal symptoms (acute dystonia, akathisia) can also occur**; acute dystonia is treated with diphenhydramine or benztropine. (S36; S37; S71)
- **Ondansetron: QT prolongation** — caution with other QT-prolonging drugs and electrolyte problems. **Common effects: constipation, headache; serotonin syndrome risk with serotonergic drugs.** (S31)
- **Cannabinoids: sedation, dizziness, mood/perception changes** (euphoria or dysphoria), tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension — counsel patients accordingly. (S38; S38 (notes); S39)
- [TB] Metoclopramide: restlessness, drowsiness and agitation in 10–20%; extrapyramidal effects in 25% at high doses; prolactin rises (galactorrhea, gynecomastia). (p. 1164)
- [TB] Droperidol prolongs the QT interval (rare fatal torsades) — only if alternatives fail. (p. 1172)

### Antiemetics — Contraindications
- **Metoclopramide: contraindicated in GI obstruction; avoid in Parkinson disease.** (S36 (notes); S37)

### Antiemetics — Drug interactions
- Ondansetron: serotonin syndrome risk with other serotonergic drugs; QT risk with other QT-prolonging drugs. (S31)
- Cannabinoids: **additive CNS depression with sedatives and alcohol**. (S38 (notes); S39)
- [TB] Aprepitant is a CYP3A4 substrate/inhibitor: lowers INR with warfarin; dexamethasone dose is often reduced with it. (p. 1171)

### Antiemetics — Pharmacokinetics
- **CYP2D6 ultrarapid metabolizers may respond less well to ondansetron.** (S31)
- **Metoclopramide: renal dosing needed; CYP2D6 poor metabolizers need lower doses** (higher exposure). (S36 (notes); S37)

### Antiemetics — Monitoring
- **Dronabinol is Schedule III; nabilone is Schedule II.** Use with caution in older adults. (S38 (notes); S39)
- Metoclopramide duration matters — many students remember only that it helps nausea. (S36 (notes))

### Antiemetics — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: first-trimester nausea and vomiting not helped by dietary changes. **Which antiemetic class has the most established safety profile for use in this patient?** (S40)
- Pyridoxine (vitamin B6), often with doxylamine, is typically first-line; ondansetron is commonly used too, though first-trimester data continue to evolve — weigh maternal benefit against fetal risk and consult current guidance. (S40; S40 (notes); S71)
- Pyridoxine is a vitamin — a preview of Session 2’s vitamins and supplements. (S40 (notes))
- Cannabinoid pharmacology is an expanding area in the newest Katzung edition. (S38 (notes))
