## Drug class: Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception
Learning objectives covered: SO1, IO6.1, IO6.2, IO6.4, IO6.6

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Class
- **The two core gonadal steroid classes, and how they are combined for contraception and hormone therapy.** (S4)
- **Figure 2. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): GnRH drives FSH/LH release, which drives gonadal sex-steroid production, which in turn negatively feeds back on the hypothalamus and pituitary —** (S8)
- **with four distinct classes of drugs intervening at different points along this axis.** This is the organizing map for the session: GnRH agonists/antagonists, SERMs, aromatase inhibitors and antiandrogens each anchor to a point on it. (S8; S8 (notes))

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Prototype + other drugs named
- Estradiol and ethinyl estradiol (estrogens); norethindrone, medroxyprogesterone, levonorgestrel (progestins); combined oral contraceptives. (S5; S6; S26)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: bind intracellular estrogen receptors (ER-alpha, ER-beta), regulating gene transcription in reproductive, bone, cardiovascular, and CNS tissue.** (S5; S4 (notes); SG U6-R1)
- Estrogen physiologic effects: endometrial proliferation, breast development, bone density maintenance, favorable lipid profile effects (↑ HDL, ↓ LDL). (S5; SG U6-R1)
- **Mechanism: bind progesterone receptors, counteracting estrogen-driven endometrial proliferation and thickening cervical mucus.** (S6)
- Unopposed estrogen chain (Figure 1): estrogen binds ER in the endometrium → proliferation and thickening of the lining → without progestin: hyperplasia and cancer risk → progestin binds PR: limits proliferation, matures the endometrium. (S7; S7 (notes))
- Unopposed estrogen drives unchecked endometrial proliferation; a progestin counteracts this proliferation. (SG U6-R2)
- Combined methods (oral pills, transdermal patch, vaginal ring) suppress ovulation via negative feedback on FSH/LH. (S9)
- [TB] Estrogens raise hepatic clotting factors II, VII, IX and X and lower antithrombin III — the basis of the clotting risk; transdermal or vaginal routes avoid first-pass liver exposure. (p. 761; p. 762)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Indications
- Estrogen clinical uses: hormonal contraception (combined with a progestin), menopausal hormone therapy for vasomotor symptoms, and hypogonadism. (S5)
- Progestins: a required component of combined hormonal contraception (protects the endometrium from unopposed estrogen), progestin-only contraception, and management of abnormal uterine bleeding. (S6)
- Progestin-only methods — the "mini-pill," depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injection, the subdermal implant, and the levonorgestrel intrauterine system — avoid estrogen-related VTE risk entirely. (S6; S9; SG U6-R4)
- Combined methods are often chosen for added benefits: cycle regulation, acne/hirsutism improvement. (S9)
- When estrogen is contraindicated, a progestin-only method is not a compromise — it is often the correct first choice (safe with VTE history, migraine with aura, or smokers over 35). (S9; S9 (notes))
- [TB] Postmenopausal hormonal therapy: vasomotor symptoms, genital atrophy and bone loss; after hysterectomy estrogen alone is fine because no progestin is needed. (p. 762; p. 763)
- [TB] An implantable progestin preparation (subdermal implant) provides long-acting progestin-only contraception. (p. 768)
- [TB] Contraception with progestins alone suits patients with hepatic disease, hypertension, or prior thromboembolism. (p. 773)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Adverse effects: venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk, increased risk with smoking and age over 35, nausea, breast tenderness, and a possible increase in certain hormone-sensitive cancer risks with long-term use.** (S5)
- Estrogen’s VTE risk is the single most tested adverse effect: know the risk factors (smoking, age > 35, personal/family VTE history) that shift a patient from "candidate" to "contraindicated." (S5 (notes))
- **Adverse effects: irregular bleeding/spotting (especially early in use), mood changes, and — with DMPA specifically — reversible bone mineral density loss with prolonged use.** (S6; S9; SG U6-R4)
- **Unopposed estrogen (estrogen without a progestin) in a patient with a uterus significantly increases endometrial cancer risk — this is why progestin-only or combined regimens, never estrogen alone, are used in patients with an intact uterus.** (S6; SG U6-R2)
- [TB] Oral contraceptive VTE risk tracks the estrogen, not the progestin; factor V Leiden and other inherited clotting disorders markedly raise it. (p. 772)
- [TB] Estrogen therapy also increases migraine, cholestasis, gallbladder disease and hypertension; oral contraceptives raise hepatic adenoma risk. (p. 764; p. 772)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Contraindications
- Estrogen contraindications: history of VTE, estrogen-sensitive breast cancer, uncontrolled hypertension, and smokers over age 35 — all raise cardiovascular/thrombotic risk substantially. Smoking combined with age over 35 makes a progestin-only method (no estrogen, no added VTE risk) the appropriate choice. (S5; S5 (notes); SG U6-R3)
- Combined methods carry estrogen-related VTE risk — require screening for contraindications. Hysterectomy patients can take estrogen alone. (S7; S9)
- [TB] Contraindications & cautions: thrombophlebitis or past thromboembolism, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, estrogen-dependent cancer, undiagnosed genital bleeding, liver disease. (p. 764; p. 773)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Drug interactions
- Enzyme inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John’s wort) lower combined contraceptive efficacy. (S45; S45 (notes))
- [TB] Rifampin and phenytoin speed estrogen/progestin metabolism, and antibiotics that disturb gut flora may cut enterohepatic recycling — both can cause contraceptive failure. (p. 771; p. 773)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Pharmacokinetics
- Not covered in materials.

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Monitoring
- Progestin-only methods have more irregular bleeding patterns, especially initially; DMPA specifically: reversible bone density loss with long-term use. (S9)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The "unopposed estrogen" point is a classic exam item: any patient with a uterus receiving estrogen needs a progestin too, unless she has had a hysterectomy. (S6 (notes); S7 (notes))
- [TB] Beneficial effects of oral contraceptives: lower risk of endometrial and ovarian cancer, ovarian cysts and benign breast disease; lifetime breast cancer risk unchanged. (p. 773; p. 774)
- [TB] Mifepristone (RU-486) blocks progesterone and glucocorticoid receptors; 600 mg with misoprostol ends early pregnancy in over 95%. (p. 775)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why a progestin with estrogen when the uterus is intact? Estrogen alone keeps the lining growing — hyperplasia, then cancer; the progestin matures it. (S6; S7)

### Estrogens, Progestins & Contraception — High-yield summary
- Estrogen = VTE (smoking, age > 35, VTE history); unopposed estrogen = endometrial cancer → always pair with a progestin if the uterus is intact. (S5; S6)
- Estrogen contraindicated → progestin-only is the right first choice; DMPA = reversible bone loss. (S6; S9)

## Drug class: SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens
Learning objectives covered: SO2, SO3, IO6.1, IO6.2, IO6.3, IO6.5

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Class
- **Tissue-selective and blocking strategies — how drugs can turn hormone signaling on in one tissue and off in another.** (S10)
- A receptor-blocking strategy and a synthesis/activation-blocking strategy exist on both the estrogen and androgen sides. (S17; S17 (notes))

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Prototype + other drugs named
- Tamoxifen, raloxifene (SERMs); anastrozole, letrozole (aromatase inhibitors); testosterone; spironolactone, flutamide, bicalutamide (antiandrogens); finasteride, dutasteride (5-ARIs). (S11; S14; S15)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: bind the estrogen receptor, but act as an agonist in some tissues and an antagonist in others — the defining feature of this class.** Same receptor, different conformation and co-regulators in each tissue. (S11; S12 (notes); SG U6-R5)
- Tamoxifen: ER antagonist in breast tissue but an ER agonist in bone (preserves density) and, notably, in the endometrium. Tamoxifen antagonizes breast and agonizes bone and endometrium. (S11; S12; S10 (notes))
- Raloxifene: ER antagonist in breast and ER agonist in bone, but — unlike tamoxifen — essentially neutral at the endometrium; tamoxifen is an ER agonist at the endometrium (raising endometrial cancer risk), both being ER antagonists in breast. (S11; S12; SG U6-R5)
- Aromatase chain (Figure 5): androgens (androstenedione, testosterone) → aromatase in fat tissue converts them to estrogens → estrogens drive ER-positive tumor growth → anastrozole and letrozole block aromatase. (S14)
- **Figure 4. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): SERMs block the estrogen receptor directly in breast tissue (while remaining agonists elsewhere), while aromatase inhibitors block the peripheral enzymatic conversion of androgens to estrogen —** (S13)
- **two distinct strategies converging on the same clinical goal in hormone-sensitive breast cancer.** Receptor blockade versus synthesis blockade. (S13; S13 (notes))
- Androgen pathway (Figure 6): testosterone (circulating androgen) → 5-alpha-reductase converts it to DHT (more potent) → binds the androgen receptor → hair follicle and prostate effects. (S16; S15 (notes))
- Finasteride and dutasteride block 5-alpha-reductase, lowering DHT; spironolactone, flutamide and bicalutamide block the androgen receptor. Blocking the enzyme or the receptor both reduce androgen action — different mechanisms grouped under the same broad "antiandrogen" label. (S16; S16 (notes); SG U6-R8)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Indications
- Tamoxifen: ER-positive breast cancer treatment and prevention. Raloxifene: preferred option for osteoporosis prevention in postmenopausal women without a breast cancer indication. (S11)
- Aromatase inhibitors block peripheral enzymatic conversion of androgens to estrogen rather than acting at the receptor — postmenopausal use only: before menopause the ovaries are the dominant estrogen source (often combined with ovarian suppression). (S13 (notes); S14; S14 (notes); SG U6-R6)
- **●  Testosterone (androgen):** used for male hypogonadism. (S15)
- **●  Spironolactone (antiandrogen):** blocks the androgen receptor (in addition to its aldosterone-antagonist diuretic effect from Unit 5) — used off-label for hirsutism and acne in women, especially in PCOS. (S15)
- **●  Flutamide / bicalutamide (antiandrogens):** pure androgen receptor blockers used primarily in prostate cancer, often combined with a GnRH agonist (leuprolide). (S15; S45)
- **●  Finasteride / dutasteride (5-alpha-reductase inhibitors):** block conversion of testosterone to the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT); used for hirsutism and androgenetic alopecia here, and — in Session 2 — for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). (S15; S15 (notes))
- Estrogen-pathway drugs are used primarily in hormone-sensitive breast cancer; androgen-pathway drugs for hirsutism, prostate cancer, alopecia, and BPH. (S17)
- [TB] Tamoxifen as adjuvant therapy cuts contralateral breast cancer by 35%; benefit does not grow beyond 5 years. (p. 774)
- [TB] Clomiphene, a partial estrogen agonist, blocks hypothalamic feedback to trigger ovulation (mostly in PCOS); multiple pregnancy about 10%. (p. 776; p. 777)
- [TB] Other inhibitors: danazol suppresses the midcycle LH/FSH surge (endometriosis; hepatic caution); exemestane is an irreversible aromatase inhibitor. (p. 775; p. 776)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Tamoxifen’s endometrial agonist activity increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer — patients require counseling on abnormal bleeding and routine gynecologic follow-up.** (S11)
- Both SERMs carry an increased VTE risk, similar in magnitude to estrogen therapy itself. (S11; S12)
- Aromatase inhibitor adverse effects: bone loss and joint pain. (S14; S14 (notes))
- Testosterone: erythrocytosis, hepatotoxicity (oral formulations), and suppression of endogenous testicular function/fertility. (S15)
- [TB] Androgens & anabolic steroids: masculinization in women, polycythemia; 17-alkylated oral androgens cause cholestasis, peliosis hepatis and liver tumors. (p. 779; p. 780)
- [TB] Flutamide causes gynecomastia and occasional reversible hepatotoxicity; bicalutamide is given once daily with a GnRH analog to prevent tumor flare. (p. 782)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Contraindications
- Spironolactone: avoid in pregnancy (teratogenic risk). (S24)
- [TB] Androgens are contraindicated in pregnant women or women who may become pregnant (fetal masculinization). (p. 780)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Drug interactions
- CYP2D6 inhibitors (paroxetine, fluoxetine) blunt tamoxifen activation. Testosterone and tamoxifen raise INR with warfarin. (S45; S45 (notes))
- Bisphosphonates and calcium with vitamin D protect bone with aromatase inhibitors. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and potassium: hyperkalemia with spironolactone. (S45; S45 (notes))

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Androgen suppression: continuous (not pulsatile) GnRH analogs such as leuprolide suppress the gonads; dutasteride has a much longer half-life than finasteride. (p. 781)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Monitoring
- Testosterone therapy requires monitoring hematocrit (erythrocytosis risk) and PSA with long-term use, with additional risks of hepatotoxicity (oral formulations) and suppression of endogenous fertility. (S15; SG U6-R7)

### SERMs, Aromatase Inhibitors & Androgens — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The tamoxifen-vs-raloxifene endometrial distinction is a classic high-yield comparison: tamoxifen carries the endometrial cancer risk because of tissue-selective agonism, not a class effect. (S11 (notes); S12 (notes))
- Finasteride/dutasteride are a direct bridge to Session 2 — the same drugs reappear for BPH (prostate growth rather than hirsutism). (S15 (notes))
- Case: a 52-year-old postmenopausal woman with ER-positive breast cancer starts tamoxifen; six months later, new-onset irregular vaginal bleeding. **What is the most likely explanation, and what should happen next?** (S18)
- **Tamoxifen acts as an estrogen receptor agonist in the endometrium even while acting as an antagonist in breast tissue — this tissue-selective agonism increases the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer.** (S18)
- **New abnormal bleeding on tamoxifen warrants prompt gynecologic evaluation, not reassurance.** A natural contrast with raloxifene’s neutral endometrial profile. (S18; S18 (notes))

## Drug class: PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap
Learning objectives covered: SO4, SO5, IO6.1, IO6.3, IO6.4, IO6.5

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Class
- **Where Chapter 40 and Chapter 41 meet — insulin resistance as a driver of reproductive endocrine disease.** (S19)
- **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is defined by a combination of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology — but its pharmacologic management is fundamentally a metabolic problem as much as a reproductive one.** (S20)
- **This is why PCOS management routinely pulls drugs from Chapter 41 (antidiabetic agents) as well as Chapter 40 (hormonal agents) — the two chapters converge on the same patient.** PCOS pharmacology is not purely "gynecologic." (S20; S20 (notes))

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Prototype + other drugs named
- Metformin; combined hormonal contraception; spironolactone; GLP-1 receptor agonists. (S24)

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Mechanism of action
- Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia are central to PCOS pathophysiology in most patients, independent of body weight. (S20)
- Hyperinsulinemia stimulates ovarian theca-cell androgen production directly and lowers hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) production, raising free (biologically active) testosterone — the mechanistic core of PCOS. (S20; S19 (notes); SG U6-R9)
- **Figure 7. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism reinforce one another in a self-sustaining cycle — hyperinsulinemia raises free testosterone, and hyperandrogenism worsens insulin resistance,** (S21)
- **explaining why metabolic therapy improves reproductive symptoms and vice versa.** Walk both arrows of the cycle. (S21; S21 (notes))
- Metformin is not a direct hormonal agent — it improves hyperandrogenism indirectly, by reducing hyperinsulinemia’s stimulatory effect on ovarian androgen production (Figure 7) — by improving insulin sensitivity, not by acting on any hormone receptor. (S22; SG U6-R10)
- Chain (Figure 8): insulin resistance → high insulin → ovarian androgens up; liver SHBG down → free testosterone up → metformin lowers insulin → androgens fall; cycles and ovulation can return. (S23; S23 (notes))

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Indications
- Clinical features follow the mechanism: hirsutism, acne, and anovulation/irregular cycles from hyperandrogenism; an elevated long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome from insulin resistance. (S20)
- **Metformin: first-line insulin-sensitizing agent in PCOS management; improves insulin sensitivity, can restore ovulatory cycles, and is commonly used alongside or instead of hormonal therapy depending on the patient's goals (cycle regulation vs. fertility).** (S22)
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly used off-label in PCOS patients with obesity, given their weight-loss and insulin-sensitizing effects — an emerging extension of the same principle. (S22; S45)
- Combined hormonal contraception remains first-line specifically for cycle regulation and hirsutism/acne when pregnancy is not desired — metformin and hormonal therapy are frequently used together, not as alternatives. (S22; S24 (notes))
- [TB] Polycystic ovary syndrome affects about 7% of women of reproductive age. (p. 776)

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Adverse effects: GI upset (common, often transient), and rare but serious lactic acidosis risk in renal impairment — recall the renal dose-adjustment principles from Unit 5.** (S22; S22 (notes))
- Common early effect: GI upset (nausea, diarrhea); take with meals. (S23)

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Contraindications
- Hold metformin for an iodinated contrast study or renal impairment. (S23; S23 (notes))

### PCOS & the Reproductive-Metabolic Overlap — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: a 24-year-old with PCOS — irregular cycles, hirsutism, BMI 31 — not trying to conceive, no contraindications to estrogen. **What are the two major pharmacologic strategies available, and how do their goals differ?** (S25)
- **Combined hormonal contraception directly regulates cycles and improves hirsutism/acne through its hormonal effects, while metformin addresses the underlying insulin resistance driving the condition metabolically.** (S25)
- **The two are frequently combined rather than chosen as alternatives — contraception for immediate symptom control, metformin for the metabolic driver and long-term diabetes risk reduction.** (S25; S25 (notes))
- Drug names reveal the class: **Stem** · **Class** · **Examples** · **Memory aid** — estr-/-estradiol estrogen (estradiol, ethinyl estradiol); -gest-/-gestrel progestin (levonorgestrel, norethindrone, medroxyprogesterone: gest- = pregnancy hormone). (S26; S26 (notes))
- -ifene SERM (tamoxifen, raloxifene: modulates the estrogen receptor); -trozole aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole, letrozole: blocks aromatase); -steride 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor (finasteride, dutasteride: blocks steroid enzyme). (S26)
- -lutamide antiandrogen (flutamide, bicalutamide: blocks the androgen receptor); -sterone androgen or antagonist (testosterone, spironolactone: steroid; drug sets the effect); -formin biguanide (metformin: lowers insulin). (S26)
- Recap: **1.** Estrogens and progestins are always paired in a patient with a uterus — unopposed estrogen significantly raises endometrial cancer risk (Chapter 40). (S27)
- **2.** SERMs and aromatase inhibitors are two distinct strategies for reducing estrogen signaling in hormone-sensitive breast cancer — tissue-selective receptor modulation (Figure 3) vs. blocking peripheral synthesis (Figure 5), compared in Figure 4. (S27)
- **3.** Finasteride and dutasteride appear here for hirsutism and alopecia — the same drugs return in Session 2 for BPH. **4.** PCOS: insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism reinforce one another (Figure 7), so metformin and hormonal therapy are frequently combined. (S27; S27 (notes))
