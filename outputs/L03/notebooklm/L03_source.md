## Topic: Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade
Learning objectives covered: SO1, IO2.1

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — Key definitions
- **Nociceptive pain:** direct activation of pain receptors by tissue injury. (S5)
- **Inflammatory pain:** pain driven by inflammatory mediators. (S5)
- **Neuropathic pain:** pain from nerve injury or dysfunction. (S5)
- **Acute pain:** recent onset, often tied to an identifiable injury or flare. (S5)
- **Chronic pain:** persists beyond expected healing time, often > 3 months. Treatment approach differs sharply between acute and chronic pain. (S5)

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — How it works
- **Tissue injury → release of inflammatory mediators → sensitization of nociceptors → pain.** (S6)
- Key mediators: **prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine, and cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6).** (S6; SG U2-R1)
- Arachidonic acid → COX enzyme → prostaglandins → pain and inflammation: the spine of the acetaminophen, NSAID, and corticosteroid sections. (S7; S7 (notes))
- **NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors act at the COX step; glucocorticoids act further upstream; cytokine inhibitors and analgesics act through separate, parallel pathways.** (S7)
- **Corticosteroids block phospholipase A₂** (membrane phospholipids → arachidonic acid), so a broad range of mediators falls. NSAIDs block only COX, so they lower prostaglandins specifically. (S8; SG U2-R2)
- [TB] **The immune response:** chronic inflammation involves eicosanoids, leukotrienes, cytokines, chemokines, and many immune cells; RA, SLE, vasculitis, and gout all stem from this cascade. (p. 675)
- [TB] The lipoxygenase branch of arachidonic acid makes leukotrienes (chemotaxis, bronchoconstriction, vascular permeability) — a separate branch from the COX step NSAIDs block. (p. 675; p. 678)

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — Clinical relevance
- Most musculoskeletal pain in this lecture is a mix of nociceptive and inflammatory; pure neuropathic pain belongs to a separate pharmacology unit. (S5 (notes))
- The mediator list is the roadmap for the lecture: NSAIDs target prostaglandins, biologic DMARDs target specific cytokines. (S6 (notes))
- [TB] **Therapeutic strategies:** goal 1 is symptom relief and function (NSAIDs and DMARDs); goal 2 is slowing tissue damage, for which DMARDs are absolutely necessary. Response is tracked with indices such as DAS28 in RA. (p. 675)

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — Drugs named as examples
- Corticosteroids (block phospholipase A₂) vs. NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors (block COX): two distinct points of intervention. (S8; S8 (notes))

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — Exam traps
- Corticosteroids act **upstream** at phospholipase A₂, not at COX — that is why their effect is broader than an NSAID’s. (S8; SG U2-R2)
- The lecture’s drugs target nociceptive and inflammatory pain; neuropathic pain is a different unit. (S5 (notes))

### Figure: Figure 1 · The arachidonic acid pathway: where analgesics act
- Original illustrative diagram: membrane phospholipids → arachidonic acid → COX-1/COX-2 → prostaglandins → pain, inflammation, fever. Corticosteroids block phospholipase A₂ upstream; NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors block the COX step itself. (S8; S8 (notes))

### Comparison: Where each drug class intervenes (S7; S8; S6 (notes); SG U2-R2)
| Drug class | Step it blocks | What falls |
|---|---|---|
| Corticosteroids | **Phospholipase A₂** (upstream) | A broad range of mediators |
| NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors | **COX** enzyme | Prostaglandins specifically |
| Biologic DMARDs (cytokine inhibitors) | Specific cytokines or immune cells | TNF-α, IL-6 signaling; B or T cells |

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why are corticosteroids broader than NSAIDs? They act at phospholipase A₂, before arachidonic acid is released, so every mediator made from it falls — NSAIDs only stop the COX branch. (S8; SG U2-R2)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Mediators that make pain: **P-B-H-C** — **P**rostaglandins, **B**radykinin, **H**istamine, **C**ytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6). (S6; SG U2-R1)

### Musculoskeletal Pain & the Inflammatory Cascade — High-yield summary
- Injury → mediators → nociceptor sensitization → pain. (S6)
- Musculoskeletal pain is driven by inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine, cytokines) — NSAIDs and corticosteroids intervene at different points along this cascade. (S31; S6; SG U2-R1)
- Corticosteroids = phospholipase A₂ (broad); NSAIDs = COX (prostaglandins). (S8; SG U2-R2)

## Drug class: Acetaminophen
Learning objectives covered: SO2, SO5, IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.6

### Acetaminophen — Class
- Effective analgesia, with a narrow margin in overdose: treats pain and fever well, but is **not an anti-inflammatory drug** the way NSAIDs are. (S9; S10 (notes))

### Acetaminophen — Prototype + other drugs named
- Acetaminophen (the lecture’s single non-NSAID analgesic). (S10)

### Acetaminophen — Mechanism of action
- **Analgesic effect:** relieves mild-to-moderate pain, with a central mechanism that is still incompletely understood. (S10)
- **Antipyretic effect:** reduces fever via central effects on the hypothalamic temperature-regulating center. (S10)
- **Anti-inflammatory effect:** minimal at the periphery — unlike NSAIDs, acetaminophen is not a reliable anti-inflammatory agent. (S10)
- [TB] Textbook adds: a weak peripheral COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor whose pain relief also involves opioid, cannabinoid, and serotonergic systems. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Indications
- Reasonable first-line for mild osteoarthritis pain. (S24)
- Generally safer first-line than an NSAID in a patient with NSAID red flags (kidney disease, anticoagulation, older age). (S21)
- [TB] Preferred over aspirin in aspirin allergy, hemophilia, peptic ulcer history, or aspirin-induced bronchospasm; alone it is inadequate for inflammatory conditions such as RA. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Overdose: glutathione stores are depleted, NAPQI accumulates, and hepatocyte injury follows.** (S11)
- [TB] Ingesting 15 g may be fatal (centrilobular hepatic necrosis); take care with liver disease, heavy alcohol use, or malnutrition. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Contraindications
- Not covered in materials.

### Acetaminophen — Drug interactions
- **Hidden overdose:** many combination cold, flu, and sleep products also contain acetaminophen — **unintentional double-dosing is a leading cause of acetaminophen-related liver injury.** (S12)
- [TB] Combining acetaminophen with an NSAID improves pain control but can potentiate the NSAID’s GI toxicity. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Pharmacokinetics
- Normal handling: acetaminophen → NAPQI (toxic metabolite) → glutathione conjugation → safe excretion. (S11)
- NAPQI = N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine — the “toxic metabolite” example from Lecture 1’s drug-metabolism section. (S11 (notes))
- [TB] Mostly conjugated to inactive sulfate and glucuronide; half-life 2–3 hours, relatively unaffected by renal function. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Monitoring
- **Always ask about ALL sources of acetaminophen.** (S12)
- Patients often don’t realize a product contains acetaminophen unless they check the label — reinforce generic-name awareness. (S12 (notes))
- [TB] Adults: generally no more than 4 g/day — and even 4 g is associated with more liver function test abnormalities. (p. 694)

### Acetaminophen — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- **Antidote: N-acetylcysteine**, a glutathione precursor — it replenishes glutathione to detoxify NAPQI. (S11; SG U2-R3)
- Case: a patient on regular acetaminophen for back pain starts an OTC cold-and-flu combination. **What is the hidden danger in this combination?** Double-dosing acetaminophen. (S12)
- [TB] **Other analgesics — tramadol:** weak μ-opioid agonist plus serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition; no anti-inflammatory effect; up to 300 mg/day; risks serotonin syndrome and seizures. (p. 694; p. 695)

### Comparison: Acetaminophen vs. NSAIDs (S10; S11; S13; S19)
|  | Acetaminophen | NSAIDs |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Yes (mild–moderate) | Yes |
| Fever | Yes (hypothalamus) | Yes |
| Inflammation | **Minimal — not reliable** | **Yes — powerful** |
| Main danger | Liver (NAPQI) in overdose or double-dosing | GI, renal, cardiovascular |
| Antidote | N-acetylcysteine | Not covered in materials |

### Acetaminophen — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why does N-acetylcysteine work? Glutathione is what safely conjugates NAPQI. Overdose uses it up; N-acetylcysteine is a glutathione precursor, so it refills the supply. (S11; SG U2-R3)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Acetaminophen does **P**ain and **P**yrexia, not **P**uffiness (inflammation). (S10)

### Acetaminophen — High-yield summary
- Acetaminophen treats pain and fever but is not a reliable anti-inflammatory agent; its overdose toxicity is mediated by NAPQI accumulation. (S31; S10; S11)
- Overdose: glutathione depleted → NAPQI → hepatocyte injury. (S11)
- Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (glutathione precursor). (S11; SG U2-R3)
- Ask about ALL sources — combination cold/flu/sleep products hide acetaminophen. (S12)

## Drug class: NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition
Learning objectives covered: SO2, SO3, SO5, IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.3, IO2.4

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Class
- Powerful anti-inflammatory effect, with real systemic risk. (S13)
- **Nonselective NSAIDs inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2.** (S16)
- **Celecoxib selectively inhibits COX-2 over COX-1.** (S17)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Prototype + other drugs named
- Ibuprofen — common first-line choice for mild-moderate pain. (S16)
- Naproxen — longer half-life, twice-daily dosing. (S16)
- Diclofenac — available in oral and topical formulations. (S16)
- Indomethacin — more potent, more GI/CNS adverse effects. (S16)
- **Ketorolac — short-term use only, significant GI/renal risk.** (S16)
- Celecoxib — the COX-2 selective agent. (S17)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Mechanism of action
- NSAID → COX inhibition → ↓ prostaglandins → ↓ pain, inflammation, fever. (S14)
- **COX-1, the “housekeeping” enzyme:** maintains gastric mucosal protection and normal renal blood flow; inhibiting it causes GI and renal adverse effects. (S15; SG U2-R4)
- **COX-2:** induced mainly at sites of inflammation and the primary source of inflammatory prostaglandins; selective inhibition targets inflammation with less GI toxicity. (S15; SG U2-R4)
- Simplified teaching model, not an absolute separation: COX-1 and COX-2 functions overlap, and COX-2 is also constitutively expressed in the kidney and elsewhere. (S15 (notes))
- [TB] Aspirin irreversibly blocks platelet COX (the effect lasts the 8–10-day life of the platelet); other nonselective NSAIDs are reversible inhibitors. (p. 675; p. 677)
- [TB] COX-2 inhibitors spare platelet thromboxane (made by COX-1) but block endothelial prostacyclin — so they are not cardioprotective. (p. 677)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Indications
- Oral NSAIDs for osteoarthritis — effective, but carry the systemic risks below. (S24)
- Topical NSAIDs — effective local relief with much lower systemic exposure. (S24)
- [TB] NSAIDs ease symptoms but, unlike DMARDs, do not alter the course of any arthritic disorder. (p. 676)
- [TB] Used in RA, spondyloarthropathies, OA, sprains and strains, low back pain, and gout (tolmetin appears ineffective in gout). (p. 676)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Gastrointestinal:** gastritis, peptic ulcer, GI bleeding. (S19)
- **Renal:** decreased renal perfusion, fluid retention, acute kidney injury. (S19)
- **Cardiovascular:** hypertension, increased risk of cardiovascular events. (S19)
- **Celecoxib:** lower risk of ulcer/bleeding, but **COX-2 selectivity is associated with increased cardiovascular event risk — a key trade-off.** (S17; SG U2-R5)
- Celecoxib’s renal risk is similar to nonselective NSAIDs — COX-2 selectivity does not protect the kidney. (S17; SG U2-R5)
- [TB] Beyond GI, renal, and CV: headache, tinnitus, dizziness, rare aseptic meningitis, liver test abnormalities, asthma, rashes, hyperkalemia. (p. 676)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Contraindications
- Use caution or avoid in: active GI bleeding or ulcer history; chronic kidney disease; heart failure or cardiovascular disease. (S20)
- Also caution or avoid with anticoagulant therapy and in late pregnancy. (S20)
- [TB] Ibuprofen is relatively contraindicated with nasal polyps, angioedema, or bronchospastic reactivity to aspirin. (p. 680)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Drug interactions
- NSAIDs + anticoagulants → ↑ bleeding risk. (S20)
- NSAIDs + ACE inhibitors/ARBs → ↑ AKI risk; add a diuretic and it is the **“triple whammy,”** a very high-yield AKI pattern. (S20; S20 (notes))
- NSAIDs + diuretics → reduced diuretic effect, ↑ renal risk. (S20)
- NSAIDs + corticosteroids → ↑ GI ulcer risk. (S20)
- [TB] Ibuprofen taken with aspirin blocks aspirin’s irreversible platelet inhibition, limiting its cardioprotective effect. (p. 680)
- [TB] Celecoxib (a CYP2C9 substrate) occasionally interacts with warfarin and raises lithium and fluconazole levels. (p. 679)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Pharmacokinetics
- Naproxen’s longer half-life allows twice-daily dosing; diclofenac comes as oral and topical forms. (S16)
- [TB] **Chemistry & pharmacokinetics:** most NSAIDs are weak acids, well absorbed, ~98% albumin-bound, metabolized by CYP3A or CYP2C, and renally excreted. (p. 675)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Monitoring
- These three systems (GI, renal, CV) are the organizing framework for every NSAID contraindication and interaction discussion (Table 1). (S19 (notes))
- [TB] Highest GI-bleeding risk: celecoxib, or a nonselective NSAID plus omeprazole or misoprostol. (p. 681)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: a 72-year-old with OA, CKD, hypertension, and an anticoagulant. **Is an NSAID a good first-line choice for this patient’s pain? Why or why not?** (S21)
- **Three NSAID red flags at once** — renal disease, anticoagulation, and age-related bleeding risk — make acetaminophen or a non-NSAID strategy generally safer first-line. (S21)
- Celecoxib is a classic “trade one risk for another” point: a better GI profile, but not a free lunch cardiovascularly or renally. (S17 (notes))
- There is no risk-free NSAID choice, only a different risk profile. (S18 (notes))
- [TB] **Choice of NSAID:** all are about equally effective, so choose by toxicity, cost, and patient factors; in renal insufficiency nonacetylated salicylates may be best. (p. 681)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why do NSAIDs hurt the stomach and kidney? Blocking COX-1, the housekeeping enzyme, removes the prostaglandins that protect gastric mucosa and keep renal blood flow normal. (S15)
- Why it happens: Why the “triple whammy”? An ACE inhibitor/ARB, a diuretic, and an NSAID each push kidney perfusion the wrong way at once — together they cause acute kidney injury. (S20; S20 (notes))
- Mnemonic (memory aid): NSAID harms = **GRC**: **G**ut, **R**enal, **C**ardiovascular (Table 1). (S19)

### NSAIDs & COX-2 Selective Inhibition — High-yield summary
- COX-1 = housekeeping (gastric protection, renal blood flow); COX-2 = inflammation. (S15; SG U2-R4)
- NSAIDs are effective but carry real GI, renal, and cardiovascular risk; celecoxib trades GI risk for cardiovascular risk (renal risk similar). (S31; S17; SG U2-R5)
- NSAID harms: GI, renal, cardiovascular. (S19)
- Triple whammy: NSAID + ACEi/ARB + diuretic → AKI. (S20 (notes))
- Ketorolac: short-term only; significant GI/renal risk. (S16)

## Drug class: Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs
Learning objectives covered: IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.3, IO2.4, IO2.5, IO2.6, SO3, SO4, SO5

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Class
- **Osteoarthritis (OA):** primarily degenerative joint disease — cartilage breakdown with mechanical wear. (S23)
- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA):** autoimmune, systemic inflammatory disease — immune-mediated joint destruction. (S23)
- OA treatment goal: pain control and function. (S23)
- RA treatment goal: suppress disease activity, not just pain. (S23)
- The hinge: OA is managed mainly with analgesics/NSAIDs, while RA requires disease-modifying therapy. (S23 (notes))
- **DMARD (Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drug):** a drug intended to alter the course of RA, not just mask its symptoms. (S25; SG U2-R6)
- **Goal of therapy:** slow or halt joint destruction and systemic inflammation, rather than providing analgesia alone. (S25)
- An NSAID can make a patient feel better without changing the disease trajectory; a DMARD is meant to change the trajectory itself. (S25 (notes); SG U2-R6)
- Targeted therapy: each biologic class corresponds to a different cytokine or cell type in the inflammatory cascade — the mediator list from the start of the lecture. (S28 (notes))
- [TB] DMARD effects take 2 weeks to 6 months to appear. Types: conventional synthetic (cs), biologic (b), and targeted synthetic (ts, e.g., tofacitinib). (p. 681)
- [TB] Biologics are large molecules, usually proteins made by recombinant DNA technology; JAK inhibitors are oral small-molecule targeted synthetic DMARDs. (p. 681; p. 686)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Prototype + other drugs named
- **Methotrexate — the anchor drug for most RA regimens.** (S26)
- Hydroxychloroquine — milder disease; relatively favorable safety profile. (S26)
- Sulfasalazine — often used in combination regimens. (S26)
- Leflunomide — alternative when methotrexate is not tolerated. (S26)
- Adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab — **TNF inhibitors**. (S28; SG U2-R8)
- Tocilizumab — **IL-6 inhibitor**. (S28; SG U2-R8)
- Rituximab — **B-cell targeted therapy**. (S28; SG U2-R8)
- Abatacept — **T-cell costimulation modulator**. (S28; SG U2-R8)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, reducing immune cell proliferation at low RA doses.** (methotrexate) (S27; SG U2-M)
- TNF inhibitors block tumor necrosis factor-alpha signaling. (S28)
- IL-6 inhibitors block interleukin-6 receptor signaling. (S28)
- B-cell targeted therapy depletes or modulates B lymphocytes. (S28)
- T-cell costimulation modulators block the second signal needed for T-cell activation. (S28; SG U2-M)
- Figure 3: abatacept acts at T-cell activation, TNF inhibitors and tocilizumab at cytokine release, rituximab at B cells — all upstream of synovial inflammation and joint destruction. (S29)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Indications
- **OA step 1:** acetaminophen — reasonable first-line for mild OA pain. (S24)
- **OA step 2:** oral NSAIDs (effective, systemic risks) or topical NSAIDs (local relief, much lower systemic exposure). (S24)
- **Intra-articular corticosteroid injections:** short-term relief for flares in a single joint. (S24)
- Other approaches: activity modification, physical therapy, weight management. (S24)
- Methotrexate anchors most RA regimens; leflunomide is the alternative when it is not tolerated. (S26)
- Rheumatoid arthritis: they modify the disease course rather than only masking symptoms. (S31; SG U2-R6)
- [TB] **Glucocorticoid drugs in rheumatic diseases:** used chronically in 30–70% of RA patients; relief is prompt and dramatic, and they slow new bone erosions. (p. 693)
- [TB] Long-term prednisone in RA: no more than 7.5 mg daily, tapered when possible — even 2.8 mg daily for over 5 years is linked to severe infections. (p. 693)
- [TB] Intra-articular corticosteroids often relieve painful symptoms for up to several months. (p. 693)
- [TB] Long-term glucocorticoid harms: osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, Cushing syndrome, infections, cataract, glaucoma, gastric ulcer, depression. (p. 694)
- [TB] Methotrexate starts at 7.5 mg weekly and rises to 15–25 mg weekly; it slows new erosions in RA. (p. 688)
- [TB] **Combination therapy with DMARDs** is now the rule when monotherapy falls short; triple therapy (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine) rivals etanercept plus methotrexate. (p. 693)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- [TB] **Boxed warning — all TNF inhibitors:** possible malignancy, especially lymphoma. (p. 692)
- [TB] **Boxed warning — all JAK inhibitors:** venous thromboembolism (DVT, PE), major adverse cardiovascular events, and cancer. (p. 686)
- Methotrexate key adverse effects: **hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, pulmonary toxicity.** (S27; SG U2-R7)
- **The major safety theme across all DMARDs: immunosuppression → increased infection risk.** This holds for conventional and biologic DMARDs alike. (S30; SG U2-R8)
- [TB] Methotrexate’s most common toxicities are nausea and stomatitis; dose-related liver enzyme elevations are frequent. (p. 689)
- [TB] TNF inhibitors: TB reactivation, fungal infections, hepatitis B reactivation, new or worse heart failure, demyelinating disease, lupus-like syndrome. (p. 690; p. 692)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Contraindications
- **Methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic).** (S27)
- [TB] Don’t stack biologics: abatacept with a TNF inhibitor probably raises serious infections; upadacitinib with biologic DMARDs is not recommended. (p. 682; p. 687)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Drug interactions
- [TB] Hydroxychloroquine can reduce methotrexate clearance. (p. 688)
- [TB] Tocilizumab stimulates CYP450 — doses of cyclosporine or warfarin may need adjusting. (p. 692)
- [TB] Methotrexate given with TNF inhibitors reduces anti-drug antibodies (infliximab, golimumab, certolizumab). (p. 691; p. 692)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Pharmacokinetics
- **Methotrexate is dosed weekly (not daily) — a critical patient-safety point, since daily dosing can be fatal.** (S27; SG U2-R7)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Monitoring
- **Folate supplementation** is routinely co-prescribed with methotrexate to reduce toxicity. (S27; SG U2-R7)
- **Baseline screening:** e.g., tuberculosis and hepatitis B/C screening before starting biologics. (S30)
- **Vaccination:** update vaccines, especially live vaccines, before starting therapy. (S30)
- **Ongoing monitoring:** regular labs to catch cytopenias, hepatotoxicity, or infection early. (S30)
- **Infection precautions:** counsel patients to report fevers or infection symptoms promptly. (S30)
- [TB] Folic acid or leucovorin reduces stomatitis, GI, and liver test problems but may cut methotrexate efficacy by 10–18%. (p. 689)
- [TB] Hydroxychloroquine: baseline eye exam, then annual screening after 5 years (retinopathy). (p. 684)
- [TB] Avoid live vaccines for up to 3 months after stopping abatacept; stop upadacitinib before a live attenuated vaccine. (p. 682; p. 687)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- A 72-year-old with OA, CKD, hypertension, and an anticoagulant: acetaminophen or a non-NSAID strategy first. (S21)
- OA and RA both affect the joint but are two very different diseases — don’t give RA patients analgesia alone. (S22; S23 (notes))
- Methotrexate’s weekly dosing is one of the highest-yield patient-safety points in all of rheumatology pharmacology. (S27 (notes))
- Match each generic name to its class: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab (TNF); tocilizumab (IL-6); rituximab (B cell); abatacept (T-cell costimulation). (S28; S29; S31)
- [TB] Topical diclofenac 1% gel is sold over the counter for OA and muscular pain. (p. 679)
- [TB] **Biosimilars:** near-copies of legacy biologics with the same efficacy, toxicity, and immunogenicity at potentially lower cost. (p. 693)
- [TB] If methotrexate alone falls short: add csDMARDs or a biologic — usually a TNF inhibitor first, then non-TNF biologics or JAK inhibitors. (p. 701)

- Lecture slides: Inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, reducing immune cell proliferation at low RA doses. (S27; SG U2-M)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why can’t RA be treated like OA? RA is immune-mediated joint destruction; analgesics ease pain but leave the immune attack — and the damage — running. (S23; S25 (notes))
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Methotrexate rules = **W-F-P**: **W**eekly (never daily), **F**olate with it, **P**regnancy contraindicated. (S27; SG U2-R7)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Methotrexate toxicity hits **L-B-L**: **L**iver, **B**one marrow, **L**ung. (S27)
- Why it happens: Why screen for TB and hepatitis first? These drugs suppress the immune response that keeps latent infections in check, so a hidden infection can reactivate. (S30)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Generic-name endings: **-mab** = monoclonal antibody (adalimumab, infliximab, tocilizumab, rituximab); **-cept** = receptor-fusion protein (etanercept, abatacept). (S28; p. 682; p. 690)

### Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid Arthritis & DMARDs — High-yield summary
- OA = degenerative → pain control; RA = autoimmune → suppress disease activity. (S23)
- OA ladder: acetaminophen → oral or topical NSAIDs → intra-articular steroids for a single-joint flare. (S24)
- DMARDs (conventional and biologic — methotrexate, adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, tocilizumab, rituximab, abatacept) modify RA’s disease course rather than only masking symptoms. (S31; S25; SG U2-R6)
- Methotrexate: DHFR inhibitor, **weekly**, with folate. (S27; SG U2-R7)
- Methotrexate toxicity: liver, marrow, lung; teratogenic. (S27)
- HCQ = milder RA; sulfasalazine = combinations; leflunomide = if MTX not tolerated. (S26)
- TNF: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab · IL-6: tocilizumab · B cell: rituximab · T-cell costimulation: abatacept. (S28; SG U2-R8)
- Shared risk: immunosuppression → infection. (S30; SG U2-R8)
- Before biologics: TB + hepatitis B/C screening, update (live) vaccines. (S30)
