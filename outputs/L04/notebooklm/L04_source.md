## Drug class: Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO4, IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.3, IO2.4, IO2.5

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Class
- Gout is a crystal-driven inflammatory arthritis with **two very different treatment phases**. (S3)
- **The chain:** purine metabolism → uric acid → monosodium urate crystals → joint inflammation. Everything downstream — colchicine, allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid — targets a specific point along this chain. (S4; S4 (notes))
- **Purine breakdown:** hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid (via **xanthine oxidase**) → hyperuricemia → urate crystals in the joint. (S5)
- Crystals activate neutrophils and macrophages (**NLRP3, IL-1β**) → the acute flare, the end of the cascade. (S5)
- **Raise urate:** diets rich in purines and fructose; alcohol, especially beer (more production, less renal excretion). (S5)
- **Lower urate excretion:** diuretics, especially thiazides and loop agents; chronic kidney disease sustains hyperuricemia. (S5)
- Walk the full chain and sort the drugs into **two buckets:** “lowers uric acid” (allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid) vs. “treats the acute flare” (colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids) — the same acute-vs-chronic distinction made explicit next. (S6; S6 (notes))
- **Acute flare** — goal: control pain and inflammation now. Agents: colchicine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids. (S7)
- **Chronic management** — goal: lower serum uric acid to prevent future flares. Agents: allopurinol, febuxostat (↓ production); probenecid (↑ excretion). (S7)
- [TB] Humans lack uricase, the enzyme other mammals use to turn uric acid into soluble allantoin; most people with hyperuricemia never have a gout attack. (p. 695)
- [TB] Treat gout, not a number: long-term drug treatment of asymptomatic hyperuricemia is unproven. Adherence is poor (18–26% in younger patients). (p. 695)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Prototype + other drugs named
- Colchicine — acute treatment (plant-derived alkaloid, no stem). (S8; S15)
- Allopurinol — first-line urate-lowering therapy for most patients. (S10)
- Febuxostat — alternative xanthine oxidase inhibitor. (S10)
- Probenecid — uricosuric (second-line oral). Pegloticase, rasburicase — uricases (refractory). (S12; S13)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: disrupts microtubule formation in neutrophils.** Colchicine binds tubulin → less neutrophil migration and NLRP3 activation → less inflammation at the crystal site. (S8; S9; SG U2-M)
- Colchicine works on the cell’s cytoskeleton, so its effect builds in the early hours of a flare. (S9 (notes))
- **Allopurinol** (via its metabolite oxypurinol) and **febuxostat** block **xanthine oxidase** → ↓ uric acid **production**. (S10; S11; SG U2-R9)
- Febuxostat is also a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, with a different chemical structure. (S10)
- **Probenecid — mechanism: inhibits renal tubular reabsorption of uric acid, increasing its excretion** (blocks URAT1). A uricosuric, not a xanthine oxidase inhibitor — a different strategy. (S12; S13; SG U2-R9)
- **Pegloticase, rasburicase:** recombinant **uricase** converts uric acid to **allantoin**, which is soluble and excreted. (S12; S13)
- Uricosurics block reabsorption; uricases remove uric acid enzymatically, which is why they suit refractory disease. (S13 (notes))
- NSAIDs and corticosteroids reduce joint inflammation in a flare. (S6)
- [TB] Allopurinol is a purine analog; its metabolite oxypurinol (half-life 15 hours) also inhibits xanthine oxidase, so it is dosed once daily. (p. 695)
- [TB] Colchicine relieves gout pain and inflammation in 12–24 hours without changing urate metabolism or excretion. (p. 696)
- [TB] Glucocorticoids in gout: reduce neutrophil migration, prostaglandins, and IL-1β. (p. 698)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Indications
- **Treat the acute flare first** (colchicine, NSAID, or corticosteroid); urate-lowering therapy is deferred until the flare resolves, then used for long-term prevention. (S14; SG U2-R10)
- Colchicine is most effective when started early in a flare — **best within 24 hours of onset**. (S8; S9)
- Allopurinol is first-line for most patients; febuxostat is the alternative when allopurinol is not tolerated or contraindicated. (S10)
- Probenecid is an older, second-line oral option. **Pegloticase — a recombinant uricase enzyme for severe, refractory gout not controlled by other agents.** (S12; S12 (notes); S13)
- [TB] Colchicine flare regimen: 1.2 mg, then 0.6 mg an hour later; prophylaxis 0.6 mg once or twice daily during urate-lowering therapy initiation. (p. 696)
- [TB] Corticosteroids are a good alternative when NSAIDs or colchicine are contraindicated, including CKD: prednisone 30–50 mg/d for 1–2 days, tapered over 7–10 days, or intra-articular. (p. 698)
- [TB] All NSAIDs except aspirin, salicylates, and tolmetin treat gout flares. (p. 697)
- [TB] Febuxostat appears safe in moderate CKD; it can be tried after allopurinol hypersensitivity, though a reaction may still occur. (p. 697)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Febuxostat: boxed warning for cardiovascular death** — it carries its own cardiovascular safety considerations. (S10; S11)
- **Colchicine: narrow therapeutic window** — GI adverse effects (diarrhea) are common and dose-limiting. (S8; S9)
- **Allopurinol:** the **HLA-B*58:01** allele raises the risk of severe hypersensitivity / severe cutaneous reactions. (S10; S11)
- Pegloticase: infusion reactions are common; anti-drug antibodies can reduce response. (S13)
- [TB] Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (SJS/TEN, fever, eosinophilia, hepatitis, renal failure) usually appears 2–4 weeks after starting — stop at the first rash. (p. 696)
- [TB] Colchicine can also cause marrow suppression, myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and neuritis; it is not removed by dialysis. (p. 696)
- [TB] Febuxostat: liver function abnormalities, diarrhea, headache, nausea. (p. 697)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Contraindications
- **Do NOT start urate-lowering therapy during an acute flare** — starting or changing it then can actually prolong the flare. Start or adjust only once the flare has resolved. (S7; S7 (notes); SG U2-R10)
- **Probenecid:** avoid with a history of uric acid kidney stones or significant renal impairment; needs adequate hydration. (S12; S13)
- **Pegloticase and rasburicase:** contraindicated in **G6PD deficiency** (hemolysis risk). (S13)
- [TB] Aspirin is avoided in gout: at low doses (≤2.6 g/d) it causes renal retention of uric acid. (p. 697)
- [TB] Uricosurics should not be used in patients who already excrete large amounts of uric acid. (p. 698)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Drug interactions
- **Colchicine + CYP3A4 / P-gp inhibitors** (clarithromycin, cyclosporine, strong CYP3A4 inhibitors) → raised levels and toxicity. (S9)
- **Allopurinol + azathioprine or mercaptopurine:** reduce the dose (xanthine oxidase also clears these drugs). (S11)
- [TB] Allopurinol with azathioprine: cut the azathioprine dose by 75%. (p. 696)
- [TB] Probenecid also reduces renal secretion of other weak acids, such as penicillin. (p. 698)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Pharmacokinetics
- Colchicine: adjust the dose for renal or hepatic impairment. (S9)
- Allopurinol: reduce the dose in renal impairment. (S11)
- Pegloticase is given by IV infusion. (S12)
- [TB] Allopurinol: start 100 mg/d (≤50 mg/d in CKD), titrate every 2–5 weeks to uric acid <6 mg/dL (max 800 mg/d). (p. 695)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Monitoring
- Xanthine oxidase inhibitors: **start low and titrate upward toward a serum urate target**. (S11)
- When starting urate-lowering therapy, give **colchicine or an NSAID as prophylaxis for 3–6 months**. (S11)
- [TB] Probenecid: keep urine volume high and urine pH above 6.0 early on to prevent stones; start 2–3 weeks after an attack. (p. 698)
- [TB] Pegloticase: premedicate with a corticosteroid and antihistamine; flares are common in the first 3–6 months. (p. 698)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- This acute-vs-chronic distinction is one of the single most testable concepts in gout pharmacology. (S7 (notes))
- Allopurinol vs. febuxostat: both sit on the same enzyme; the choice turns on safety profile and tolerability. (S11 (notes))
- **HLA-B*5801** is a direct callback to Lecture 1’s pharmacogenomics section. (S10 (notes))
- Pegloticase is reserved for refractory cases given its cost, infusion-reaction risk, and need for specialty administration. (S12)
- Case: sudden, severe pain, redness, and swelling of the first metatarsophalangeal joint — an acute gout flare. **Is this the moment to start acute treatment, chronic urate-lowering therapy, or both?** Acute treatment now; ULT later. (S14)
- **Name clues:** suffixes often reveal the mechanism before you recall the drug — use them as memory hooks, then confirm each class before prescribing. (S15; S15 (notes))
- **-ostat** marks xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol is the exception); **-ase** names enzymes, so pegloticase and rasburicase are uricases; **-sone, -olone** mark corticosteroids used to treat flares. (S15)
- [TB] Allopurinol already running when a flare hits? Don’t stop it — stopping can worsen and lengthen the attack. (p. 696)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why not start allopurinol in a flare? Shifting urate levels mobilizes crystals — starting or changing ULT during a flare can worsen or prolong it. Calm the flare first. (S7 (notes); SG U2-R10)
- Why it happens: Why does colchicine have to start early? It works on the neutrophil’s cytoskeleton, stopping migration — once neutrophils have flooded the joint, it has less to prevent. (S9; S9 (notes))
- Mnemonic (memory aid): **Make less, pee more, break it down:** xanthine oxidase inhibitors make less; probenecid pees more; pegloticase breaks it down. (S10–S13; SG U2-R9)

### Gout: Acute Flares & Urate-Lowering Therapy — High-yield summary
- Acute gout treatment and chronic urate-lowering therapy are different goals, started at different times. (S49; S7)
- Flare: colchicine, NSAID, or corticosteroid. Prevention: allopurinol/febuxostat (↓ production) or probenecid (↑ excretion). (S7; SG U2-R9)
- Colchicine: microtubules; best within 24 h; diarrhea; CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors. (S8; S9)
- Allopurinol: HLA-B*58:01; febuxostat: boxed CV death warning. (S10; S11)
- Pegloticase/rasburicase: no G6PD deficiency. (S13)

## Drug class: Skeletal Muscle Relaxants
Learning objectives covered: SO2, SO4, IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.3, IO2.5, IO2.6

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Class
- Different mechanisms for **spasm, spasticity, and rigidity** — not the same thing, and they often require distinct pharmacologic approaches. (S16; S17)
- **Muscle spasm:** sudden, involuntary contraction, often from acute musculoskeletal injury; typically treated with centrally acting relaxants. (S17)
- **Spasticity:** velocity-dependent increased tone from **upper motor neuron injury**. **Rigidity:** constant increased tone, independent of velocity. (S17)
- **Where tone is controlled:** upper motor neurons (descending inhibitory pathways) ▸ spinal interneurons (GABA, glycine, alpha-2) ▸ alpha motor neuron ▸ neuromuscular junction (ACh release) ▸ muscle fiber contraction (SR Ca²⁺ release). (S18)
- **Centrally acting** (act within the CNS to reduce muscle tone): cyclobenzaprine, tizanidine, baclofen — first-line; carisoprodol, methocarbamol, metaxalone, chlorzoxazone — other options. (S19)
- **Peripherally / locally acting:** dantrolene acts directly on skeletal muscle itself; botulinum toxin acts at the neuromuscular junction — useful when a central mechanism is not desired (or not safe). (S19)
- **Highest-yield distinction:** “where does the drug act?” sorts every agent into one of two buckets — **dantrolene is the only one in the peripheral (muscle) bucket.** (S20; S20 (notes); SG U2-R11)
- [TB] Spasticity is common after spinal injury, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, and stroke; these drugs relieve painful spasm better than they restore function. (p. 510; p. 511)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Prototype + other drugs named
- Baclofen — GABA-B agonist (spinal cord). (S21)
- Tizanidine — central α2-adrenergic agonist. (S23)
- Cyclobenzaprine — tricyclic-like central relaxant (brainstem). (S25)
- Dantrolene — RyR1 blocker in the muscle fiber. (S28)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Mechanism of action
- **Central agents** reduce CNS excitatory output to motor neurons → ↓ muscle tone. Baclofen and tizanidine act on spinal interneurons and afferents; cyclobenzaprine acts in the brainstem. (S18; S20; SG U2-R11)
- **Baclofen — mechanism: GABA-B receptor agonist in the spinal cord.** Binding GABA-B → opens K⁺ channels, closes Ca²⁺ channels (presynaptic inhibition) → less excitatory transmitter release → lower motor neuron firing → reduced spasticity. (S21; S22; SG U2-M)
- **Tizanidine — mechanism: central α2-adrenergic agonist.** Activating alpha-2 receptors → reduced presynaptic release of excitatory amino acids → lower motor neuron activity → reduced muscle tone. (S23; S24)
- **Cyclobenzaprine:** acts in the brainstem; antagonizes **5-HT2** and descending serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways → reduced tonic somatic motor activity → muscle relaxation. Structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants. (S25; S26)
- **Dantrolene — mechanism: decreases calcium release from the skeletal muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum.** Nerve impulse → DHPR activated → **RyR1** opens → Ca²⁺ released → contraction; dantrolene blocks RyR1 → less Ca²⁺ → relaxation. (S28; S29; S18; SG U2-R11; SG U2-M)
- Dantrolene acts inside the fiber on RyR1 — directly on the muscle fiber, not the CNS — distinct from every centrally acting agent. (S28; S18)
- **Botulinum toxin — blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction** (ACh release at the junction) — a third mechanism, distinct from both CNS-acting drugs and dantrolene’s sarcoplasmic-reticulum action. (S27; S27 (notes); S18)
- [TB] Diazepam (GABA-A) reduces spasticity partly in the spinal cord, but sedates at the doses needed. (p. 511)
- [TB] Dantrolene binds RyR1; cardiac and smooth muscle use RyR2, so they are minimally depressed. (p. 513)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Indications
- **Baclofen:** spasticity, particularly from spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis; intrathecal option for severe spasticity. (S21; S22)
- Cyclobenzaprine — **primary use: short-term treatment of acute, localized muscle spasm**; short-term use only, 2–3 weeks. (S25; S26)
- Dantrolene — **clinical use #1: malignant hyperthermia (a true emergency indication)** (triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine) — a true emergency, perioperative indication where the drug is literally life-saving. (S28; S28 (notes); S29; SG U2-R11)
- **Dantrolene #2:** chronic spasticity, when central agents are not appropriate; also used for neuroleptic malignant syndrome. IV formulation for acute crisis. (S28; S29)
- **Botulinum toxin:** focal spasticity or dystonia; effect is local and temporary, requiring repeat injections. (S27)
- [TB] Malignant hyperthermia: give IV dantrolene 1 mg/kg, repeated as needed, while controlling acidosis and temperature. (p. 513)
- [TB] Cyclobenzaprine is ineffective for spasm from cerebral palsy or spinal cord injury. (p. 513)
- [TB] Gabapentin has shown promise as a spasmolytic in multiple sclerosis. (p. 512)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- [TB] Botulinum toxin: black box warning for distant spread of toxin, greatest in children treated for spasticity. (p. 513)
- **Baclofen:** CNS depression — sedation and weakness, additive risk with other CNS depressants. (S21; S22)
- **Baclofen withdrawal:** abrupt discontinuation (especially intrathecal) can cause a dangerous syndrome — **seizures, hyperthermia, rebound spasticity**. (S21; S22)
- **Tizanidine:** sedation, hypotension and bradycardia, dry mouth, **hepatotoxicity**. (S23; S24)
- **Cyclobenzaprine:** prominent sedation (commonly dosed at night); **anticholinergic** effects — dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion. (S25; S26)
- **Dantrolene:** hepatotoxicity; generalized weakness. (S29)
- **Carisoprodol — metabolized to meprobamate; Schedule IV controlled substance with dependence/misuse potential.** (S27)
- **Chlorzoxazone:** rare but notable hepatotoxicity risk. Methocarbamol is generally less sedating than cyclobenzaprine; metaxalone has a relatively favorable sedation profile. (S27)
- [TB] Tizanidine causes hypotension in 16–33%; abrupt withdrawal causes rebound hypertension and tachycardia. (p. 512)
- [TB] High-dose baclofen: somnolence, respiratory depression, coma; more seizures in epilepsy. (p. 512)
- [TB] Carisoprodol withdrawal after long use: anxiety, tremor, twitching, insomnia, hallucinations. (p. 511)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Contraindications
- Cyclobenzaprine: use caution in older adults (**Beers list**); MAOIs contraindicated. (S25; S26)
- Tizanidine with ciprofloxacin or fluvoxamine is contraindicated (CYP1A2). (S24)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Drug interactions
- **Tizanidine + CYP1A2 inhibitors** (ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine) → strongly raised levels; additive effect with antihypertensives. (S24)
- **Cyclobenzaprine:** serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol; MAOIs contraindicated. (S26)
- **Baclofen:** additive sedation with other CNS depressants. (S21)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Pharmacokinetics
- Baclofen: renal dose adjustment. (S22)
- [TB] Tizanidine: start 2 mg every 6–8 hours, max 36 mg/d; adjust for hepatic or renal impairment. (p. 512)
- [TB] Dantrolene: only about one-third of an oral dose is absorbed; half-life about 8 hours. (p. 513)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Monitoring
- Tizanidine requires liver function monitoring (LFTs) with regular use. (S23; S24)
- Never stop intrathecal baclofen abruptly. (S21; S22)
- [TB] Botulinum toxin injections: no more often than every 3 months. (p. 513)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Name clues: **-curonium / -curium** nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers (vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium); **succinylcholine** depolarizing blocker; **-azepam** benzodiazepine (GABA-A, diazepam). (S30)
- Name clues: **cyclo-** tricyclic-like (cyclobenzaprine); **-idine** central alpha-2 agonist (tizanidine); baclofen has no shared stem (GABA analog); **-trolene** blocks RyR1 (dantrolene); **-toxin** blocks ACh release (botulinum toxin). (S30)
- [TB] Neuromuscular blockers (anesthesia, ICU) paralyze at the end plate and have no CNS activity; succinylcholine is the only depolarizing one. (p. 499; p. 503)
- [TB] Succinylcholine can cause fatal hyperkalemia after burns, denervation, or major trauma, and with volatile anesthetics can trigger malignant hyperthermia. (p. 508)
- [TB] Sugammadex reverses rocuronium/vecuronium; it binds progestins, so use backup contraception for 7 days. (p. 509; p. 510)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why is dantrolene the malignant hyperthermia drug? MH is runaway calcium release through RyR1 in muscle; a CNS drug can’t reach that — dantrolene blocks RyR1 itself. (S28; S29; SG U2-R11)
- Why it happens: Why does stopping intrathecal baclofen go badly? The spinal cord loses its GABA-B brake all at once: seizures, hyperthermia, and rebound spasticity. (S21; S22)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): **Cyclobenzaprine looks like a tricyclic, acts like one:** sleepy, dry, can’t pee, confused — and serotonin syndrome with SSRIs. (S25; S26)

### Skeletal Muscle Relaxants — High-yield summary

- Tizanidine: α2; LFTs; no ciprofloxacin/fluvoxamine. (S23; S24)

## Drug class: Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis
Learning objectives covered: SO3, SO4, IO2.1, IO2.2, IO2.3, IO2.4, IO2.5, IO2.6

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Class
- Bone pharmacology is about **balancing bone formation and bone resorption**. (S31; S49)
- **Osteoblasts** build new bone matrix (promoted by several osteoporosis therapies); **osteoclasts** resorb existing bone (suppressed by most osteoporosis therapies). (S32)
- Nearly every osteoporosis drug shifts this balance one way or the other — slowing osteoclasts or stimulating osteoblasts. (S32 (notes))
- **RANKL/OPG:** osteoblasts or stromal cells express RANKL → RANKL binds RANK on osteoclast precursors → osteoclast differentiation and activation → bone resorption releases calcium. (S33)
- **OPG** is a decoy receptor that blocks RANKL. Estrogen increases OPG; denosumab mimics OPG. Sclerostin inhibits Wnt signaling in osteoblasts. (S33)
- **PTH:** intermittent dosing favors formation; continuous exposure raises RANKL and resorption. (S33)
- **Calcium homeostasis:** PTH raises serum calcium (mobilizes bone calcium when levels drop); vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption; calcitonin lowers serum calcium and opposes PTH; calcium is the substrate being regulated (intake, bone storage, excretion). (S34)
- **PTH feedback loop:** falling serum calcium → PTH → raises calcium via bone resorption, renal reabsorption, and (via vitamin D activation) intestinal absorption; restored calcium inhibits further PTH. (S35; SG U2-R12)
- **Risk factors:** aging; estrogen deficiency (e.g., postmenopausal status); **chronic glucocorticoid use**; low calcium or vitamin D intake; low physical activity. (S36)
- [TB] About 98% of the body’s calcium is in bone; PTH, FGF23, and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D are the principal regulators. (p. 811; p. 812)
- [TB] Excess endogenous PTH nets bone resorption, but low intermittent doses build bone first — the basis for teriparatide. (p. 815)
- [TB] Glucocorticoids block vitamin D–driven gut calcium transport, raise renal calcium loss, block formation, and initially stimulate resorption. (p. 818)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Prototype + other drugs named
- Alendronate — oral bisphosphonate (first-line). (S37)
- Denosumab — RANKL monoclonal antibody. (S42)
- Teriparatide — PTH analog (anabolic). (S42)
- Raloxifene — SERM; romosozumab — sclerostin antibody. (S42)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Mechanism of action
- Bisphosphonates — **mechanism: inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.** They bind hydroxyapatite at resorption sites → osteoclasts take them up → nitrogen-containing agents inhibit **FPP synthase** (mevalonate pathway) → loss of prenylation of small GTPases → **osteoclast apoptosis**. (S37; S38)
- **Denosumab:** monoclonal antibody, **RANKL inhibitor** — blocks osteoclast formation. (S42; S43; SG U2-M)
- **Teriparatide** (PTH 1-34, daily injection) / **abaloparatide:** PTH-analog “anabolic” agents that stimulate osteoblasts and new bone formation. (S42; S43; SG U2-M)
- **Romosozumab:** sclerostin antibody that both builds bone and limits resorption — increases formation, decreases resorption → BMD up, fracture risk down. (S42; S43)
- **SERMs** — selective estrogen receptor modulators (raloxifene): estrogen-like effect on bone — antiresorptive, like calcitonin. (S42; S43)
- **Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis:** glucocorticoids increase osteoclast-mediated resorption and suppress osteoblast-driven formation. (S53)
- [TB] Amino-bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate) inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway. (p. 820)
- [TB] Denosumab does not kill osteoclasts and does not deposit in bone, so its effect reverses when stopped. (p. 820)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Indications
- **Bisphosphonates are first-line for most patients with osteoporosis.** (S37)
- Higher-risk patients may need an **anabolic agent first**. (S45)
- Raloxifene also reduces breast cancer risk. (S42; S43)
- [TB] Raloxifene protects against spine fractures but not hip fractures; bisphosphonates, denosumab, and teriparatide protect both. (p. 827)
- [TB] Vitamin D (800 IU/d) with calcium improves bone density, reduces falls, and prevents fractures — the base under every osteoporosis drug. (p. 827)
- [TB] Calcitonin is less effective (spine fractures only); bisphosphonates and calcitonin are first-line for Paget disease. (p. 828; p. 829)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Romosozumab: boxed warning for MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death.** (S43)
- [TB] Romosozumab: black box — contraindicated after MI or stroke within the past year. (p. 820)
- **Bisphosphonates:** esophageal irritation; hypocalcemia; osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare); atypical femoral fractures (rare, with long-term use). (S38; S40)
- **IV zoledronic acid:** acute-phase reaction (flu-like), mitigated with acetaminophen. (S41)
- **Raloxifene:** raises VTE risk; hot flashes. (S43)
- **Denosumab discontinuation causes rebound vertebral fractures** — transition to a bisphosphonate. (S43)
- [TB] Osteonecrosis of the jaw is rare at osteoporosis doses (~1/100,000 patient-years), more common with high-dose IV zoledronate for cancer. (p. 820)
- [TB] Denosumab: possible infection risk, transient hypocalcemia (CKD, vitamin D deficiency), and rebound resorption if interrupted. (p. 820)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Contraindications
- Bisphosphonates: contraindicated with severe renal impairment (CrCl below 30–35 mL/min) — some are avoided in significant renal impairment. (S38; S45)
- Oral bisphosphonates: avoid in esophageal stricture, achalasia, or inability to sit upright. (S41)
- [TB] Unlike bisphosphonates, denosumab can be used in advanced renal disease (not renally cleared) — watch calcium. (p. 820)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Drug interactions
- Food, calcium, coffee, and antacids reduce oral bisphosphonate absorption further. (S41)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Pharmacokinetics
- Bisphosphonates have a long skeletal half-life — the effect persists after stopping. (S38)
- Poor oral absorption (under 1%) → take fasting with plain water. (S41)
- **Dosing:** alendronate, risedronate — oral, typically weekly; ibandronate — oral, monthly; zoledronic acid — IV, as infrequently as once yearly. (S37; S39)
- Teriparatide is limited to 2 years lifetime, then follow with an antiresorptive. (S43)
- [TB] Bisphosphonates: less than 10% of an oral dose is absorbed; about half goes to bone, the rest is excreted unchanged in urine. (p. 819)
- [TB] Denosumab 60 mg SC every 6 months; teriparatide 20 mcg SC daily; romosozumab 210 mg SC monthly for one year, then an antiresorptive. (p. 819; p. 827)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Monitoring
- **Oral counseling:** take with a **full glass of water**, first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach well before food, and **remain upright** (sitting or standing) for the specified period after dosing (30 minutes). (S40; S41; SG U2-R13)
- Ensure calcium and vitamin D repletion before starting; correct hypocalcemia first. (S38; S41)
- Consider a **drug holiday after 3–5 years** of oral therapy (osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femur fracture with long-term use). (S38)
- [TB] Reassess after 5 years of bisphosphonate (3 for zoledronate); a drug holiday if fracture risk allows. (p. 820)

### Bone Pharmacology & Osteoporosis — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The “remain upright” point is extremely high-yield: esophageal injury is largely preventable with correct administration. (S40 (notes); S41)
- Dosing frequency is a major real-world driver of which bisphosphonate a patient actually takes consistently. (S39 (notes))
- Teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab are the **anabolic exception** — they actively build new bone; most other osteoporosis drugs just slow resorption. (S42 (notes); S44)
- Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis is especially high-yield — it connects to the corticosteroid use for OA/RA in Session 1. (S36 (notes))
- **Choosing therapy:** fracture risk, renal function, previous fracture history, glucocorticoid exposure, adherence (weekly oral vs. yearly IV vs. injectable), and route preference. This checklist is a direct echo of Lecture 1’s clinical decision-making framework. (S45; S45 (notes))
- Name clues: **-dronate** bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid = zoledronate); **-sumab** bone monoclonal antibodies (denosumab RANKL, romosozumab sclerostin); **-fene** SERM (raloxifene); **-tide** PTH analogs (teriparatide, abaloparatide); calcitonin has no shared stem (hormone). (S46)
