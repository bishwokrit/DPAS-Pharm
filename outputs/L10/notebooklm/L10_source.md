## Drug class: RAAS Blockers
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO2, IO5.1, IO5.2, IO5.4, IO5.5

### RAAS Blockers — Class
- **One pathway, four drug classes, and the preferred therapy for hypertension in CKD.** Today we follow the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, the pathway these drugs act on. (S3; S3 (notes))
- **Figure 8. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): angiotensinogen is converted to angiotensin I by renin, then to angiotensin II by ACE; angiotensin II drives both vasoconstriction and aldosterone release.** (S4; SG U5-R7)
- **Direct renin inhibitors, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and aldosterone antagonists each block a different point in this cascade.** (S4; SG U5-R7)
- This is the conceptual spine for the session: walk the cascade before naming drugs — angiotensinogen, renin, angiotensin I, ACE, angiotensin II, vasoconstriction and aldosterone. (S4 (notes))

### RAAS Blockers — Prototype + other drugs named
- ACE inhibitors: captopril, lisinopril, enalapril — "-pril" suffix across the class. ARBs: losartan, valsartan — "-sartan" suffix. Direct renin inhibitor: aliskiren. (S5; S7)

### RAAS Blockers — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: block the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, reducing both vasoconstriction and aldosterone release (Figure 8).** (S5)
- ACE also breaks down bradykinin: ACE inhibitor blocks ACE → less angiotensin II (less vasoconstriction and aldosterone) → less bradykinin breakdown, so bradykinin accumulates → cough or angioedema → switch to an ARB for cough. (S6; S6 (notes))
- ARBs block the AT1 receptor directly, rather than preventing angiotensin II formation. Aliskiren blocks the rate-limiting first step of the entire cascade. (S7)
- Renoprotection: by reducing angiotensin II, these drugs preferentially dilate the efferent (outflow) arteriole of the glomerulus, lowering intraglomerular pressure and reducing proteinuria — a direct, structural renoprotective effect beyond systemic BP control. (S8; SG U5-R8)
- Chain (Figure 10): angiotensin II normally constricts the efferent arteriole → ACE inhibitor or ARB removes that signal → efferent arteriole dilates; intraglomerular pressure falls → proteinuria falls; creatinine rises up to ~30%. (S9)

### RAAS Blockers — Indications
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs are first-line antihypertensive therapy in CKD, especially with proteinuria — not simply because they lower blood pressure.** (S8)
- ARBs: no bradykinin-related cough — preferred alternative when ACE inhibitor cough is intolerable. ARBs do not raise bradykinin, so they are the usual switch. (S6; S7)
- Aliskiren: limited clinical use; useful mainly as a teaching example of where a drug could act upstream of ACE — included for conceptual completeness on Figure 8. (S7; S7 (notes))
- The drug class students might expect to avoid in kidney disease is actually the preferred therapy, precisely because of how it works at the glomerulus. (S8 (notes))

### RAAS Blockers — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Adverse effects: dry cough (bradykinin accumulation — the single most common reason for switching to an ARB), angioedema (rare but potentially life-threatening), and hyperkalemia.** (S5)
- Cough is bradykinin-mediated, not allergic. Angioedema is rare but can be life-threatening: stop the drug. (S6)
- An initial rise in serum creatinine of up to ~30% after starting therapy is expected and acceptable — reflects reduced intraglomerular pressure, not drug-induced kidney injury; a larger rise warrants evaluation for renal artery stenosis. (S5; SG U5-R9)
- Students frequently get the creatinine point wrong: many want to stop the ACE inhibitor at the first rise. It is a predictable hemodynamic effect, not toxicity — the mechanism and the "side effect" are two faces of the same action. (S5 (notes); S8; S9 (notes))

### RAAS Blockers — Contraindications
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy — fetal renal toxicity; avoid in women who are or may become pregnant.** ARBs share the same pregnancy contraindication and hyperkalemia risk as ACE inhibitors. (S5; S7)
- Aliskiren: generally not combined with an ACE inhibitor or ARB due to additive hyperkalemia/hypotension risk with no added benefit — never in diabetes. (S7; S7 (notes))

### RAAS Blockers — Drug interactions
- Potassium supplements, potassium-sparing diuretics and trimethoprim: hyperkalemia with ACE inhibitors and ARBs. NSAIDs blunt antihypertensives and raise AKI risk with an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus diuretic. (S55; S55 (notes))
- [TB] Hyperkalemia with potassium-sparing diuretics rises with drugs that cut renin or angiotensin II: β blockers, NSAIDs, aliskiren, ACE inhibitors, ARBs. (p. 275)

### RAAS Blockers — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Enalapril’s ACE-inhibition half-life is about 4 hours, yet once-daily dosing works: concentrations stay far above the C50, so ACE stays inhibited. (p. 50)

### RAAS Blockers — Monitoring
- **Monitor potassium and renal function within 1–2 weeks of starting or up-titrating therapy, and periodically thereafter.** (S8; S9 (notes))
- **Hold or use with caution during acute illness causing volume depletion ("sick day rules") — combined with reduced renal perfusion, RAAS blockade can precipitate acute kidney injury.** (S8)
- Sick-day rules: hold the ACE inhibitor or ARB during vomiting, diarrhea or dehydration. An early creatinine rise is expected and hemodynamic, not nephrotoxicity; a larger rise warrants checking for volume depletion or renal artery stenosis. (S9; SG U5-R9)

### RAAS Blockers — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: a patient with hypertension and diabetic nephropathy is started on lisinopril; two weeks later serum creatinine has risen from 1.1 to 1.4 mg/dL. **Is this an indication to stop the ACE inhibitor?** (S10)
- **A rise of roughly 27% is within the expected range for ACE inhibitor initiation, reflecting reduced intraglomerular pressure from efferent arteriolar dilation — not drug toxicity.** (S10; S10 (notes))
- **Therapy should generally continue with ongoing monitoring; a rise beyond approximately 30%, or a much sharper increase, should prompt evaluation for renal artery stenosis or volume depletion.** (S10)
- A student who reflexively says "stop the drug" has missed that this creatinine rise is expected and even reflects the mechanism working as intended. (S10 (notes))

### RAAS Blockers — Memory hooks
- Why it happens: Why does creatinine rise on an ACE inhibitor? The drug lowers glomerular pressure on purpose — that same drop protects the kidney from proteinuria. (S8; S9; SG U5-R8)
- Mnemonic (memory aid): Thirty is fine, more is a sign: up to ~30% creatinine rise → continue; more → look for renal artery stenosis or volume depletion. (S5; S10; SG U5-R9)

### RAAS Blockers — High-yield summary
- ACEi/ARB first-line in proteinuric CKD: efferent dilation lowers intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria. (S8; SG U5-R8)
- Creatinine rise up to ~30% is expected; check K⁺ and creatinine in 1–2 weeks; sick-day rules; never in pregnancy. (S5; S8; S9; SG U5-R9)

## Drug class: Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension
Learning objectives covered: SO3, IO5.1, IO5.2, IO5.3, IO5.4

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Class
- **RAAS blockers are first-line — but rarely the whole story.** Other major classes: CCBs, beta-blockers, diuretics, central agents and direct vasodilators. (S11; S11 (notes); S12 (notes))
- This four-card slide is a brief classification recap — CCBs and beta-blockers get fuller treatment later this session (angina) and in cardiovascular coursework. (S12 (notes))

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Prototype + other drugs named
- Calcium channel blockers: dihydropyridines (amlodipine) and non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem, verapamil). Beta-blockers: metoprolol, atenolol. Central α2 agonists: clonidine, methyldopa. Direct arteriolar vasodilator: hydralazine. (S12; S28)

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Mechanism of action
- Dihydropyridines (amlodipine): arteriolar dilation. Non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem, verapamil): also ↓ HR/contractility. (S12)
- Beta-blockers: ↓ heart rate, ↓ contractility, ↓ renin release. (S12)
- Chain (Figure 11): dihydropyridine arteriolar dilation → afterload falls; blood pressure lowers. Non-DHP CCB or beta-blocker: heart rate and contractility fall → renin release falls. (S13; S13 (notes))
- Clonidine or methyldopa stimulates central α2 receptors → sympathetic outflow falls, so BP falls. Hydralazine dilates arterioles directly → reflex tachycardia and fluid retention. (S16; S16 (notes))

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Indications
- Beta-blockers: not generally first-line for uncomplicated HTN, but compelling indications include concurrent CAD/heart failure/arrhythmia. (S12)
- If all these classes lower blood pressure similarly, why are RAAS blockers preferred in CKD? Similar BP lowering; ACE inhibitors and ARBs add renoprotection — the renoprotective gap in Figure 12, not a difference in BP control. (S14; S14 (notes))
- Thiazide / loop diuretics (covered in depth in Session 1): thiazides first-line with preserved renal function; loops preferred as GFR falls. (S12)
- Central agents (clonidine, methyldopa) and hydralazine: reserved for resistant hypertension or specific situations (e.g., methyldopa in pregnancy). (S12; S16)
- [TB] Diuretics enhance the efficacy of many agents, particularly ACE inhibitors; hydralazine and minoxidil need a diuretic because they cause salt and water retention. (p. 280)
- [TB] Spironolactone may be the most effective single agent for drug-resistant hypertension. (p. 280)

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- Peripheral edema is a CCB class adverse effect. (S12)
- Hydralazine: reflex tachycardia and fluid retention — must be paired with a beta-blocker and a diuretic. (S16; S16 (notes))
- Abrupt clonidine withdrawal causes rebound hypertension. (S16; S16 (notes))

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Contraindications
- Atenolol accumulates in renal impairment (reduce dose when CrCl is below 35 mL/min); beta-blockers: do not stop abruptly. (S47; S48)

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Drug interactions
- Diltiazem or verapamil with a beta-blocker risks bradycardia and AV block; dihydropyridines pair with beta-blockers without that conduction risk. (S13; S13 (notes))
- Verapamil and diltiazem raise digoxin levels. Rifampin and carbamazepine lower CCB levels; grapefruit juice raises dihydropyridine levels. (S55; S55 (notes))

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Pharmacokinetics
- Not covered in materials.

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Monitoring
- **Resistant hypertension (BP above goal despite three or more antihypertensive classes, including a diuretic) is common in CKD, often driven by volume overload.** (S15)
- First step: confirm diuretic therapy is adequate — a thiazide may simply no longer be effective at the patient’s current GFR (Session 1’s loop-vs-thiazide cutoff). (S15; S15 (notes))
- Switching from a thiazide to a loop diuretic, or increasing loop diuretic dosing, is often the most effective single change in advanced CKD. (S15)
- **Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone) can be added for resistant hypertension, but require close potassium and renal-function monitoring, especially on top of an ACE inhibitor or ARB.** (S15)

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- "Resistant hypertension in CKD" is often really a diuretic-selection problem, not a need for an entirely new drug class — check diuretic adequacy first; thiazide to loop switch; add spironolactone with close monitoring. (S15 (notes))
- Case: CKD (eGFR 28 mL/min), still hypertensive despite lisinopril, amlodipine, and hydrochlorothiazide. **What is the most likely contributor to inadequate control, and what change would you suggest first?** (S17)
- **At this GFR, hydrochlorothiazide has largely lost its natriuretic efficacy (Session 1). Switching to a loop diuretic such as furosemide or torsemide is usually the most effective next step before adding an entirely new drug class.** (S17)
- This case is a direct callback to the Session 1 "Choosing a Diuretic for Advancing CKD" case — point out the parallel if you remember it. (S17 (notes))

- Why it happens: Why pair hydralazine with a beta-blocker and a diuretic? Direct dilation triggers reflex tachycardia and fluid retention; the partners cancel both. (S16)

### Other Antihypertensives & Resistant Hypertension — High-yield summary
- Non-DHP CCB + beta-blocker → bradycardia/AV block; DHP + beta-blocker is fine. (S13)
- Resistant HTN in CKD: fix the diuretic first (thiazide → loop), then spironolactone with K⁺ monitoring. (S15; S17)

## Drug class: Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy
Learning objectives covered: SO4, SO5, IO5.1, IO5.2, IO5.3, IO5.6

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Class
- **Where cardiovascular and renal pharmacology converge — and where that convergence can go wrong.** This section covers vasodilators and angina, then the cardiorenal overlap. (S18; S18 (notes))
- **Figure 15. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): angina results when myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply; nitrates reduce preload, dihydropyridine CCBs reduce afterload,** (S21)
- **non-dihydropyridine CCBs also reduce heart rate/contractility and increase coronary flow, and beta-blockers reduce heart rate and contractility.** (S21)
- This figure organizes every antianginal drug class by exactly which side of the supply/demand equation it affects — sort nitrates, each CCB subtype, and beta-blockers onto it from memory. (S21 (notes))

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Prototype + other drugs named
- Nitroglycerin (sublingual for acute angina; IV/patch for sustained use), isosorbide mononitrate/dinitrate (oral, longer-acting). (S19)

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: release nitric oxide, activating guanylate cyclase and increasing cGMP, producing smooth muscle relaxation — predominantly venodilation at usual doses, reducing preload and myocardial oxygen demand.** (S19; SG U5-R11)
- Chain (Figure 14): nitrate donates nitric oxide (NO) → NO activates guanylate cyclase, so cGMP rises → cGMP relaxes smooth muscle: venodilation → preload and oxygen demand fall. (S20; S20 (notes))
- **Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine) — predominantly arteriolar dilation, reducing afterload; the same mechanism used for hypertension (Section 5).** (S22; SG U5-R10)
- **Non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem, verapamil) — also reduce heart rate and contractility and increase coronary blood flow; avoid combining with beta-blockers due to additive bradycardia/AV block risk.** (S22)

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Indications
- CCBs are the clearest example in the unit of the same drug class, same mechanism, treating two different conditions (hypertension and angina). (S22; SG U5-R10)
- This is the deliberate cardiovascular-renal "overlap" moment the lecture title promises — calcium channel blockers are not a separate topic from the antihypertensive content in Section 5; they are the same drugs doing double duty. (S22 (notes))

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Adverse effects: headache (vasodilation), reflex tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension.** (S19)
- **Adverse effects: peripheral edema and reflex tachycardia (dihydropyridines); bradycardia, AV block, and constipation (non-dihydropyridines, especially verapamil).** (S22)

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Contraindications
- **Absolute contraindication: concurrent use with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil and similar) — the combination can cause life-threatening hypotension.** (S19; SG U5-R11)
- PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil block cGMP breakdown, causing severe hypotension with nitrates. (S20; S20 (notes))
- The PDE5-inhibitor interaction is one of the highest-stakes medication-safety points in this whole unit — a real-world, repeatedly tested safety question worth a memorable callout. (S19 (notes))

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Drug interactions
- **Figure 16. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): an NSAID (afferent arteriolar constriction), an ACE inhibitor or ARB (efferent arteriolar dilation), and a diuretic (volume depletion) each independently reduce glomerular filtration pressure —** (S23; SG U5-R12)
- **combined, the effect on kidney perfusion is additive to synergistic, substantially raising acute kidney injury risk.** An extremely common, often missed, entirely preventable cause of AKI. (S23; S23 (notes); SG U5-R12)
- Three hits (Figure 17): NSAID constricts the afferent arteriole → ACE inhibitor or ARB dilates the efferent arteriole → diuretic causes volume depletion → filtration pressure collapses: AKI. (S24; S24 (notes))
- [TB] NSAIDs blunt loop and thiazide action by reducing renal prostaglandins — significant in nephrotic syndrome or cirrhosis. (p. 270; p. 272)

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Pharmacokinetics
- Not covered in materials.

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Monitoring
- Tolerance develops with continuous exposure — a nitrate-free interval (typically overnight) is required to maintain efficacy with long-acting formulations. (S19; S20)
- Prevention: counsel patients about over-the-counter NSAIDs — ask which OTC NSAIDs they commonly take. (S24; S24 (notes))

### Nitrates, Angina & the Triple Whammy — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The triple whammy is the capstone cardiorenal interaction: Session 1’s diuretics + this session’s RAAS blockers + NSAIDs students will meet constantly in practice. (S23 (notes))
- Hypertension → CKD: uncontrolled blood pressure causes glomerular capillary injury and nephrosclerosis over time — a leading cause of CKD progression, alongside diabetes. (S25; S25 (notes))
- CKD → hypertension: reduced renal function causes volume overload and RAAS activation, which worsens and complicates blood pressure control, often requiring multiple drug classes. (S25; S25 (notes))
- **Figure 18. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): hypertension and chronic kidney disease drive one another in a self-reinforcing cycle — uncontrolled blood pressure causes glomerular injury and CKD progression,** (S26)
- **while CKD causes volume overload and RAAS activation that worsens hypertension.** (S26)
- Figure 18 is the conceptual closer for the whole unit — tie it back to Figure 8: RAAS blockade is effective precisely because it intervenes in both directions of this cycle at once. (S26 (notes))
- Case: an older adult on lisinopril and hydrochlorothiazide starts a regular OTC NSAID for a knee injury; one week later, lethargy and an acute rise in creatinine. **What is the most likely explanation, and how could this have been prevented?** (S27)
- **This is a classic "triple whammy" presentation (Figure 16) — the NSAID, ACE inhibitor, and diuretic each independently reduced glomerular filtration pressure, and together precipitated acute kidney injury.** (S27; SG U5-S)
- **Prevention requires counseling patients on RAAS blockers and diuretics to avoid regular NSAID use and to seek guidance before starting one, even an over-the-counter product.** (S27; SG U5-S)
- This case is the direct clinical application of Figure 16 and reinforces IO5.6 (patient counseling): the preventable element is entirely what the patient was or wasn’t told about OTC NSAID use. (S27 (notes))
- Same patient, eGFR now 25: hydrochlorothiazide has likely lost much of its efficacy — switching to a loop diuretic (furosemide, torsemide) is more effective for blood pressure regardless of the NSAID issue. (SG U5-S)
- Counsel the patient to avoid regular OTC NSAID use and to check with a prescriber or pharmacist before starting any new OTC product, given the RAAS blocker + diuretic combination already in place. (SG U5-S)

### Putting It All Together: Renal Toolkit — How it works
- **1.** Diuretic classes (Session 1) and RAAS-blocking antihypertensives both map onto specific, drug-targetable points in renal physiology — the nephron (Figure 1) and the RAAS cascade (Figure 8), respectively. (S31)
- **2.** ACE inhibitors and ARBs are preferred first-line therapy for hypertension in CKD because of their renoprotective efferent-arteriolar mechanism, not just their blood-pressure-lowering effect. (S31)
- **3.** Calcium channel blockers exemplify the cardiovascular-renal overlap directly — the same drug class, same mechanism, treats both hypertension and angina. (S31)
- **4.** The "triple whammy" (NSAID + ACEi/ARB + diuretic) is a classic, common, and preventable cause of acute kidney injury that draws on content from both sessions at once. (S31)
- **5.** Hypertension and chronic kidney disease exist in a bidirectional, self-reinforcing cycle (Figure 18) — which is exactly why managing one well so often means managing the other. (S31)
- All three threads center on glomerular filtration pressure. (S31 (notes))
- Connecting back to earlier units: drug-metabolism/dosing concepts (Unit 1) and the biologic-mechanism callbacks from Units 2–4 all reinforce that pharmacology is cumulative — the cardiorenal interface is where several organ-system units visibly intersect. (S31 (notes))

### Putting It All Together: Renal Toolkit — Clinical relevance
- **Patient A — advancing CKD, hypertension no longer controlled on a thiazide:** switch to a loop diuretic; check potassium and volume status. (S30; S15; S17)
- **Patient B — proteinuric CKD, needs first-line antihypertensive therapy:** ACE inhibitor or ARB; check potassium and creatinine in 1–2 weeks and pregnancy potential. (S30; S5; S8)
