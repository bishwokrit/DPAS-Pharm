## Drug class: Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO4, IO4.1, IO4.2, IO4.4, IO4.6

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Class
- **Suppressing, replacing, or selectively modulating the reproductive axis.** Today the same negative-feedback logic explains contraception. (S3; S3 (notes))
- **Figure 11. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): exogenous estrogen and progestin suppress GnRH pulsatility and the FSH/LH surge, preventing the dominant-follicle development and LH surge required for ovulation.** (S4; S4 (notes))
- This is the same negative-feedback template from Figure 1 (Session 1), now applied to the HPG axis. (S4 (notes))

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Prototype + other drugs named
- Combined oral contraceptives (ethinyl estradiol plus a progestin); progestin-only: norethindrone "minipill," depot medroxyprogesterone, levonorgestrel IUD; SERMs tamoxifen, raloxifene; testosterone; finasteride; spironolactone. (S5; S7)

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: estrogen + progestin suppress GnRH/FSH/LH, preventing ovulation (Figure 11); progestin also thickens cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.** (S5; S6 (notes))
- Chain (Figure 12): ethinyl estradiol plus progestin enter the bloodstream → hypothalamus lowers GnRH pulses; pituitary lowers FSH and LH → no follicle maturation and no LH surge, so no ovulation → result: pregnancy prevented (over 99% with perfect use). (S6; S4 (notes))
- SERMs: tamoxifen or raloxifene bind the estrogen receptor → the receptor shape recruits different co-factors in each tissue. (S8)
- Breast: blocked (antagonist), so cancer growth is slowed. Bone: activated (agonist), so bone density is kept. Endometrium: tamoxifen activates (cancer risk); raloxifene blocks. (S8)
- Testes make testosterone → 5-alpha-reductase converts it to DHT in prostate and skin → DHT drives prostate growth and hair loss → finasteride blocks 5-alpha-reductase → lower DHT: smaller prostate, less hair loss. (S9; S9 (notes))

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Indications
- Combined contraception is available as oral pills, transdermal patch, or vaginal ring. (S5)
- Tamoxifen — antagonist in breast tissue (breast cancer treatment/prevention), agonist in endometrium (raises endometrial cancer risk). Tissue-selective activity is the defining feature of this class. (S7; S7 (notes))
- Raloxifene — antagonist in breast/endometrium, agonist in bone (osteoporosis prevention) — ties back to Unit 2 bone pharmacology; prevents vertebral fracture; not used to treat breast cancer. (S7; S8)
- Testosterone — replacement for hypogonadism; monitor hematocrit (erythrocytosis risk) and lipids. Finasteride — 5-α-reductase inhibitor; reduces conversion of testosterone to DHT (BPH, androgenetic alopecia). (S7)
- Spironolactone — antiandrogen effect at high doses; used off-label for hirsutism/acne (same drug as the mineralocorticoid antagonist from Session 1): same molecule, different receptor, different clinical use. (S7; S7 (notes); S9)

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- Combined pills: risks include venous thromboembolism, hypertension and stroke. Common effects: nausea, breast tenderness, breakthrough bleeding. (S6)
- Tamoxifen raises endometrial cancer and VTE risk and causes hot flashes; patients should report vaginal bleeding. Raloxifene does not increase endometrial cancer, but raises VTE risk and hot flashes. (S8; S8 (notes))
- Finasteride lowers PSA by about half, so double the PSA value when screening; sexual side effects occur. (S9; S9 (notes))

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Contraindications
- **Contraindications: history of VTE/stroke, estrogen-sensitive cancer, uncontrolled hypertension, migraine with aura, and smokers age 35 or older (increased thromboembolic risk).** Liver disease too. (S5; S5 (notes))
- Smoking at age 35+ substantially raises thromboembolic/cardiovascular risk with estrogen — the basis of the contraindication. (S5; SG U4-R10)
- "Smoker over 35" and "migraine with aura" are the classic exam triggers for choosing a progestin-only method — smokers 35 or older, migraine with aura and prior VTE rule out estrogen. (S5 (notes); S6)
- Progestin-only options (e.g., the "minipill," depot medroxyprogesterone (bone density warning), levonorgestrel IUD) avoid estrogen-related thromboembolic risk and are preferred when estrogen is contraindicated. (S5; S6; S6 (notes))
- Both SERMs are contraindicated with prior VTE. Finasteride is teratogenic: pregnant women should not handle crushed or broken tablets. Testosterone: avoid in prostate cancer. (S8; S9; S9 (notes))

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Drug interactions
- Enzyme inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John’s wort, some antiepileptics) lower contraceptive efficacy; use backup contraception. (S5 (notes); S6; S67)
- CYP2D6 inhibitors (paroxetine, fluoxetine) blunt tamoxifen activation. (S8 (notes); S67)

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Pharmacokinetics

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Monitoring
- Testosterone replacement: monitor hematocrit, PSA and lipids. (S9; S9 (notes))

### Gonadal Hormones & Inhibitors — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The contraindication list is the highest-yield content in contraception. (S5 (notes))

## Drug class: Insulin
Learning objectives covered: SO2, IO4.1, IO4.2, IO4.5, IO4.6

### Insulin — Class
- **Many mechanisms, one shared goal: glycemic control without excess hypoglycemia.** Today we cover the many ways to lower glucose. (S10; S10 (notes))
- **Figure 15. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): glucose entry raises ATP, closing K-ATP channels and triggering depolarization and calcium-dependent insulin release;** (S11; S11 (notes))
- **sulfonylureas/meglitinides force channel closure directly, while GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors amplify glucose-dependent release via the incretin pathway.**  (S11)
- This is the mechanistic foundation for the insulin secretagogue and incretin-based drug classes that follow — the "glucose-dependent" nature of incretin-based therapy is exactly why these agents carry lower hypoglycemia risk than sulfonylureas. (S11 (notes))
- [TB] Diabetes mellitus has four categories: type 1 (beta-cell destruction, absolute insulin deficiency), type 2, other specific types, and gestational diabetes (about 7% of US pregnancies). (p. 789; p. 790)
- [TB] Diagnosis: fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥6.5%, confirmed by repeat testing. (p. 790)

### Insulin — Prototype + other drugs named
- Insulin lispro, aspart (rapid); regular (short); NPH (intermediate); glargine, detemir, degludec (long-acting). (S12)

### Insulin — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: exogenous insulin activates the insulin receptor, promoting cellular glucose uptake and suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis.** (S12)
- Chain (Figure 16): insulin binds the insulin receptor on muscle, fat and liver cells → receptor autophosphorylation starts the signal cascade → GLUT4 moves to the cell surface so glucose enters. (S13; S13 (notes))
- Then the liver stores glycogen and stops making glucose → blood glucose falls (hypoglycemia is the main risk). (S13)

### Insulin — Indications
- Rapid-acting (lispro, aspart) — mealtime dosing; onset ~15 minutes. Short-acting (regular) — onset ~30 minutes. (S12)
- Intermediate-acting (NPH) — cloudy suspension; more variable absorption. Long-acting (glargine, detemir, degludec) — basal coverage; relatively peakless profile. (S12)
- Rapid-acting is given with meals; regular 30 minutes before. NPH peaks at about 4–10 hours; glargine, detemir and degludec are basal. Only regular insulin is given IV. (S12 (notes); S13)
- Sort any named insulin into rapid, short, intermediate or long-acting before discussing regimens — the scaffold for every insulin question. (S12 (notes))
- [TB] Glucagon (IM, SC, IV, or 3-mg nasal powder) treats severe hypoglycemia when oral glucose is impossible; place the patient on their side (vomiting). (p. 789)

### Insulin — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Primary adverse effect across all insulins: hypoglycemia — counsel patients on recognition and treatment (rapid-acting carbohydrate).** (S12)
- Adverse effects: hypoglycemia, weight gain, lipohypertrophy, hypokalemia. Never share pens. (S13)
- [TB] Rotate injection sites to avoid lipohypertrophy; insulin allergy (IgE) is rare. (p. 794; p. 795)

### Insulin — Contraindications
- Not covered in materials.

### Insulin — Drug interactions
- Beta-blockers can mask hypoglycemia warning symptoms. Glucagon and dextrose: rescue for severe hypoglycemia. (S12 (notes); S13; S67)

### Insulin — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Insulin preparations: lispro/aspart onset 5–15 min, 3–4 h duration; regular 30–60 min, 6–8 h; NPH 2–4 h, peak 6–7 h; glargine about 24 h, peakless. (p. 791; p. 793)
- [TB] Glargine is acidic (pH 4.0) — never mix it in the same syringe with other insulins. (p. 793; p. 794)

### Insulin — Monitoring
- Rule of 15 for hypoglycemia: 15 g carbohydrate, recheck in 15 minutes. (S12 (notes); S13)

### Insulin — Clinical pearls / "why" hook

## Drug class: Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs
Learning objectives covered: SO2, SO4, IO4.1, IO4.2, IO4.3, IO4.4, IO4.5

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Class
- Four non-incretin oral cards: metformin, sulfonylureas/meglitinides, TZDs, SGLT2 inhibitors; then the incretin classes (GLP-1 RAs, DPP-4 inhibitors) and the site-of-action map. (S14; S14 (notes))
- **Figure 19. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): glucose-lowering drug classes act at distinct sites — pancreatic β-cell, liver, muscle/fat, kidney, gut, and the incretin axis — each converging on overall blood glucose control.** (S17; S17 (notes))

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Prototype + other drugs named
- Metformin; glipizide, glyburide (sulfonylureas); repaglinide (meglitinide); pioglitazone (TZD); empagliflozin (SGLT2); exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide (GLP-1 RA); sitagliptin, linagliptin (DPP-4). (S14; S18)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Mechanism of action
- Metformin decreases hepatic gluconeogenesis: enters hepatocytes through OCT1 → inhibits mitochondrial complex I and activates AMPK → gluconeogenesis falls → insulin sensitivity improves in muscle → fasting glucose falls without hypoglycemia. (S14; S15; S15 (notes))
- Sulfonylureas / meglitinides force K-ATP channel closure — insulin secretagogues (glipizide, glyburide, repaglinide). TZDs (pioglitazone) increase peripheral insulin sensitivity. (S14)
- Match each drug class to its mechanism/site: metformin — ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis; sulfonylureas — force K-ATP channel closure (insulin secretagogue); TZDs — ↑ peripheral insulin sensitivity; SGLT2 inhibitors — block renal glucose reabsorption. (SG U4-M; SG U4-R11)
- GLP-1 receptor agonists — glucose-dependent insulin release, slowed gastric emptying; DPP-4 inhibitors — prevent endogenous GLP-1 breakdown (incretin pathway). Sulfonylureas carry the highest hypoglycemia risk (glucose-independent insulin release). (SG U4-M; SG U4-R11)
- SGLT2 inhibitors block renal glucose reabsorption: glucose is filtered at the glomerulus → SGLT2 in the proximal tubule normally reabsorbs about 90% → empagliflozin blocks SGLT2. (S14; S16)
- Glucose and water are lost in the urine (glucosuria) → glucose, weight and blood pressure fall; infection risk rises. (S16; S16 (notes))
- GLP-1 receptor agonists: glucose-dependent insulin release, slow gastric emptying, promote satiety/weight loss. DPP-4 inhibitors prevent breakdown of endogenous GLP-1, prolonging its glucose-dependent effect. (S18; S18 (notes))
- Incretin chain (Figure 20): food in the gut releases GLP-1 → GLP-1 acts on the beta cell: glucose-dependent insulin release, less glucagon → DPP-4 normally breaks GLP-1 down quickly. (S19; S19 (notes))
- Sitagliptin blocks DPP-4; semaglutide and liraglutide mimic GLP-1 → lower glucose, slower gastric emptying and less appetite. (S19)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Indications
- Metformin: first-line for type 2 diabetes; no weight gain and low hypoglycemia risk. (S14; S15; S15 (notes))
- SGLT2 inhibitors: cardiorenal benefit — benefits in heart failure and chronic kidney disease, beyond glucose lowering. (S14; S16; S16 (notes))
- **Figure 21. Original illustrative comparison (illustrative relative values, not from Katzung): insulin and sulfonylureas show the greatest HbA1c-lowering efficacy but also carry the highest hypoglycemia risk,** (S20)
- **while metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors show a more favorable efficacy-to-hypoglycemia-risk balance.**  (S20)
- Figure 21 is the quantified summary of the drug-class discussion — it answers "why is metformin still first-line": not because it is the most potent, but because of its favorable risk profile. (S20 (notes))
- [TB] Semaglutide lowers HbA1c 1.5–1.8%; liraglutide 3 mg and semaglutide 2.4 mg are approved for weight loss without diabetes. (p. 800)
- [TB] SGLT2 inhibitors lower HbA1c 0.5–1%, cause 2–5 kg weight loss, and reduce heart-failure events (dapagliflozin hazard ratio 0.74). (p. 801; p. 802)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- Sulfonylureas: highest hypoglycemia risk among oral agents. TZDs: fluid retention/heart failure, weight gain. (S14)
- Metformin: GI upset is common — start low and take with food; vitamin B12 deficiency with long-term use; monitor B12 levels. (S15; S15 (notes))
- SGLT2 inhibitors: genital yeast (mycotic) infections, volume depletion, euglycemic DKA — hold when ill or before surgery; rare Fournier gangrene. (S14; S16; S16 (notes))
- GLP-1 RAs: nausea/GI upset, vomiting, pancreatitis risk and gallstones. DPP-4 inhibitors are generally weight-neutral, well tolerated, with low hypoglycemia risk. (S18; S18 (notes); S19)
- [TB] Sulfonylureas: hypoglycemia and weight gain; elderly patients are at particular risk. (p. 796; p. 797)
- [TB] Acarbose (an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, a drug that affects absorption of glucose): treat hypoglycemia with glucose, not sucrose. (p. 799)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Contraindications
- Metformin: avoid in significant renal impairment — lactic acidosis risk; avoid when eGFR is below 30; hold for contrast dye with reduced kidney function (iodinated contrast). (S14; S15; S15 (notes); S67)
- GLP-1 RAs: contraindicated with personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2 (thyroid C-cell warning). (S18; S18 (notes); S19)
- SGLT2 inhibitors: avoid for glycemic effect when eGFR is low. (S16)
- [TB] Metformin: full dose at eGFR 45–60, cautious at 30–45, contraindicated below 30; avoid in decompensated liver failure; check renal function at least annually. (p. 798)
- [TB] Thiazolidinediones are contraindicated in NYHA class III and IV heart failure. (p. 799)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Drug interactions
- Do not combine a GLP-1 RA with a DPP-4 inhibitor. Gemfibrozil raises repaglinide levels (CYP2C8). (S19; S67)

### Non-Insulin Glucose-Lowering Drugs — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Sitagliptin: reduce dose in renal impairment (50 mg at eGFR 30–50, 25 mg below 30). Exenatide is not approved below eGFR 30. (p. 800; p. 801)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Class
- **A brief recap of bone pharmacology, then a new angle: PTH itself.** Unit 2 covered osteoporosis drug therapy in depth: bisphosphonates, denosumab, teriparatide, and raloxifene (a SERM, also discussed again this session). (S22; S23)
- That therapy targets bone remodeling directly, generally in patients with normal parathyroid function. **This section takes a different angle: what happens — and what drugs are used — when parathyroid hormone secretion itself is the primary problem.** (S23; S23 (notes))
- **If you need a refresher on bisphosphonate dosing or denosumab safety, revisit the Unit 2, Session 2 materials — that content is not repeated here.** (S23)
- This section is intentionally NOT re-teaching osteoporosis pharmacotherapy — it is new content on parathyroid pathology, so don’t expect bisphosphonate-level detail here. (S23 (notes))
- **Figure 22. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): hyperparathyroidism (excess PTH, high calcium) is treated with cinacalcet to lower PTH,** (S24)
- **while hypoparathyroidism (deficient PTH, low calcium) is treated with calcitriol and oral calcium, sometimes with recombinant PTH 1-84 in severe cases.** (S24)
- Figure 22 is the organizing concept for the section — two opposite pathologies, two opposite drug strategies, both targeting the same hormone axis. (S24 (notes))

### Parathyroid Disorders — Prototype + other drugs named
- Cinacalcet (calcimimetic); calcitriol (active vitamin D) plus calcium; recombinant human PTH 1-84. (S25)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Mechanism of action
- Cinacalcet — a calcimimetic; activates the calcium-sensing receptor on parathyroid cells, reducing PTH secretion. (S25)
- Chain (Figure 23): parathyroid cells sense calcium through the CaSR → low calcium keeps PTH release high → cinacalcet makes the receptor more sensitive to calcium. (S26)
- The cell reads calcium as high and lowers PTH → PTH and serum calcium fall. (S26; S26 (notes))
- Calcitriol (active vitamin D) plus oral calcium — mainstay for hypoparathyroidism, since PTH deficiency impairs vitamin D activation; calcitriol bypasses the PTH activation step. (S25; S25 (notes))

### Parathyroid Disorders — Indications
- Cinacalcet: used for secondary hyperparathyroidism (e.g., in chronic kidney disease on dialysis) and parathyroid carcinoma. (S25; S26)
- Recombinant human PTH 1-84 — reserved for patients not adequately controlled on calcium/calcitriol alone. (S25)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- Cinacalcet adverse effects: nausea, hypocalcemia and QT prolongation, so monitor calcium. (S26)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Contraindications
- Not covered in materials.

### Parathyroid Disorders — Drug interactions
- Cinacalcet is a CYP2D6 inhibitor. Thiazides raise calcium, loops lower it. Vitamin D (cholecalciferol) replaces or supports calcitriol in deficiency. (S26; S67)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Pharmacokinetics
- Not covered in materials.

### Parathyroid Disorders — Monitoring
- Hyperparathyroidism — cinacalcet (lowers PTH secretion); hypoparathyroidism — calcitriol (bypasses the PTH-dependent vitamin D activation step) plus oral calcium. (SG U4-R13)
- Opposite monitoring targets: watch for hypocalcemia with cinacalcet, hypercalcemia/hypercalciuria with calcitriol/PTH replacement — a symmetry worth drawing out. (S25; S25 (notes); S26)

### Parathyroid Disorders — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: a patient with chronic kidney disease is found to have an elevated PTH level with low-normal serum calcium. A second, unrelated patient who underwent recent thyroidectomy develops perioral numbness and low serum calcium with a low PTH level. (S27)
- **How does drug therapy differ between these two patients?** (S27)
- **The CKD patient has secondary hyperparathyroidism and may benefit from cinacalcet to reduce PTH secretion.** (S27)
- **The post-thyroidectomy patient has surgical hypoparathyroidism (inadvertent parathyroid gland injury) and needs calcitriol plus calcium replacement — the opposite pharmacologic strategy for the opposite underlying problem.** (S27; S27 (notes))
- Post-thyroidectomy hypoparathyroidism is a classic, testable scenario and a bridge back to thyroid surgery from Session 1 (antithyroid drugs can also precede surgical management). (S27 (notes))

## Topic: Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit

### Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit — Key definitions
- **Applying the whole Endocrine pharmacology toolkit.** The shared principle is negative feedback suppression by exogenous hormone. For each patient, name a first-line approach AND the single biggest safety concern. (S29; S29 (notes); S30 (notes))

### Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit — How it works
- **1.** The hypothalamic-pituitary-target gland axis is a shared template across thyroid, adrenal, and gonadal pharmacology — exogenous hormone therapy suppresses the levels above it via negative feedback. (S31)
- **2.** Antithyroid drug selection (methimazole vs. PTU) and glucocorticoid tapering both hinge on understanding exactly where and how each drug intervenes in its pathway. (S31)
- **3.** Glucose-lowering drugs act at distinct sites (pancreas, liver, muscle/fat, kidney, gut, incretin axis) — drug selection balances efficacy against hypoglycemia and other class-specific risks. (S31)
- **4.** Hyperparathyroidism and hypoparathyroidism require opposite drug strategies (lowering vs. raising effective PTH activity) — a new angle distinct from, but related to, the osteoporosis pharmacotherapy of Unit 2. (S31)
- **5.** Endocrine pharmacology is deeply interconnected — spironolactone, dopamine agonists, and SERMs each reappear across more than one organ system in this unit alone. (S31)
- Connecting back: osteoporosis pharmacology (Unit 2) and drug-metabolism/interaction concepts (Unit 1) both reappear here — pharmacology is cumulative, not siloed by unit. (S31 (notes))

### Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit — Clinical relevance
- **Patient A — Graves disease, in thyroid storm:** PTU (blocks synthesis and peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion); check liver toxicity. (S30; S35)
- **Patient B — chronic prednisone user, abruptly out of refills:** restart the glucocorticoid and taper; the risk is adrenal insufficiency from HPA suppression. (S30; S35)
- **Patient C — type 2 diabetes, older adult with fall history, needs add-on therapy:** DPP-4 or SGLT2 inhibitor; avoid sulfonylurea hypoglycemia. (S30; S21)
- **Patient D — post-thyroidectomy, perioral numbness and low calcium:** calcitriol plus calcium; monitor for hypercalcemia. (S30; S27)

### Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit — Drugs named as examples
- **A cumulative self-test spanning both sessions — use this to review before exams.** The recall quiz is a standalone resource, separate from in-lecture quick checks. (S32; S32 (notes); S35 (notes))
- **Recall Q1:** PTU blocks both new thyroid hormone synthesis and peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion, giving it a faster effect on circulating active hormone than methimazole — important in thyroid storm. (S33; S35)
- **Recall Q2:** a patient on chronic glucocorticoid therapy abruptly stops the medication and develops hypotension and weakness: HPA-axis suppression leaves endogenous cortisol production unable to resume quickly. (S33; S35)
- Chronic exogenous glucocorticoid exposure suppresses CRH/ACTH via negative feedback (Figure 9), reducing the adrenal cortex’s stimulus; abrupt withdrawal does not give the axis time to recover, precipitating adrenal insufficiency. (S35)
- **Recall Q3:** smoking at 35 or older contraindicates estrogen-containing contraception (thromboembolic and cardiovascular risk). **Q4:** sulfonylureas force K-ATP closure regardless of glucose; incretins act only when glucose is high. (S33; S34; S35)
- **Recall Q4 distractor:** DPP-4 inhibitors do affect insulin secretion — they prolong GLP-1’s glucose-dependent effect. (S34; S35)
- **Recall Q5:** a patient with hypoparathyroidism after thyroid surgery needs treatment — the mainstay is calcitriol plus oral calcium: because PTH is required to activate vitamin D, calcitriol (already-active vitamin D) bypasses that activation step. (S34; S35; SG U4-R13)
- **Recall Q6:** SGLT2 inhibitors block renal glucose reabsorption, increasing urinary glucose excretion and creating a favorable environment for genital fungal overgrowth — a mechanism-based adverse effect. (S34; S35; SG U4-R12)

### Putting It All Together: Endocrine Toolkit — Exam traps
- Choosing the most potent glucose-lowering drug instead of the safest for an older adult who falls. (S21 (notes))
- Integrated case — diabetes: a DPP-4 or SGLT2 inhibitor is preferable to a sulfonylurea given the fall history — sulfonylureas force glucose-independent insulin release, raising hypoglycemia-driven fall risk. (SG U4-S)
- Integrated case — thyroid storm: beta-blocker + PTU first, then iodine solution only after PTU (never before — iodine first would supply substrate for new hormone synthesis before synthesis is blocked, worsening the crisis). (SG U4-S)
- Corticosteroids also help in thyroid storm by blunting peripheral conversion and covering relative adrenal insufficiency. (SG U4-S)
- Shared concept: effective endocrine drug selection depends on knowing exactly where in a pathway (or which receptor/site) a drug acts — that position determines both its efficacy and its specific risks. (SG U4-S)
