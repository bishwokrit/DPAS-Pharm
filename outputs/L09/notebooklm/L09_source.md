## Drug class: Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO2, IO5.1, IO5.5

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Class
- **Where diuretics act, and why renal function changes how every drug is dosed.** Renal physiology and pharmacokinetic principles set the dosing logic for everything that follows. (S4; S4 (notes))
- **The kidney is a major route of elimination for many drugs and active metabolites — renal function directly determines how long a drug stays in the body.** (S5)
- Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and creatinine clearance (CrCl) are the standard measures used to estimate renal drug-elimination capacity. (S5; S5 (notes))
- This is foundational Chapter 3 content applied specifically to the renal unit: renal dosing is not a separate topic but the lens through which every drug in this unit (and this course) should be viewed. (S5 (notes))

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Prototype + other drugs named
- Digoxin, most antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin, many beta-lactams), many direct oral anticoagulants, metformin, and the diuretics covered later in this session are examples of drugs requiring renal dose adjustment. (S5; S5 (notes))

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Mechanism of action
- Flow (Figure 1): GFR falls → renally cleared drug or active metabolite accumulates → toxicity. (S6; S6 (notes))
- **Figure 2. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): filtrate flows through the proximal tubule, thick ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct — each diuretic class blocks sodium reabsorption at a different, specific segment.** (S7)
- Share of filtered sodium reabsorbed: proximal tubule about 65%, thick ascending limb about 25%, distal tubule 5-8%, collecting duct 2-3% — this previews the potency ranking in Figure 7. (S7 (notes))
- This nephron map is the conceptual spine of the session — every diuretic class is anchored back to its segment. (S7 (notes))
- Match each diuretic class to the nephron segment where it acts: loop diuretics (furosemide) — thick ascending limb; thiazide diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide) — distal convoluted tubule. (SG U5-R2; SG U5-M)
- Potassium-sparing — aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone) and ENaC blockers (amiloride) — collecting duct; carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) — proximal tubule; osmotic diuretics (mannitol) — throughout. (SG U5-R2; SG U5-M)
- CAI — proximal tubule; osmotic — acts osmotically throughout the filtrate-containing segments, mainly the proximal tubule and descending limb. (SG U5-R2; SG U5-M)
- [TB] Renal tubule transport mechanisms: the proximal tubule reabsorbs about 66% of Na⁺ and 85% of NaHCO₃; organic acid secretion there delivers most diuretics to their luminal site. (p. 262; p. 263)
- [TB] Collecting tubule system: only 2–5% of NaCl, but where aldosterone acts and K⁺ is secreted; upstream diuretics deliver more Na⁺ here — the basis of diuretic K⁺ wasting. (p. 264; p. 265)
- [TB] Renal autacoids: adenosine (A1) lowers GFR; prostaglandins (PGE2) aid loop action; natriuretic peptides (nesiritide) and urodilatin cause natriuresis but have a limited clinical role. (p. 266)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Indications
- Three levers: reduce the dose, extend the dosing interval, or both — the specific approach depends on the drug. Reduced GFR makes a renally cleared drug accumulate if unadjusted. (S6; S8; SG U5-R1)
- Reduce the maintenance dose when the drug’s effect is concentration-dependent; extend the dosing interval when the drug has a long half-life; monitor for toxicity after each dose change. (S6)
- Loading doses are often unchanged; maintenance doses are what get adjusted. (S6 (notes); S8)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- As GFR falls, renally cleared drugs accumulate — dosing must be adjusted by reducing the dose, extending the interval, or both. (S5)
- Active or toxic metabolites that are renally cleared matter just as much as the parent drug. (S8)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Contraindications
- Not covered in materials.

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Drug interactions
- **Renal impairment also raises drug-interaction risk — drugs that are individually safe can become dangerous in combination when renal clearance is already reduced (a theme that returns explicitly in Session 2's "triple whammy" discussion).** (S5)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Pharmacokinetics in two numbers: volume of distribution links amount in the body to concentration; clearance links elimination rate to concentration. (p. 43)
- [TB] Maintenance dose = clearance × target concentration (steady state depends only on clearance); loading dose = volume of distribution × target concentration, given slowly IV. (p. 51; p. 52)
- [TB] Drug accumulation: doses repeated more often than every four half-lives accumulate; steady state takes about four half-lives. (p. 47; p. 48)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Monitoring
- Check creatinine clearance before dosing any renally cleared drug (lower maintenance dose, extend the interval, or check CrCl) — especially in older adults. (S6; S8)
- Reassess as renal function changes — acute illness, dehydration, or new nephrotoxic drugs can drop GFR quickly. (S8; S8 (notes))
- When in doubt, consult dosing references or clinical pharmacy — this is not a "memorize every number" exercise; the point is the habit of checking renal function before dosing. (S8; S8 (notes))
- [TB] Distribution & clearance: adjust a renally cleared drug in proportion to creatinine clearance (Cockcroft-Gault; women 85% of the calculated value). (p. 55)
- [TB] The target concentration strategy: choose a target, predict V and CL from weight and renal function, dose, measure the response, revise, repeat. (p. 53)
- [TB] Dosing history: without it a drug level loses its predictive value; draw digoxin at least 6 hours after a dose. (p. 54; p. 55)

### Renal Dosing & Diuretic Sites — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Renal function determines drug dosing broadly, not just for diuretics — GFR/creatinine clearance should be checked before dosing any renally cleared drug (Chapter 3). (S25)
- [TB] Textbook case: 85-year-old, 60 kg, CrCl 22 mL/min — digoxin clearance falls to 3.7 L/h, so about 127 mcg/day: two 62.5-mcg tablets. (p. 42; p. 56)

## Drug class: Loop & Thiazide Diuretics
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO3, SO4, SO5, IO5.1, IO5.2, IO5.3, IO5.4

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Class
- **The two workhorse classes — one for potent natriuresis, one for everyday hypertension control.** They act on the thick ascending limb and the distal tubule. (S9; S9 (notes))
- Loops are the most potent class; thiazides are first-line for hypertension. (S10; S12)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Prototype + other drugs named
- Furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide: loop diuretics. Hydrochlorothiazide: thiazide; chlorthalidone: thiazide-like. (S10; S12)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Mechanism of action
- **Mechanism: block the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb — the site responsible for ~25% of filtered sodium reabsorption.** (S10)
- **Figure 3. Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): loop diuretics block NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, reducing Na+/Cl-/K+ reabsorption and collapsing the lumen-positive potential that normally drives paracellular calcium and magnesium reabsorption —** (S11)
- **explaining the increased calcium and magnesium excretion seen with this class.** The lumen-positive potential (from K⁺ recycling) is the link students skip when they memorize “loops cause hypocalcemia” without the why. (S11; S11 (notes); SG U5-R3)
- **Mechanism: block the Na-Cl cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule — a less potent natriuretic site (~5–8% of filtered sodium) than the loop.** (S12)
- Follow the thiazide chain step by step (Figure 4): NCC block in the DCT → sodium and water loss → lower intracellular sodium → calcium retained through the NCX exchanger → falling urinary calcium. (S13; S13 (notes); SG U5-R3)
- Contrast this with loop diuretics, which raise urinary calcium (Figure 3). (S13 (notes))
- In one line: loop diuretics collapse the lumen-positive potential driving paracellular Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ reabsorption (↑ excretion); thiazides increase distal calcium reabsorption through a separate mechanism (↓ excretion). (SG U5-R3)
- [TB] Loops work from the lumen, so their effect tracks proximal secretion; NSAIDs and probenecid compete and reduce it. NSAIDs also blunt the PGE2 contribution. (p. 269; p. 270)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Indications
- Loops: edema (heart failure, cirrhosis, renal disease) and, at times, hypertension when a thiazide alone is insufficient (e.g., significant renal impairment). (S10)
- Loop diuretics increase calcium excretion — useful in hypercalcemia, and the opposite effect of thiazides. (S10; S10 (notes))
- Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone — first-line agents for essential hypertension; chlorthalidone has a longer half-life and is often preferred for 24-hour BP control. (S12)
- Thiazides decrease calcium excretion — can be therapeutically useful in patients with osteoporosis and recurrent calcium-containing kidney stones. (S12; S13)
- [TB] Hypercalcemia: normal saline plus IV furosemide (80–120 mg) — a loop alone causes volume contraction and stops working. (p. 280)
- [TB] Nephrolithiasis: thiazide plus more fluid and less salt; do not restrict dietary calcium. (p. 280)
- [TB] Diabetes insipidus (nephrogenic, including lithium-induced): thiazides reduce polyuria; amiloride blocks lithium entry; monitor lithium levels. (p. 280)
- [TB] Nonedematous states, hypertension: chlorthalidone lowers BP more than HCTZ but has more adverse effects; loops reserved for GFR < 30–40 mL/min or heart failure. (p. 280)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Adverse effects: hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, ototoxicity (especially with rapid IV administration or high doses), and hyperuricemia.** (S10)
- **Adverse effects: hyponatremia (can be significant, especially in older adults), hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia (can precipitate gout), and hypercalcemia.** (S12)
- Loops carry greater risk of ototoxicity and more pronounced electrolyte shifts than thiazides. (S14)
- Thiazides: hyponatremia, gout, and hypokalemia are common adverse effects. (S13)
- [TB] Thiazide hyperglycemia occurs at HCTZ > 50 mg/d, not at 12.5 mg/d or less; correcting hypokalemia partly reverses it. (p. 272)
- [TB] Thiazides are the diuretics most associated with gout and cause hypomagnesemia more than loops; loop ototoxicity is usually reversible. (p. 271; p. 272; p. 273)
- [TB] Risk of hyponatremia with different diuretic classes: thiazides highest (elderly women); loops protective (odds ratio 0.61). (p. 272; p. 281)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Contraindications
- Furosemide carries a sulfonamide-related structure — historical cross-reactivity concern with sulfa allergy, though the clinical risk is now considered low; still worth a careful allergy history. (S10; S10 (notes))
- Thiazides lose efficacy as GFR falls below approximately 30 mL/min — loop diuretics are generally preferred once renal function is significantly reduced. (S12; S13)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Drug interactions
- Digoxin: hypokalemia from loops increases toxicity. Aminoglycosides: additive ototoxicity with loops. (S38; S38 (notes))
- Lithium: levels rise with thiazides. NSAIDs blunt the diuretic effect and raise AKI risk. (S38; S38 (notes))
- [TB] Diuretic combinations: loop agents and thiazides (often metolazone) are synergistic in loop resistance — but cause large fluid shifts and K⁺ wasting. (p. 277)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Pharmacokinetics
- Chlorthalidone: long half-life of 40-60 hours. (S31)
- Torsemide: longer half-life and more predictable absorption than furosemide. Bumetanide is about 40 times more potent than furosemide mg for mg. (S28; S29)
- [TB] Furosemide lasts 2–3 hours; torsemide 4–6 hours and absorbs in 1 hour, nearly as completely as IV. Torsemide is mainly cleared by the liver. (p. 269)
- [TB] Chemistry & pharmacokinetics: all thiazides are sulfonamides given orally; chlorothiazide is the only parenteral thiazide. (p. 271)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Monitoring
- Loops: monitor potassium, magnesium, creatinine. Thiazides: monitor sodium, potassium, calcium, glucose. (S27; S30)
- IV furosemide slowly (no faster than 4 mg/min) — ototoxicity with rapid IV or high doses. (S27)

### Loop & Thiazide Diuretics — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- The calcium-handling difference between loops and thiazides is one of the highest-yield comparison points in this whole unit: loops increase Ca2+ excretion, thiazides decrease it — state it both ways. (S10 (notes); S25; SG U5-R3)
- Case: hypertension and worsening CKD (eGFR now 25 mL/min), well controlled on chlorthalidone for years, but blood pressure has crept up. **What is the most likely explanation, and what change in therapy would you consider?** (S15)
- **Thiazide diuretics lose much of their natriuretic efficacy once GFR falls below roughly 30 mL/min, because there is too little filtered sodium reaching the distal tubule for the drug to act on meaningfully.** (S15)
- **A loop diuretic, which acts upstream at the thick ascending limb, remains effective even in advanced CKD and is the more appropriate choice here.** (S15; S15 (notes))
- The “thiazides lose efficacy in advanced CKD” point bridges directly to Session 2’s hypertension-in-CKD content. (S12 (notes))
- [TB] Edematous states, heart failure: loops are standard, but overdiuresis cuts preload and cardiac output; hypokalemia worsens arrhythmias and digitalis toxicity. (p. 278)
- [TB] Kidney disease and renal failure: little benefit below GFR 5 mL/min; high-dose loops or loop plus metolazone treat overload; avoid acetazolamide. (p. 278; p. 279)
- [TB] Textbook case: HCTZ added to chronic furosemide in CKD → 15 kg lost, BP 91/58, creatinine 10.8 — hypovolemic AKI; monitor closely, ideally inpatient. (p. 261; p. 285)

### Figure: Figure 3 · NKCC2: the loop diuretic target
- Original illustrative diagram (not from Katzung): blocking NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb cuts Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ reabsorption and collapses the lumen-positive potential (from K⁺ recycling) that drives paracellular Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ reabsorption — so loops raise urinary calcium and magnesium. (S11; S11 (notes))

### Figure: Figure 4 · Thiazides: NCC block and calcium reabsorption
- **Schematic (illustrative)** — Figure 4. Distal convoluted tubule: thiazide block of NCC lowers intracellular sodium, which favors Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange (NCX), so more calcium is reabsorbed and urinary calcium falls (illustrative schematic). (S13; S13 (notes))

- Lecture slides: Thiazides lose efficacy below GFR ~30 mL/min; switch to a loop. (S12; S15)
- Textbook: The exact GFR cutoff is debated; chlorthalidone still lowered BP in stage 4 CKD. (p. 279; p. 280)

### Conflict between sources: Hypomagnesemia
- Lecture slides: Listed as a loop adverse effect. (S10)
- Textbook: More likely with thiazides than with loops. (p. 273)

## Drug class: Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics
Learning objectives covered: SO1, SO3, SO4, SO5, IO5.1, IO5.2, IO5.3, IO5.4, IO5.6

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Class
- **Smaller in potency, but each with a distinct and important clinical niche.** Potassium-sparing, carbonic anhydrase inhibitor and osmotic diuretics act on the collecting duct and the proximal tubule. (S16; S16 (notes))
- **Two mechanistically distinct subgroups acting at the collecting duct, both reducing potassium and hydrogen ion secretion.** (S17)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Prototype + other drugs named
- Spironolactone, eplerenone: aldosterone receptor antagonists. Amiloride, triamterene: epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) blockers. (S17)
- Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor: acetazolamide. Osmotic diuretic: mannitol. (S19)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Mechanism of action
- Aldosterone binds the mineralocorticoid receptor → increases ENaC and Na/K pump activity → K+ and H+ secreted into the tubule. Blocking either step reduces potassium and hydrogen secretion. (S18; S18 (notes))
- Spironolactone and eplerenone block the receptor (block aldosterone’s effect directly); amiloride and triamterene block ENaC directly, independent of aldosterone. (S17; S18; S18 (notes))
- **Acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor):** blocks Na+/HCO3- reabsorption in the proximal tubule → less HCO3- reabsorbed → bicarbonate diuresis, metabolic acidosis; weak diuretic effect. (S19; S20; SG U5-R6)
- **Mannitol (osmotic diuretic):** an inert, filtered but poorly reabsorbed solute that holds water in the tubule lumen osmotically — osmotic diuresis. (S19; S20)
- Side by side: acetazolamide removes bicarbonate reabsorption; mannitol removes water reabsorption by staying in the lumen. Both act before the loop, so their natriuretic potency is modest. (S20 (notes))
- Figure 6 (**Schematic (illustrative)**): proximal tubule — carbonic anhydrase inhibition and osmotic holding of water (illustrative schematic). (S20)
- **Figure 7. Original illustrative comparison (illustrative values, not from Katzung): loop diuretics block roughly 25% of filtered sodium reabsorption, substantially more than thiazides, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, or potassium-sparing diuretics —** (S21)
- **directly reflecting the percentage normally reabsorbed at each nephron segment in Figure 2.** This is why loops are reached for first when potent natriuresis is the goal. (S21; S21 (notes))

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Indications
- Potassium-sparing agents are often used in combination with a loop or thiazide diuretic specifically to offset potassium loss from the more potent class. (S17)
- Acetazolamide: valued for non-diuretic uses — glaucoma (reduces aqueous humor production), altitude sickness prophylaxis, and metabolic alkalosis correction; also raised intracranial pressure. (S19; S20)
- Mannitol: used to reduce elevated intracranial or intraocular pressure and in certain cases of rhabdomyolysis-associated kidney injury prevention. (S19)
- Know the niche, not an everyday use: acetazolamide = glaucoma/altitude sickness; mannitol = ICP/IOP reduction. (S19 (notes))
- [TB] Potassium-sparing diuretics fit mineralocorticoid excess: primary (Conn syndrome) or secondary hyperaldosteronism (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome). (p. 274)
- [TB] Hepatic cirrhosis: resistant to loops but unusually responsive to spironolactone; aggressive diuresis risks hepatorenal syndrome and encephalopathy. (p. 279)
- [TB] Renal & cardiac protection: aldosterone antagonists are cardioprotective and lower albuminuria; finerenone slowed CKD in type 2 diabetes. (p. 281)
- [TB] Idiopathic edema (women 20–30): rule out surreptitious diuretic use; prefer moderate salt restriction. (p. 279)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Adverse effects (boxed warnings first)
- **Primary adverse effect across the class: hyperkalemia — the risk rises sharply when combined with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or in renal impairment (a direct preview of Session 2's cardiorenal drug-interaction content).** (S17; SG U5-R5)
- Spironolactone also has antiandrogen effects (gynecomastia, menstrual irregularities) that eplerenone largely avoids. (S17)
- Acetazolamide causes a mild metabolic acidosis as a predictable adverse effect, plus potassium loss; watch for kidney stones. (S19; S20)
- Mannitol expands extracellular volume — can worsen volume overload if used inappropriately; avoid it in heart failure and volume depletion. (S19; S20)
- [TB] K⁺-sparing agents can cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis (like type IV RTA); triamterene can cause kidney stones and, with indomethacin, acute renal failure. (p. 275)
- [TB] Acetazolamide: drowsiness, paresthesias, calcium phosphate stones, hyperammonemia in cirrhosis; monitor potassium and phosphorus. (p. 268)
- [TB] Mannitol: early ECF expansion and hyponatremia, later dehydration, hypernatremia and hyperkalemia; AKI in 6–7% of patients. (p. 276)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Contraindications
- Mannitol requires intact renal perfusion to be effective. (S19; S20 (notes))
- Eplerenone: avoid with potassium above 5.5 mEq/L, CrCl below 30 mL/min, or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as ketoconazole). (S33)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Drug interactions
- Hyperkalemia risk rises with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or CKD — both block aldosterone’s effect, so the classes are combined carefully. (S18; S18 (notes))
- Flag the hyperkalemia-with-ACEi/ARB interaction as a preview: this exact combination becomes a major theme in Session 2 when RAAS blockers are introduced. (S17 (notes))
- Potassium supplements and trimethoprim: hyperkalemia with potassium-sparing diuretics. (S38; S38 (notes))
- Sulfonamide allergy: caution with loops, thiazides, acetazolamide. Metformin, vancomycin, DOACs: renally cleared; need dose adjustment. (S38; S38 (notes))
- [TB] Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (erythromycin, fluconazole, diltiazem, grapefruit juice) raise eplerenone but not spironolactone levels. (p. 275)
- [TB] RALES showed spironolactone helps severe heart failure on an ACE inhibitor — afterward hyperkalemia admissions and deaths rose. (p. 275)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Pharmacokinetics
- [TB] Acetazolamide diuresis fades within 2–3 days (reduce dose in renal insufficiency); mannitol must be given IV — orally it causes osmotic diarrhea. (p. 268; p. 275)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Monitoring
- Spironolactone: monitor potassium and creatinine within 1 week; life-threatening hyperkalemia, especially in CKD or with ACE inhibitor/ARB. (S32)
- Mannitol: monitor osmolality and electrolytes. (S37)

### Potassium-Sparing, CAI & Osmotic Diuretics — Clinical pearls / "why" hook
- Case: a patient with stage 3 chronic kidney disease and heart failure is taking an ACE inhibitor and is started on spironolactone for additional heart-failure benefit; two weeks later a routine basic metabolic panel shows potassium 6.1 mEq/L. (S23)
- **What is the mechanistic explanation for this finding, and what does it suggest about future drug combinations in this unit?** (S23; S23 (notes))
- **Both the ACE inhibitor (by reducing aldosterone) and spironolactone (by directly blocking the aldosterone receptor) independently reduce potassium excretion — combined, and in the setting of CKD (which already impairs potassium excretion), the hyperkalemia risk compounds.** (S23)
- **This is the same additive-risk logic that will reappear as the "triple whammy" in Session 2.** The ACEi/aldosterone-antagonist pair is one of three “whammies” formalized next session. (S23; S23 (notes))
- Recap: diuretic classes are organized by nephron site (Figure 2): loop diuretics (thick ascending limb) are most potent, followed by thiazides (distal tubule), then potassium-sparing and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. (S25)
- Recap: loop and thiazide diuretics have opposite effects on calcium excretion (Figure 3); potassium-sparing diuretics carry a hyperkalemia risk that compounds with ACE inhibitors or ARBs. (S25)
- Session 2 (Thursday, November 19) continues with antihypertensive therapy in chronic kidney disease and the cardiovascular-renal overlap, including vasodilators and angina pharmacology — RAAS blockers and the triple whammy first. (S25; S25 (notes))
- [TB] SGLT2 inhibitors block proximal glucose and Na⁺ reabsorption; main indication now cardiorenal protection. Adverse reactions: sixfold genital fungal infections in women, initial GFR dip. (p. 268; p. 269)
- [TB] Antidiuretic hormone antagonists (vaptans): oral tolvaptan (V2), IV conivaptan (V1a and V2) for SIADH when water restriction fails; watch sodium and liver tests. (p. 276; p. 277)
